# Epidemiology: studying disease in populations

Epidemiology is the science of how diseases spread and how we know what causes or prevents them. This branch covers measurement, transmission and the reproduction number, herd immunity, outbreaks and elimination, causation, the main study designs (randomised trials, cohort, case-control and self-controlled designs), bias and confounding, base rates, surveillance, efficacy and waning, and systematic reviews. Almost every argument in the [vaccines course](../immunology/vaccines/README.md), for or against, depends on these tools.

**Prerequisites:** [Microbiology: infection and transmission](../microbiology/06-infection-and-transmission.md). Basic arithmetic and percentages.

## Suggested reading order

Lessons 01–05 cover how diseases spread; 06–11 cover how to judge evidence; 12–14 cover surveillance, effectiveness and reviews. Read in order. Lessons 10 (bias) and 11 (base rates) are the most useful for evaluating claims you meet in the news.

## Key studies & sources

Every source below was checked to exist (DOI, court record or official page). Notes summarise what each found and its main strengths or limitations.

### Peer-reviewed studies

- [Hill, *Proceedings of the Royal Society of Medicine* 1965](https://doi.org/10.1177/003591576505800503): Bradford Hill's lecture setting out nine considerations for judging causation. Still the standard framework; Hill himself warned they are not a checklist.
- [Farrington, *Biometrics* 1995](https://doi.org/10.2307/2533328): Introduced the self-controlled case series for vaccine safety; applied first to MMR and meningitis. Removes fixed confounders; assumes events do not affect later vaccination.
- [Whitaker et al., *Statistics in Medicine* 2006](https://doi.org/10.1002/sim.2302): Tutorial on the SCCS method, with its assumptions and extensions.
- [Jackson et al., *International Journal of Epidemiology* 2006](https://doi.org/10.1093/ije/dyi274): Showed that observational studies of flu vaccine in seniors were inflated by the healthy-vaccinee effect, because "benefit" appeared before flu season. A key demonstration that confounding can flatter vaccines.
- [Guerra et al., *Lancet Infectious Diseases* 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1473-3099(17)30307-9): Systematic review of measles R₀ estimates; found a much wider range than the textbook 12–18. Highlights how R₀ depends on setting and method.
- [Warfel, Zimmerman & Merkel, *PNAS* 2014](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1314688110): Baboon experiments: acellular pertussis vaccine prevented disease but not colonisation or transmission. Animal model; important for understanding pertussis resurgence.
- [Klein et al., *NEJM* 2012](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1200850): Kaiser Permanente case-control study showing waning protection after the fifth DTaP dose. Large, well-defined population; observational.
- [Polack et al., *NEJM* 2020 (BNT162b2 trial)](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2034577): The Pfizer–BioNTech phase 3 trial: 95% efficacy against symptomatic COVID-19 over about two months. Large and randomised; manufacturer funded; not designed to assess transmission or mortality.

### Systematic reviews and methodology

- [Lundh et al., Cochrane methodology review, 2017](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.MR000033.pub3): Cochrane methodology review: industry-sponsored drug and device studies more often report favourable results and conclusions than non-industry studies.
- [Jefferson et al., *BMJ* 2009](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.b354): Review of influenza vaccine studies: industry-funded studies were published in higher-impact journals and cited more, independent of methodological quality.

### Official reports and guidance

- [Lazarus et al., *Electronic Support for Public Health–VAERS* final report to AHRQ (2010)](https://digital.ahrq.gov/sites/default/files/docs/publication/r18hs017045-lazarus-final-report-2011.pdf): Report to AHRQ on automated VAERS reporting at Harvard Pilgrim; widely quoted statement that fewer than 1% of vaccine adverse events are reported. A pilot project, not a measured national rate.
- [Shaw et al., *MMWR Surveillance Summaries* 2025](https://doi.org/10.15585/mmwr.ss7402a1): CDC's Autism and Developmental Disabilities Monitoring Network estimate: about 1 in 31 eight-year-olds identified with autism in 2022 across 16 sites. Surveillance, not a census; sites vary widely.
- [CDC, *Principles of Epidemiology in Public Health Practice* (self-study course)](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/csels/dsepd/ss1978/index.html): Free introductory course covering rates, surveillance and outbreak investigation.

## References & further reading

- **Beginner.** Adam Kucharski, *The Rules of Contagion* (2020). How outbreaks spread, from diseases to rumours, by a mathematical epidemiologist.
- **Beginner.** Ben Goldacre, *Bad Science* (2008). How evidence is misused, with chapters on the MMR scare; written from a strongly pro-evidence-based-medicine stance.
- **Beginner.** Ben Goldacre, *Bad Pharma* (2012). Industry influence on trials and publication; useful counterweight on conflicts of interest.
- **Intermediate.** Kenneth J. Rothman, *Epidemiology: An Introduction* (2nd ed., 2012). Clear introduction to study design and causation.
- **Intermediate.** Leon Gordis, *Epidemiology* (later editions by David Celentano and Moyses Szklo). Widely used textbook.
- **Intermediate.** Johan Giesecke, *Modern Infectious Disease Epidemiology*. Accessible introduction focused on infections.
- **Advanced.** Roy M. Anderson and Robert M. May, *Infectious Diseases of Humans: Dynamics and Control* (1991). The classic text on transmission models and herd immunity.
- **Advanced.** Kenneth J. Rothman, Sander Greenland and Timothy L. Lash, *Modern Epidemiology*. Standard advanced reference.
- **Advanced.** Miguel Hernán and James Robins, *Causal Inference: What If* (2020). Rigorous modern treatment of causation in observational data; freely available from the authors.
