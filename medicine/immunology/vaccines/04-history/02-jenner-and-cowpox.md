# Jenner and cowpox

**Prerequisites:** [[variolation|Variolation]]

@@IMG:W:Edward Jenner|Edward Jenner@@

## The observation

Country people in parts of England believed that dairymaids who had caught **cowpox**, a mild disease causing sores on cows' udders and on milkers' hands, were protected against smallpox. At least one farmer, Benjamin Jesty of Dorset, deliberately inoculated his wife and sons with cowpox during a 1774 epidemic. But it was Edward Jenner (1749–1823), a country doctor in Berkeley, Gloucestershire, who tested the idea and published it.

## The experiment

On 14 May 1796 Jenner took material from cowpox sores on the hand of a dairymaid, Sarah Nelmes, and inserted it into scratches on the arm of James Phipps, the eight-year-old son of his gardener. Phipps developed a mild illness. Six weeks later Jenner inoculated him with smallpox matter (variolation), and the boy did not develop smallpox. In modern terms, this was a challenge experiment on a child, with no consent process recognisable today.

The Royal Society declined to publish his first report. Jenner gathered more cases and in 1798 published, at his own expense, *An Inquiry into the Causes and Effects of the Variolae Vaccinae*. The word **vaccine** comes from the Latin *vacca*, cow; Louis Pasteur later extended the term to all such preparations in Jenner's honour ([[c:riedel2005]]).

## Rapid spread

Vaccination spread across Europe and beyond within a few years, despite opposition and ridicule (cartoons showed vaccinated people sprouting cows' heads). Without refrigeration, vaccine was kept alive by passing it **arm to arm**: material from a vaccinated person's sore was used to vaccinate the next. In 1803–1806 Spain's Balmis Expedition carried cowpox to its American and Asian colonies by vaccinating orphan boys in sequence during the voyage. Thomas Jefferson championed vaccination in the United States.

Arm-to-arm vaccination had real dangers: it could transmit other infections, including syphilis, and documented clusters of vaccinal syphilis in the 19th century fed opposition. Production later moved to calves (animal lymph), and glycerine was added as a preservative.

## What was in the vaccine?

The virus used for smallpox vaccination in the 20th century, **vaccinia**, is not the same as modern cowpox virus, and its origin is uncertain. Genetic studies of old vaccine samples suggest that some early vaccines were derived from horsepox or a related virus. Jenner himself suspected a link to "grease", a disease of horses.

## Assessment

Jenner did not understand immunology, and his claim that vaccination gave lifelong protection was wrong (revaccination was later found necessary). But his work replaced the dangerous practice of variolation with a far safer one and led eventually to the eradication of smallpox. See [[smallpox-eradication|smallpox eradication]] and [[edward-jenner|Edward Jenner]].

Opposition to vaccination began almost immediately, on religious, medical and political grounds, and grew when governments made it compulsory. See [[compulsory-vaccination-and-the-anti-vaccination-leagues|compulsory vaccination and the anti-vaccination leagues]].

## Key ideas

- Jenner's 1796 experiment showed cowpox inoculation protected against smallpox; he published in 1798.
- "Vaccine" comes from *vacca*, cow.
- Arm-to-arm transfer spread vaccination worldwide but could transmit syphilis.
- The 20th-century vaccine virus, vaccinia, is of uncertain origin.
