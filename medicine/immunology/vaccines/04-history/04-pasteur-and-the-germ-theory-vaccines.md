# Pasteur and the germ theory vaccines

**Prerequisites:** [[jenner-and-cowpox|Jenner and cowpox]]; [[bacteria|Bacteria]]

@@IMG:W:Louis Pasteur|Louis Pasteur@@

## From cowpox to a general principle

Jenner's vaccine relied on a lucky natural relative of smallpox. The next step was to make weakened versions of disease-causing microbes deliberately, which required the **germ theory of disease**, established in the 1860s–1880s through the work of Louis Pasteur in France, Robert Koch in Germany and others. Koch identified the bacteria causing anthrax (1876), tuberculosis (1882) and cholera (1883).

## Chicken cholera and anthrax

In 1879 Pasteur's laboratory found that cultures of the bacterium causing chicken cholera, left to age, no longer killed chickens, and that chickens given the aged culture survived later exposure to fresh, virulent bacteria. Pasteur called the weakened microbes "vaccines" in honour of Jenner.

In May 1881, at Pouilly-le-Fort, Pasteur staged a famous public trial of an anthrax vaccine: vaccinated sheep, cows and goats survived a lethal dose of anthrax while unvaccinated animals died. It made him a celebrity. Historian Gerald Geison's study of Pasteur's private notebooks, published in 1995, showed that the vaccine used was prepared by a chemical method (potassium dichromate) similar to that of his rival Jean-Joseph-Henri Toussaint, rather than the oxygen attenuation method Pasteur implied he had used. Geison's account has itself been debated, but it is a reminder that scientific heroes' public accounts are not always complete.

## Rabies

In July 1885 Pasteur treated nine-year-old Joseph Meister, bitten by a rabid dog, with a series of injections of dried spinal cord from rabid rabbits, progressively less weakened. Meister survived. Pasteur did not know the cause of rabies (a virus, invisible to microscopes of the time), had limited animal data, and was not a physician. His success, followed by many more treated patients, led to the founding of the **Pasteur Institute** in Paris (1887–1888), funded by public subscription. See [[rabies|rabies]].

## Other early vaccines

The decades around 1900 produced vaccines against several bacterial diseases:

- **Cholera** and **plague** vaccines, developed by Waldemar Haffkine in the 1890s and tested on a large scale in India, where Haffkine first tested the cholera vaccine on himself. In 1902, 19 villagers in Mulkowal, Punjab, died of tetanus after receiving contaminated plague vaccine; Haffkine was blamed and suspended, then later exonerated when the contamination was traced to a bottle opened at the vaccination site.
- **Typhoid** vaccine, developed by Almroth Wright in Britain in 1896 and used by the British Army in the Boer War and the First World War.

These killed-bacteria vaccines were crude by modern standards and often caused strong reactions, but they established that vaccines could be made in the laboratory against many diseases.

## Legacy

Pasteur's work turned vaccination from a single empirical trick into a method that could be applied to any pathogen that could be grown and weakened. The Pasteur Institute and its overseas branches went on to develop BCG, yellow fever vaccines and much else. See [[louis-pasteur|Louis Pasteur]] and [[early-vaccines-toxoids-and-bcg|early vaccines: toxoids and BCG]].

## Key ideas

- The germ theory made it possible to create weakened vaccines deliberately.
- Pasteur's laboratory produced chicken cholera, anthrax and rabies vaccines (1879–1885).
- Geison's study of Pasteur's notebooks showed his public accounts were not fully accurate.
- Haffkine's cholera and plague vaccines and Wright's typhoid vaccine followed; the Mulkowal disaster showed the dangers of contamination.
