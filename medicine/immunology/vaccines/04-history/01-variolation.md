# Variolation

**Prerequisites:** [[smallpox|Smallpox]]; [[immunological-memory|Immunological memory]]

@@IMG:W:Lady Mary Wortley Montagu|Lady Mary Wortley Montagu, who promoted variolation in England after seeing it in Constantinople@@

## The idea

Long before anyone knew about viruses or immune systems, people noticed that smallpox survivors did not catch it again. **Variolation** (also called inoculation) exploited this: material from a smallpox pustule or scab was deliberately introduced into a healthy person, usually through a small cut in the skin or, in China, by blowing powdered scabs into the nose. This usually caused a milder illness than natural smallpox, followed by lifelong immunity. Written records from China describe nasal insufflation by the 16th century, and the practice was also known in India, the Ottoman Empire and parts of Africa.

## Arrival in Britain and America

Two routes brought variolation to the English-speaking world in the same year, 1721.

- **Lady Mary Wortley Montagu**, wife of the British ambassador to the Ottoman Empire and herself scarred by smallpox, saw variolation in Constantinople and had her son inoculated there in 1718. During a London epidemic in 1721 she had her daughter inoculated in front of court physicians. That year six condemned prisoners at Newgate were variolated in the "Royal Experiment" in exchange for pardons; all survived, and members of the royal family were then inoculated.
- In **Boston**, the minister Cotton Mather had learned of the practice from Onesimus, an enslaved West African man, and from reports in the Royal Society's journal. During the 1721 epidemic, the physician Zabdiel Boylston inoculated about 240 people. Mather and Boylston reported that about 2% of those inoculated died, compared with about 14% of people who caught smallpox naturally in the epidemic, one of the earliest uses of numerical comparison to evaluate a medical intervention ([[c:riedel2005]]). Boston's other doctors bitterly opposed them, and a bomb was thrown through Mather's window.

## Risks and benefits

Variolation was not safe. Perhaps 1–2% of those inoculated died, and inoculated people were contagious and could start outbreaks if not isolated. Its value depended on the context: in an epidemic, with natural smallpox killing perhaps 15–30% of those infected, the trade was favourable. George Washington ordered the Continental Army inoculated in 1777, after smallpox had devastated American troops.

Variolation also raised arguments that echo in modern debates: whether it was right to deliberately cause illness, whether it interfered with divine providence, whether individuals could be compelled for the public good, and how to weigh a small, certain risk against a larger, uncertain one. These are recurring themes in vaccine ethics; see [[weighing-risks-against-benefits|weighing risks against benefits]].

## Replacement

Once Jenner's cowpox vaccination (1796) offered similar protection without the risk of spreading smallpox, variolation declined. Britain banned it in the Vaccination Act of 1840, which also provided free vaccination.

## Key ideas

- Variolation deliberately infected people with smallpox material to cause a milder disease and lasting immunity.
- It was practised in Asia and Africa long before 1721, when it reached Britain (Montagu) and Boston (Onesimus, Mather and Boylston).
- About 1–2% of those inoculated died, much fewer than with natural smallpox, but inoculated people could spread the disease.
- It raised ethical questions about risk and compulsion that persist today.
