# Early vaccines: toxoids and BCG

**Prerequisites:** [[pasteur-and-the-germ-theory-vaccines|Pasteur and the germ theory vaccines]]; [[toxoid-vaccines|Toxoid vaccines]]

@@IMG:W:Emil von Behring|Emil von Behring, whose diphtheria antitoxin won the first Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine@@

## Antitoxins: passive immunity

In 1890 Emil von Behring and Shibasaburo Kitasato showed that serum from animals immunised against diphtheria or tetanus toxin could neutralise the toxin and protect other animals. Horse **antitoxin** became the first effective treatment for diphtheria, a leading killer of children, and Behring received the first Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1901. Antitoxin gives passive immunity, borrowed antibodies that fade within weeks; see [[passive-and-active-immunity|passive and active immunity]].

## A tragedy creates regulation

In 1901, 13 children in St. Louis died of tetanus after receiving diphtheria antitoxin made from a horse that had tetanus; in the same year, smallpox vaccine contaminated with tetanus was blamed for deaths of children in Camden, New Jersey. Congress responded with the **Biologics Control Act of 1902**, which required licensing and inspection of manufacturers of vaccines and antitoxins, four years before the more famous Pure Food and Drug Act. It was the beginning of US vaccine regulation; see [[licensing-and-regulators|licensing and regulators]].

## Toxoids: active immunity against toxins

Antitoxin treated diphtheria but did not prevent it. In the early 1920s Gaston Ramon at the Pasteur Institute found that treating diphtheria toxin with formaldehyde and heat destroyed its toxicity while preserving its ability to induce antibodies. This **toxoid** (Ramon's *anatoxine*) became the diphtheria vaccine; tetanus toxoid followed. In Britain, Alexander Glenny found that adding aluminium salts enhanced the response to toxoid (1926), the origin of **aluminium adjuvants** still used today; see [[adjuvants|adjuvants]] and [[aluminium-adjuvants|aluminium adjuvants]]. Tetanus toxoid was given to Allied troops in the Second World War; tetanus among wounded soldiers became rare.

## Whole-cell pertussis

In the 1930s, Pearl Kendrick and Grace Eldering, working for the Michigan Department of Health in Grand Rapids, developed a whole-cell pertussis vaccine and tested it in a carefully controlled field trial that showed substantial protection. By the late 1940s pertussis vaccine was combined with diphtheria and tetanus toxoids as **DTP**, used for decades. Its reactogenicity later became the centre of one of the biggest vaccine controversies; see [[dtp-and-whole-cell-pertussis|DTP and whole-cell pertussis]].

## BCG

Albert Calmette and Camille Guérin at the Pasteur Institute in Lille spent 13 years passing a strain of *Mycobacterium bovis* through hundreds of cultures until it lost virulence. BCG was first given to an infant in 1921 and became the world's tuberculosis vaccine. In 1930 contaminated BCG killed more than 70 infants in Lübeck, Germany; see [[tuberculosis-and-bcg|tuberculosis and BCG]].

## Yellow fever and influenza

Max Theiler's live attenuated 17D yellow fever vaccine (1937), made by passaging the virus in mouse and chick tissue, won him the 1951 Nobel Prize. Inactivated influenza vaccine, developed by Thomas Francis Jr. and Jonas Salk among others, was first used in the US military in the 1940s. These were made possible by new methods of growing viruses in fertilised hens' eggs and later in cell culture; John Enders, Thomas Weller and Frederick Robbins's 1949 discovery that poliovirus could be grown in non-nervous tissue cultures opened the way to polio and measles vaccines.

## Key ideas

- Antitoxins (1890s) gave passive immunity; contaminated antitoxin led to the Biologics Control Act of 1902.
- Toxoids (1920s) and aluminium adjuvants (1926) produced lasting active immunity to diphtheria and tetanus.
- Whole-cell pertussis vaccine (1930s) and BCG (1921) followed, then yellow fever (1937) and influenza (1940s) vaccines.
- Cell culture (1949) made possible the virus vaccines of the 1950s and 1960s.
