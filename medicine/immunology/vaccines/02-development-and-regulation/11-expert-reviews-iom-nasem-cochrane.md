## Why expert reviews matter

Individual studies conflict. Expert reviews try to weigh all the evidence on a question using explicit rules. In vaccine safety, two kinds dominate: reviews by the **US National Academies** (the Institute of Medicine until 2015, now the National Academy of Medicine within NASEM), and **Cochrane systematic reviews**. Both are cited by people on all sides, and both have critics.

## The National Academies' vaccine-safety reviews

The National Childhood Vaccine Injury Act of 1986 required reviews of vaccine adverse events, partly to inform the [[the-vaccine-injury-table|Vaccine Injury Table]]. Key reports:

| Year | Report | Main findings |
|---|---|---|
| 1991 | Adverse effects of pertussis and rubella vaccines ([Institute of Medicine, *Adverse Effects of Pertussis and Rubella Vaccines* (1991)](https://doi.org/10.17226/1815)) | Evidence supported causal links between whole-cell pertussis vaccine and anaphylaxis and prolonged inconsolable crying, and between rubella vaccine and acute arthritis; it did not support a link to infantile spasms or SIDS |
| 1994 | Adverse events associated with childhood vaccines ([Institute of Medicine, *Adverse Events Associated with Childhood Vaccines* (1994)](https://doi.org/10.17226/2138)) | Examined diphtheria, tetanus, measles, mumps, polio, hep B and Hib vaccines; accepted some links (for example, vaccine-strain polio from oral polio vaccine, anaphylaxis) |
| 2001–2004 | Immunization Safety Review series, including thimerosal, MMR, SIDS and the final autism report ([Institute of Medicine, *Immunization Safety Review: Vaccines and Autism* (2004)](https://doi.org/10.17226/10997)) | Evidence favoured rejecting a causal link between MMR or thimerosal-containing vaccines and autism |
| 2011 | Adverse effects of vaccines: evidence and causality ([Institute of Medicine, *Adverse Effects of Vaccines: Evidence and Causality* (2011)](https://doi.org/10.17226/13164)) | Of 158 vaccine–event pairs, the evidence convincingly supported causation for 14 and favoured acceptance for 4, favoured rejection for 5 (including MMR and autism), and was inadequate to decide for the remaining 135 |
| 2013 | The childhood immunization schedule and safety ([Institute of Medicine, *The Childhood Immunization Schedule and Safety* (2013)](https://doi.org/10.17226/13563)) | No evidence the schedule as a whole was unsafe, but few studies of the entire schedule; a new trial of the schedule against no vaccination would be unethical |
| 2024 | Adverse effects of COVID-19 vaccination ([NASEM, *Evidence Review of the Adverse Effects of COVID-19 Vaccination* (2024)](https://doi.org/10.17226/27746)) | mRNA vaccines cause myocarditis; Janssen favoured acceptance for TTS and Guillain-Barré; evidence favoured rejection for mRNA vaccines and infertility, Bell's palsy, GBS, TTS and heart attack; four kinds of shoulder injury from poorly placed injections; most of 85 conclusions were "inadequate to accept or reject" |

Two features stand out. First, these committees do say vaccines cause harms when evidence supports it. Second, the most common verdict is "inadequate evidence", which critics cite as proof of how little is known and defenders interpret as reflecting rare outcomes and limited research rather than suspected harm. Both readings have some basis; "inadequate" means exactly that, neither safe nor harmful.

Criticisms: the reviews are sponsored by HHS agencies (HRSA, CDC), which critics see as a conflict; committee members are screened for financial conflicts but some critics argue the screening excludes vaccine-safety critics while including people sympathetic to vaccination programmes. The 2004 autism report was criticised by advocacy groups for relying heavily on epidemiology and downplaying biological hypotheses.

## Cochrane reviews

Cochrane, an international network, produces systematic reviews of interventions using pre-registered protocols and its GRADE certainty ratings. Vaccine reviews include:

- **MMR** (2020): found no evidence of an association with autism and moderate evidence of an increased risk of febrile seizures shortly after vaccination; it also found many included studies at high risk of bias ([Di Pietrantonj et al., Cochrane review of MMR/MMRV vaccines, 2020](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD004407.pub4)).
- **Influenza vaccines in healthy adults** (2018): modest reductions in influenza (about 2.3% to 0.9% risk in one comparison) and little evidence on hospitalisations; Tom Jefferson's influenza reviews have long been cited by sceptics of flu vaccination policy ([Demicheli et al., Cochrane review of influenza vaccines in healthy adults, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD001269.pub6)).
- **HPV** (2018): high-certainty evidence of protection against precancer in young women, without increased serious adverse events ([Arbyn et al., Cochrane review of HPV vaccines, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD009069.pub3)). It was sharply criticised by Lars Jørgensen, Peter Gøtzsche and Tom Jefferson for missing trials, using adjuvant comparators and underplaying possible harms ([Jørgensen, Gøtzsche & Jefferson, *BMJ Evidence-Based Medicine* 2018](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjebm-2018-111012)). Gøtzsche was expelled from Cochrane's governing board later in 2018, in a dispute described by his supporters as being about his criticism and by Cochrane as being about conduct.
- **Rotavirus** (2021) and **acellular pertussis** (2014) reviews found substantial protection ([Bergman et al., Cochrane review of rotavirus vaccines, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD008521.pub6); [Zhang et al., Cochrane review of acellular pertussis vaccines, 2014](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD001478.pub6)).

Cochrane reviewers have themselves studied funding bias: Jefferson's 2009 review of influenza-vaccine studies found that industry-funded studies were published in more prestigious journals and cited more often regardless of quality, and that publicly funded studies were less likely to report conclusions favourable to vaccines ([Jefferson et al., *BMJ* 2009](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.b354)). See [[conflicts-of-interest-and-funding|conflicts of interest and funding]].

## Key ideas

- The National Academies have reviewed vaccine safety since 1991, accepting some harms (anaphylaxis, myocarditis after mRNA vaccines, TTS after Janssen) and rejecting others (MMR and autism).
- The most common verdict is "inadequate evidence", which is interpreted differently by critics and defenders.
- Cochrane reviews are independent of governments but have had their own disputes, especially over HPV vaccines.
- Sponsorship and committee selection are recurring criticisms.
