## Ethics grew out of scandal

Modern research ethics were built largely in reaction to abuses. The **Nuremberg Code** (1947), after Nazi medical experiments, established that voluntary consent is essential. The World Medical Association's **Declaration of Helsinki** (1964, revised many times) set out principles including independent ethical review and limits on placebo use. In the US, the **Belmont Report** (1979) named three principles: respect for persons, beneficence and justice.

Vaccine research features in this history, and these episodes still shape trust:

- **Willowbrook (1956–1971).** At a New York state institution for children with intellectual disabilities, Saul Krugman and colleagues deliberately infected children with hepatitis to study the disease and test protection. Parents consented, but admission to the overcrowded institution was at times linked to joining the study. The work distinguished hepatitis A from B and contributed to vaccines, but is widely cited as unethical.
- **Tuskegee (1932–1972).** Not a vaccine study, but the US Public Health Service's decades-long observation of untreated syphilis in Black men, without informed consent and with treatment withheld even after penicillin became standard, is often cited as a reason for distrust of public health among Black Americans.
- **Trovan, Kano, Nigeria (1996).** Pfizer tested an antibiotic during a meningitis epidemic; families alleged lack of consent, and the episode later fed into the Kano polio vaccine boycott. See [[trovan-kano-and-the-polio-boycott|Trovan, Kano and the polio boycott]].
- **Early polio trials** tested vaccines on institutionalised children before the 1954 field trial, a common practice at the time.

## Informed consent in vaccine trials

Participants must be told the purpose, procedures, risks and benefits, and that they may withdraw. For children, parents give permission and children old enough give assent. Ethics boards review protocols; independent monitoring boards watch for harm during trials.

## Human challenge studies

In a **controlled human infection** (challenge) study, volunteers are deliberately exposed to a pathogen, usually after vaccination or placebo, under close medical supervision. This can test efficacy quickly with few participants. It has a long history: Jenner's 1796 experiment exposed James Phipps to smallpox after cowpox, and Walter Reed's 1900 yellow fever experiments used volunteers exposed to mosquitoes (with informed consent forms that are among the earliest on record).

Modern challenge studies are used mainly where the disease is treatable or self-limiting: malaria (participants are treated promptly), cholera, typhoid, influenza and RSV. They helped license an oral cholera vaccine (Vaxchora) in the US in 2016, based on a challenge trial. In 2021, the UK ran the first SARS-CoV-2 challenge study in young healthy volunteers, controversial because no rescue therapy was fully reliable at the time.

Arguments for: speed, small numbers, detailed immune studies. Arguments against: deliberately causing illness, concerns about paying participants, and limited generalisability from young healthy volunteers.

## Ethics in deployment

Ethical questions continue after licensing:

- **Placebos when an effective vaccine exists** (see [the placebo debate](03-placebo-controls-debate.md)).
- **Trials in low-income countries**: questions about standards of care, consent and whether communities benefit. The Dengvaxia episode in the Philippines is a later example of deployment ethics; see [[dengvaxia-in-the-philippines|Dengvaxia in the Philippines]].
- **Unblinding and crossover**: offering placebo recipients the vaccine once it is proven effective, as happened with COVID-19 trials, helps participants but ends long-term controlled comparison.

## Key ideas

- Research ethics (Nuremberg, Helsinki, Belmont) grew from abuses; Willowbrook is a vaccine-related example, Tuskegee a broader one that shapes trust.
- Informed consent and independent review are required for vaccine trials.
- Human challenge studies deliberately infect volunteers to test vaccines quickly; they are used where disease is treatable and remain ethically debated.
- Ethical issues continue after licensing, especially in low-income settings.
