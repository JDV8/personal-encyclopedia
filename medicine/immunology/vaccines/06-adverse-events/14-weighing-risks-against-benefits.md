## The basic comparison

Every vaccine decision weighs the risks of vaccinating against the risks of not vaccinating. The comparison requires four numbers: how likely the person is to get the disease without the vaccine, how harmful the disease is, how much the vaccine reduces that risk, and how often the vaccine causes harm. All of these vary by age, health, place and time.

## Worked examples

- **Measles and MMR**: measles kills about 1–3 per 1,000 children infected in rich countries and causes encephalitis in about 1 per 1,000; MMR causes febrile seizures in about 1 per 3,000 doses and ITP in about 1 per 30,000–40,000, both generally self-limiting ([France et al., *Pediatrics* 2008](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2007-1578); [measles](../03-diseases/measles.md)). Where measles circulates, the comparison is lopsided. Where it has been eliminated, the individual benefit depends on herd immunity, which itself depends on most people being vaccinated.
- **Rotavirus vaccine**: about 1–6 extra intussusception cases per 100,000 infants against tens of thousands of hospitalisations prevented each year in the US ([intussusception](05-intussusception-and-rotavirus-vaccines.md)).
- **COVID-19 vaccines**: for older adults in 2021, the benefit was very large; for healthy young males after prior infection, the small myocarditis risk was closer to the small benefit ([myocarditis](08-myocarditis-and-mrna-vaccines.md); [VITT](09-vitt-and-adenovirus-vector-vaccines.md)).
- **Smallpox vaccine**: once smallpox was eradicated, any risk was too much, and routine vaccination stopped.
- **Oral polio vaccine**: worth its VAPP risk during eradication, not in countries free of wild polio ([vaccine-derived polio](10-vaccine-derived-polio.md)).

## Why people weigh the same numbers differently

Benefit–risk judgements are not purely technical:

- **Omission bias**: harms caused by acting (vaccinating) often feel worse than equal or larger harms from not acting.
- **Visibility**: vaccine harms are concrete and identifiable; prevented diseases are invisible statistics.
- **Free-riding**: when others are vaccinated, an individual's own risk of disease falls, so the individual calculation differs from the community one. This is the core of the ethical debate about mandates ([[mandates-and-ethics|mandates and ethics]]).
- **Trust**: if people doubt official estimates of risk or benefit, the numbers carry less weight ([trust and misinformation](../07-controversies/19-trust-and-misinformation.md)).
- **Uncertainty**: rare or long-term harms may not yet be known for new vaccines, while benefits may be overestimated from early studies. Critics argue that regulators underweight this uncertainty; regulators argue that delaying vaccines has costs too.

## Absolute versus relative risk

Benefit–risk is best expressed in absolute terms: events per million doses, or number needed to vaccinate to prevent one case versus number needed to harm. Relative risks ("doubles the risk") sound alarming for rare events and impressive for vaccine efficacy, and both sides of the debate use them selectively ([efficacy, effectiveness and waning](../../../epidemiology/13-efficacy-effectiveness-and-waning.md)).

## Compensation as part of the balance

Because vaccination benefits society as well as the individual, many countries accept a duty to compensate the rare people harmed. No-fault programmes are designed to acknowledge that vaccine injuries happen without requiring proof of negligence ([VICP](../08-litigation-and-compensation/03-vaccine-injury-compensation-program.md); [Canada's programmes](../08-litigation-and-compensation/07-canadas-compensation-programs.md)).

## Key ideas

- Benefit–risk compares disease risk, disease severity, vaccine effectiveness and vaccine harm, all of which vary by person and setting.
- The same vaccine can be clearly worthwhile in one group and marginal in another.
- Psychology, trust and ethics shape how people weigh the numbers; absolute risks communicate them best.
