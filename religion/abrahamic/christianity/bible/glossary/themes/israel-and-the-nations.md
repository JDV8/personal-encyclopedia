From the beginning, the promise to Abraham is for a particular people and, through them, a blessing for the families of the earth. The Torah and the Former Prophets are mostly the story of that particular people in the land. The prophets then place Israel among the nations: oracles against empires, a surprising use of Assyria or Cyrus as the Lord's rod or anointed, and visions of nations coming to Zion (Isaiah 2, Micah 4) or being judged in the valley of decision (Joel). Jonah is the sharpest short tale of pity for a foreign city.

The New Testament's argument is that this blessing of the nations has happened, unexpectedly, through a crucified Messiah, without erasing Israel. Acts narrates the threshold; Paul in Romans 9–11 forbids Gentile boasting over the branches. Later Christian history often failed that test. A first-principles reading keeps the particularity of Israel and the scope of the blessing together, because the Bible does.
