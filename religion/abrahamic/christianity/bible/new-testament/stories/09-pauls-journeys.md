# Paul's journeys

**Prerequisites:** [The early church](08-early-church.md), [Acts](../books/history/acts.md) 13–28, and [Paul's letters](../05-pauls-letters.md).

**Key ideas:** Acts arranges Paul's work in a series of journeys from Antioch and, finally, to Rome; the letters are earlier and should correct as well as fill out the narrative; the council of Jerusalem, the cities of the Aegean, and the long arrest are the spine; a map is useful, and a timetable should stay humble.

## A working map

The traditional "three missionary journeys" plus a voyage to Rome is a teaching scheme, not a label Acts itself prints. It is still a usable map.

- **First journey (Acts 13–14):** Cyprus and the cities of southern Asia Minor (Pisidian Antioch, Iconium, Lystra, Derbe), with a pattern of synagogue preaching, mixed response, and return to Antioch.
- **The council (Acts 15):** the Gentile question, testimony from Peter and from Barnabas and Paul, and James's judgment, with a letter about a few required abstentions. Galatians 2 is the essential companion and is not easy to line up date for date.
- **Second journey (Acts 15:36–18:22):** after a split with Barnabas, Paul travels with Silas through the Cilician hinterland, takes Timothy, is directed in a vision to Macedonia, and works in Philippi, Thessalonica, Beroea, Athens, and Corinth (a long stay, and the likely setting of 1 Thessalonians).
- **Third journey (Acts 18:23–21:17):** a long Ephesus stay, a swing through Macedonia and Greece, the collection in the background, a farewell at Miletus, and the road to Jerusalem.
- **Arrest and Rome (Acts 21–28):** a riot in the temple, hearings, the appeal to Caesar, shipwreck, and arrival in the capital, where the book ends with preaching under a kind of house arrest.

## Letters on the road

The undisputed letters do not narrate journeys as Acts does; they assume them. 1 Thessalonians looks back to a short, intense founding visit. The Corinthian correspondence implies a painful visit and changed travel plans that Acts does not all report. Romans is written, probably from Corinth, looking west to Spain. Philippians and Philemon (and, if authentic in this setting, Colossians and Ephesians) speak from a prison. A responsible map uses both kinds of source and admits where they chafe: the number of Jerusalem visits is the classic example.

Historically, the Aegean years in the early 50s are the best-attested period of Paul's life. The later Roman stay, and the tradition of a subsequent release or of martyrdom under Nero, go beyond Acts and depend on later sources and on inferences from the Pastorals, whose authorship is itself debated.

## What the journeys are for

Acts wants the reader to see the word running from Jerusalem to the capital, through synagogue and agora, in front of magistrates and a philosopher's court, without being a revolt against Rome. The letters want the reader to see churches — messy, gifted, and in need of a gospel that does not require Gentiles to become Jews, and that still does not treat Israel as discarded. Both aims belong in the picture. A "missionary biography" that only traces miles is thinner than either source.

The collection for Jerusalem, easy to treat as a footnote, is in fact one of Paul's great theological projects: a material sign that Gentile churches owe themselves to the saints in the city from which the gospel went out. That it may have failed as diplomacy, if Romans 15's fears were well founded, does not make it less important.
