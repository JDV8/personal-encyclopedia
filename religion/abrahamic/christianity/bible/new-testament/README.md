# New Testament

**Prerequisites:** the Bible [foundations](../README.md), especially [canon](../02-canon-and-hebrew-bible-vs-old-testament.md) and [genres](../04-genres.md). The [Old Testament path](../old-testament/README.md), or at least [exile and return](../old-testament/10-exile-and-return.md), should come first.
**Key ideas:** a first-century Jewish movement in the Roman world; start with that context, then the Gospels, then the church's first history, then Paul, then Revelation.

The New Testament is twenty-seven books written in Greek, all, in the usual scholarly dating, within the first century or the early second. It is shorter than the Old Testament and more tightly focused: Jesus of Nazareth, and the communities that formed around the claim that he was raised from the dead and is Lord and Messiah.

A Christian Bible invites you to turn the page from Malachi into Matthew, as if the second library simply continues the first. In a way it does: the same God, the same scriptures (quoted constantly), the same hopes of covenant and Davidic restoration. In another way it does not. Between the last Old Testament events and the first Gospel scenes lie centuries of Persian, Hellenistic, and Roman rule, and a Jewish world of temple, synagogue, parties, and scripture that the Gospels assume and rarely pause to explain. So this path starts with that world, not with a birth narrative.

Traditional Christian reading takes these books as apostolic witness, theologically unified, and historically reliable in their main claims. Historical-critical reading dates, sources, and diversifies them: Mark earlier than Luke and Matthew, John later and different, some letters of Paul undisputed and some debated, Revelation as apocalyptic resistance literature. This path will keep both in view, as usual.

## References & further reading

- Bart D. Ehrman, *The New Testament: A Historical Introduction to the Early Christian Writings* (Beginner). A clear scholarly introduction to the books and their settings.
- Raymond E. Brown, *An Introduction to the New Testament* (Intermediate). Fuller, more cautious, and widely used in seminary classrooms.
- N. T. Wright, *The New Testament and the People of God* (Advanced). Places the texts inside Second Temple Jewish hope and early Christian claims.
