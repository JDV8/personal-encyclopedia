# 2 Peter

**Name and meaning.** The Second Letter of Peter, a testament-like warning against false teachers and a defense of the certainty of the Lord's coming, even if it seems delayed. It is closely related to [Jude](jude.md), with which it shares a block of material, and it contains the New Testament's most explicit statement about the inspiration of prophecy and a memorable word about the Lord's patience.

**Prerequisites:** [1 Peter](1-peter.md) and [Jude](jude.md).

## Dating and authorship

The letter claims to be Peter's second letter, written near his death, recalling the transfiguration and referring to Paul's letters as already in a collection, "which the ignorant and unstable twist… as they do the other scriptures" (3:15–16). Traditional reading accepts that claim. The majority of modern critical scholars date the book late (even early second century), making it perhaps the latest New Testament writing, because of the Greek, the Pauline-letter collection already in view, the delay-of-the-parousia problem, and the literary relationship with Jude (usually taken as 2 Peter's source). It was also one of the slower books to be received as canonical. Both the traditional and the critical accounts should be taught.

## Context

Scoffers are asking, "Where is the promise of his coming?" and false teachers are accused of greed, sexual license, and a misuse of freedom. The author answers with a reminder of divine judgment in the past (the angels who sinned, the flood, Sodom), with a doctrine of delayed judgment as patience, and with a hope of new heavens and a new earth.

## Outline

- **1.** A greeting dense with promises; a ladder of virtues; a reminder that will be useful "after my departure"; the transfiguration as eyewitness confirmation; prophecy not of one's own interpretation.
- **2.** False teachers, in language very close to Jude; Balaam; a bleak portrait of people who promise freedom and are themselves enslaved.
- **3.** The scoffers; a day as a thousand years; the Lord's patience; a dissolution and renewal of creation; a final charge to grow in grace, and a doxology.

## Key passages and ideas

1:16–21, the transfiguration and the lamp of prophecy, is a theory of testimony: this is not "cleverly devised myths," and scripture is not a private playground. 3:8–13, the patience of the Lord who does not want any to perish, and the hope of a new creation after a fiery judgment, has shaped later eschatology (including some readings that press the fire more literally than others). 3:15–16 is priceless historical evidence that Pauline letters were already being read as a group and were beginning to be classed with "scriptures," and that they were already hard to interpret.

The relationship with Jude is a first-principles lesson in how early Christian writers reused one another. Comparison of the two is the right exercise, not a scandal.

If the letter is late, its claim to be Peter's testament is a known ancient genre (compare the farewell of Deuteronomy, or later testaments in Jewish literature), not simply a modern idea of fraud. First-century and second-century readers had to decide whether such a voice was faithful. The church eventually said yes, slowly. That slowness is worth remembering when the book is used today as if its authorship had never been a question. The content that won it a place is the combination of ethical warning and a patient, creation-wide hope.

## Themes and reception

Themes include knowledge, memory, false teaching, delay, patience, and new creation. The book's difficult canonical history is worth knowing: some early lists doubted it, and then the church kept it. Read it with Jude, and with the delay already visible in [2 Thessalonians](../pauline-epistles/2-thessalonians.md).

The ethical lists and the attack on the teachers are severe. As with other polemic in the New Testament, a modern reader has to distinguish the pastoral need to warn from the temptation to use this language against every opponent.
