# Hebrews

**Name and meaning.** The Letter (or, better, the sermon) to the Hebrews is formally anonymous. Its traditional English title reflects an early inference that it was written to Jewish Christians. It is a carefully crafted argument that Jesus is the final and superior revelation, the true high priest after the order of Melchizedek, and the mediator of a new covenant, and that the hearers must not drift back or grow dull.

**Prerequisites:** [Leviticus](../../../../old-testament/books/law-torah/leviticus.md), [Psalms](../../../../old-testament/books/wisdom-poetry/psalms.md) 110, and [Jeremiah](../../../../old-testament/books/major-prophets/jeremiah.md) 31. Helpful: [Pauline epistles](../pauline-epistles/README.md), to see both kinship and difference.

## Dating and authorship

The oldest manuscripts do not name an author. The Eastern church long associated the work with Paul; the West was more hesitant, and modern scholarship almost unanimously rejects Pauline authorship on grounds of style, vocabulary, and theology (the letter's Greek is more polished than Paul's, and its priesthood argument is unique). Suggested authors have included Barnabas, Apollos (Luther's suggestion), Priscilla, and others; none is more than a guess. "Only God knows," as Origen already said. Dates often fall in the 60s or 80s CE. If the present temple is assumed still to stand (the argument is made from the tabernacle more than from Herod's temple), a date before 70 is possible; if the book is coping with the temple's loss, a later date is also possible. The destruction is not mentioned.

## Context

The hearers know scripture well, have already suffered (10:32–34), and are tired. The danger is not described as a return to "Judaism" in later Christian slogans so much as a drifting, a neglect, a shrinking back. The author uses typological reading of the Greek Old Testament, exhortation, and an elevated Christology to stiffen their endurance.

## Outline

- **1–2.** The Son superior to angels; a warning not to drift; the Son who shares flesh and blood and is a merciful high priest.
- **3–4.** Jesus greater than Moses; the promised rest, and the warning of Psalm 95.
- **5–7.** The high priest after Melchizedek; a warning against dull hearing; the sure hope "behind the curtain."
- **8–10.** The new covenant; the earthly sanctuary as a sketch; the once-for-all offering; a call to draw near, and a severe warning against persistent rejection.
- **11–13.** The catalogue of faith; Jesus the pioneer and perfecter; discipline; a final exhortation, including the altar, the city to come, and a doxology.

## Key passages and ideas

The opening sentences (1:1–4) are a Christological overture: the God who spoke by the prophets has spoken by a Son, heir, agent of creation, radiance of glory, who made purification and sat down. Psalm 110 is the engine of the priesthood argument: a priest-king forever, not from Levi. Melchizedek of Genesis 14 becomes, in this typological reading, a figure of an earlier and greater priesthood. The new covenant of Jeremiah 31 is quoted at length and said to make the first one "obsolete."

That last claim has to be taught with historical and moral care. For the author, it is a scriptural argument about covenants and sacrifices. For later Christian anti-Jewish reading, it became a blanket dismissal of Israel. The book itself is written, in all probability, to people of Israel’s scriptures, not as a Gentile sneer. 11:1 and the roll of ancestral faith, and 12:1–2, Jesus as pioneer and perfecter, have had a huge devotional afterlife. The severe warnings (6:4–8; 10:26–31) have troubled later debates about apostasy and assurance.

The Greek of Hebrews is among the most cultivated in the New Testament, which is one more reason the Pauline ascription faded under closer study. The author thinks like a preacher who knows how to use typology without pausing to explain the method: Melchizedek, the veil, the once-a-year entrance, the camp outside which Jesus suffered. A reader who does not yet know Leviticus will feel lost, and that is appropriate. The book was written for insiders of Israel's cultic imagination. It cannot be made easy by skipping the types.

## Themes and reception

Themes include revelation, priesthood, covenant, once-for-all sacrifice, faith as endurance, and a city to come. The book's path into the canon was slower in the West than in the East. It became central to Christian typological reading of the Old Testament and to later doctrines of atonement and of Christ's heavenly intercession. Read it as a sermon, slowly, with Leviticus open.
