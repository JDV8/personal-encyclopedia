# James

**Name and meaning.** The Letter of James, a tightly packed work of moral exhortation in good Greek, addressing "the twelve tribes in the Dispersion." It is less a narrative letter than a collection of sayings and short essays on trials, speech, wealth, partiality, and a faith that does something.

**Prerequisites:** [Sermon on the Mount](../../../stories/03-sermon-on-the-mount.md) (the kinship of tone is close) and [Paul's letters](../../../05-pauls-letters.md) on faith and works, which this book is often read against.

## Dating and authorship

Tradition identifies the author with James "the Lord's brother," the leader of the Jerusalem church (Acts 15; Galatians 1–2), which would place the letter relatively early (James was killed in the early 60s, according to Josephus). Many modern scholars, noting the quality of the Greek and a possible lag after the first generation, date it later in the first century and treat the name as a pseudonym, while others still defend the traditional ascription. Luther famously called it an "epistle of straw" because he thought it undercut justification by faith; that reception is part of the book's history and is no longer a consensus even in Lutheran scholarship.

## Context

The hearers are assemblies (*synagogai* in 2:2) where the poor may be dishonored and the rich honored, where teachers talk more than they do, and where patience is needed until the coming of the Lord. The wisdom is international (it sometimes sounds like Proverbs or like Jesus' sayings) and also sharply social.

## Outline

- **1.** Trials and steadfastness; asking for wisdom; the poor and the rich; hearing and doing the word; a religion that bridles the tongue and visits orphans and widows.
- **2.** No partiality; faith and works, with Abraham and Rahab as evidence that "faith without works is dead."
- **3.** The tongue; two kinds of wisdom.
- **4–5.** Conflicts and friendship with the world; a warning to rich oppressors; patience; the yes and no; prayer for the sick; bringing back a wanderer.

## Key passages and ideas

2:14–26 is the famous crux. James says a person is justified by works and not by faith alone, and that even the demons believe. Paul, in Galatians and Romans, says a person is justified by faith apart from works of the law. The two can be read as a contradiction, or as different opponents (Paul against requiring circumcision for Gentile inclusion; James against a barren confession that leaves the poor unclothed). Most later Christian theology has tried to hold them together; a first-principles reading should let each author finish talking first.

1:17, 1:27, 3:1–12, and 5:1–6 are equally characteristic: generous gift from the "Father of lights," a practical definition of religion, a devastating essay on speech, and a prophetic woe against withheld wages. The letter is closer to the prophets and to the Sermon on the Mount than to a Pauline rereading of Abraham, even though it uses Abraham too.

The letter's address to "the twelve tribes in the Dispersion" can be read literally (Jewish Christians abroad) or theologically (the whole church as Israel in exile). Either way, James writes as a teacher of a people who already confess "our glorious Lord Jesus Christ" (2:1) and who still need to be told that favoritism in the assembly is blasphemy against that name. The Greek is competent; the moral world is that of Israel's wisdom and of Jesus' own sayings. A late Gentile church that had forgotten that combination would have found this book uncomfortable. That is one reason to keep reading it.

## Themes and reception

Themes include integrity, speech, the poor, patience, and an active faith. After Luther's suspicion, the book was recovered as wisdom and as social protest. Its canonical status was questioned in antiquity by some, and then secured. Read it aloud; it was meant to sting.

The letter also assumes a praying, anointing, confessing community (5:13–20). It is not only ethics. It is a picture of a modest assembly that still believes prayer matters for the sick and that restoring a wanderer covers a multitude of sins.
