# 2 Corinthians

**Name and meaning.** The Second Letter of Paul to the Corinthians in the canon, though the relationship was more complicated than the numbering suggests: a painful visit, a severe letter, and a reconciliation lie behind this text, and many scholars think the canonical book itself may combine more than one letter. It is Paul's most personal letter, a defense of his apostleship and of a ministry that looks weak.

**Prerequisites:** [1 Corinthians](1-corinthians.md) and [Paul's journeys](../../../stories/10-pauls-journeys.md).

## Dating and authorship

Undisputed Paul, usually about 55–56 CE, written from Macedonia after Titus brought good news from Corinth (2:12–13; 7:5–16). A common critical view treats chapters 1–9 (or 1–7) as a letter of consolation and chapters 10–13 as a harsher letter (perhaps the "severe letter," or a later one) against rival "super-apostles." Others defend the unity of the book as a single, shifting argument. The collection for Jerusalem (chapters 8–9) may also have had a separate life. Traditional reading takes the sixteen chapters as one letter. Both possibilities should be named.

## Context

Paul's authority in Corinth has been challenged by rival missionaries who, in his telling, boast in rhetoric, visions, and support, and who belittle his bodily presence and his refusal to take pay. He answers with a theology of weakness: the treasure is in clay jars, and the Lord's power is made perfect in weakness.

## Outline

- **1–2.** Blessing the God of all consolation; a delayed visit explained; forgiveness for an offender; God's triumph in Christ as a fragrance.
- **3–7.** Ministers of a new covenant; the veil; treasure in clay jars; the ministry of reconciliation; an appeal not to receive grace in vain; the joyful report from Titus.
- **8–9.** The collection: the example of Macedonia, Christ's generosity, cheerful giving.
- **10–13.** A harsher tone: boasting "as a fool," the list of hardships, the thorn in the flesh, a coming third visit, a closing trinitarian blessing.

## Key passages and ideas

3:6–18, the new covenant and the Spirit who gives freedom, rereads [Exodus](../../../../old-testament/books/law-torah/exodus.md) and [Jeremiah](../../../../old-testament/books/major-prophets/jeremiah.md) in a Pauline key. 5:17–21, the new creation and the ministry of reconciliation ("God was in Christ reconciling the world to himself"), is among the most quoted summaries of Paul's gospel. 8:9 uses Christ's "becoming poor" as the pattern for the collection: theology funds ethics.

The "fool's speech" in chapters 11–12 inverts every Greco-Roman honor code: Paul boasts of beatings, shipwrecks, anxiety for the churches, and a thorn he could not pray away. The thorn is not identified; the sentence "my grace is sufficient for you" is the point, not the diagnosis. The letter ends without a tidy narrative resolution; the church's later inclusion of both 1 and 2 Corinthians implies that the relationship, however strained, held.

The geography of the letter is pastoral as well as physical. Paul writes of a door opened in Troas that he could not enter because he had no rest in his spirit until he found Titus (2:12–13). The mission field is real, and so is anxiety. That is why the later "fool's speech" is not a random boast: the churches already know this man's itinerary of weakness. The collection chapters then test whether reconciliation has economic teeth. Macedonia, though poor, has begged to take part; Corinth, though gifted, has been slow. Generosity, in this letter, is a grace (the same word as "gift") and a proof that the reconciling word has been believed.

## Themes and reception

Themes include consolation, apostolic weakness, new covenant, reconciliation, generosity, and boasting in the Lord. The letter has been a school for pastors and for anyone who has had to defend a vocation without becoming a braggart. Partition theories matter for historical reconstruction; they should not keep a reader from following the emotional and theological movement of the canonical book.
