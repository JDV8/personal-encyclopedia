# 1 Corinthians

**Name and meaning.** The First Letter of Paul to the Corinthians (at least the first that entered the canon; 5:9 mentions an earlier letter). It is a pastoral and argumentative letter to a gifted, divided, and morally messy church in a major Greek city, covering factions, lawsuits, sexuality, idol-food, worship, spiritual gifts, and the resurrection.

**Prerequisites:** [Paul's letters](../../../05-pauls-letters.md) and [Paul's journeys](../../../stories/10-pauls-journeys.md). Acts 18 is the founding narrative.

## Dating and authorship

Undisputed Paul, usually dated about 54–55 CE, written from Ephesus (16:8). The letter answers reports from "Chloe's people" and a set of written questions ("now concerning…"). Some scholars see seams that might suggest a composite, but most treat 1 Corinthians as a single letter, if a loosely structured one.

## Context

Corinth was a Roman colony, a port, and a place of status-seeking, patronage, and religious variety. The church included people of different social levels (1:26–31; 11:17–22). Wisdom, rhetoric, and spiritual display were prized. Paul had founded the community and is now a physically absent apostle trying to reassert a gospel of the cross in a church tempted to trade it for a more impressive spirituality.

## Outline

- **1–4.** Factions around Paul, Apollos, Cephas, and "Christ"; the word of the cross versus the wisdom of the world; apostles as servants and as "the rubbish of the world."
- **5–6.** A case of sexual immorality; lawsuits among believers; the body as a temple.
- **7.** Marriage, celibacy, and remaining in one's calling, "in view of the impending crisis."
- **8–10.** Food offered to idols: knowledge, love, the weak, Israel's examples, and the Lord's table versus the table of demons.
- **11.** Head coverings in worship; abuses at the Lord's Supper and the tradition of the institution.
- **12–14.** Spiritual gifts, the body metaphor, the hymn to love in chapter 13, and order in prophesying and tongues.
- **15.** The resurrection of Christ and of the dead; the Adam–Christ contrast; a mystery of change.
- **16.** The collection, travel plans, final greetings.

## Key passages and ideas

1:18–25, the cross as scandal and foolishness, is the letter's theological center. All the later problems (status at the table, showy gifts, denial of a future resurrection) are, in Paul's mind, versions of fleeing the cross. Chapter 13 is not a wedding reading first of all; it is the criterion for a church addicted to gifts. Chapter 15 is the earliest extant list of resurrection witnesses (including a appearance to "more than five hundred" and to James and to Paul himself), and a long argument that if the dead are not raised, Christian faith is empty.

The passages on women in worship (11:2–16; 14:34–35) and on sexuality are among the most contested in contemporary reading. A first-principles approach describes what Paul argues in context (honor, creation order, local custom, the coming of the kingdom) and does not pretend that later churches have agreed on how to apply them. The Lord's Supper tradition in 11:23–26 is among the oldest liturgical texts in the New Testament, older than the written Gospels.

The collection of chapter 16, easy to treat as housekeeping, is in fact part of the letter's theology of the body. The Gentile churches are to remember "the saints" in Jerusalem with money, not only with doctrine. That project will dominate [2 Corinthians](2-corinthians.md) 8–9 and will take Paul, in Acts, toward a dangerous appointment in the city. A church divided by status at the Lord's table is being asked to become, across the empire, a single economy of gift. The closing "Maranatha" and the grace of the Lord Jesus belong with that horizon: this community is not a local club. It waits for a coming, and it already owes itself to others.

## Themes and reception

Themes include the cross, unity, the body, holiness, love, and resurrection. The letter is a primary source for early Christian worship, social life, and conflict. It has been used for church order, for charismatic renewal, and for moral discipline, sometimes wisely and sometimes as a blunt instrument. Read it as a conversation with one ancient city church, not as a manual dropped from the sky.
