# Philemon

**Name and meaning.** The Letter to Philemon, the shortest of the undisputed Pauline letters, written from prison to a householder (probably in Colossae) about Onesimus, an enslaved man who has been with Paul and is now being sent back. It is a masterpiece of persuasion and a difficult text for later ethics of slavery.

**Prerequisites:** [Colossians](colossians.md), which greets many of the same people and mentions Onesimus, and [Paul's letters](../../05-pauls-letters.md).

## Dating and authorship

Undisputed Paul, written from prison, often dated in the mid-50s (if from Ephesus) or early 60s (if from Rome), and closely tied to Colossians by overlapping names (Archippus, Onesimus, Epaphras, Mark, Aristarchus, Demas, Luke). Traditional and critical readings agree on Pauline authorship here, even when they disagree about Colossians.

## Context

Onesimus has come to Paul, become a Christian, and been "useful" (the name *Onesimus* means useful). The legal and social facts are not all stated. He may be a runaway, or a slave sent to the prisoner, or a man in some other breach with his master. Paul will not command Philemon, though he could "in Christ be bold enough to order." He appeals for love's sake, asks that Onesimus be received "no longer as a slave but more than a slave, a beloved brother," and hints that Philemon will do "even more than I say." He offers to pay any debt, writing the pledge in his own hand, and mentions his hope of a visit.

## Outline

The letter is too short for heavy division, but its rhetoric has a clear movement:

- Greeting to Philemon, Apphia, Archippus, and the church in the house, with thanksgiving for Philemon's love and faith.
- The appeal for Onesimus: Paul's child in prison, once useless, now useful to both; sent back "as my own heart"; a possible word-play on receiving him as Paul's heart and as a partner.
- The IOU, the confidence of obedience, a requested guest room, greetings, grace.

## Key passages and ideas

The theological hinge is "a beloved brother… both in the flesh and in the Lord." Paul does not write an abolitionist tract, and later slaveholding Christians often used that silence (and other household codes) to defend slavery. Other readers, including many later abolitionists, have heard in "more than a slave" and "even more than I say" a pressure that, if followed through, undermines the institution. A first-principles reading must report both the letter's restraint and its explosive request, and must not pretend that the history of interpretation has been innocent.

The letter is also a lesson in Christian persuasion: thanksgiving before the ask, public address (the church hears the letter), humor, and a bill that Paul will cover. Authority here is real and is deliberately not used as a command.

Colossians 4:9, which commends Onesimus as "the faithful and beloved brother, who is one of you," is the canonical companion to this note. If the two letters traveled together, the church in Philemon's house heard the personal appeal in public and also heard Onesimus named as a coworker. That is more than a private favor. It is a community being asked to recognize a new status that the law of the household did not yet know how to name.

## Themes and reception

Themes include partnership, intercession, the transformation of a social relationship by the gospel, and the limits of what a short letter will say. Onesimus later appears in tradition as a bishop; that is reception, not a claim of the text. Read Philemon with Colossians, and with the household codes, as part of one early Christian conversation about existing social structures — a conversation the churches have had to continue, and in some centuries failed badly.
