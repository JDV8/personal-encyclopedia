# 2 Timothy

**Name and meaning.** The Second Letter to Timothy, the most personal of the Pastorals: a farewell-like charge from a prisoner who has been deserted by some and supported by others, urging Timothy to rekindle a gift, to endure hardship, and to hold to the sacred writings he has known from childhood.

**Prerequisites:** [1 Timothy](1-timothy.md) and [Paul's letters](../../05-pauls-letters.md).

## Dating and authorship

See [1 Timothy](1-timothy.md). If Pauline, this may be the last letter, from a second Roman imprisonment, near the end of Paul's life (tradition places his death in the mid-60s under Nero). If deutero-Pauline, it is a testament in the apostle's voice, gathering his legacy for a next generation. The personal names (Lois, Eunice, Onesiphorus, Demas, Luke, Mark, and many others) give it a strongly particular feel either way.

## Context

The speaker is in chains, "already being poured out as a libation," and he asks Timothy to come before winter, to bring a cloak and the books, and to keep the faith in a time when people will not endure sound teaching. Cowardice, shame at a imprisoned apostle, and the lure of "itching ears" are the pastoral dangers.

## Outline

- **1.** Thanksgiving for Timothy's sincere faith; a call not to be ashamed; the pattern of sound words; examples of defection and of Onesiphorus's courage.
- **2.** A good soldier, athlete, and farmer; a trustworthy saying about dying and living with Christ; a worker who rightly handles the word; a warning about disputes and about Hymenaeus and Philetus, who say the resurrection has already happened.
- **3.** Last days of stress and of religious form without power; the example of Paul's persecutions; the sacred writings that are able to instruct for salvation and are "inspired by God" (3:16).
- **4.** A solemn charge to preach the word; the finished race; practical requests and greetings; "the Lord will rescue me… and save me for his heavenly kingdom."

## Key passages and ideas

1:7, a spirit not of cowardice but of power, love, and self-discipline, has had a long devotional life and belongs in a context of shame and fear. 2:2, entrusting the teaching to faithful people who will teach others, is a classic text for later ideas of succession, without yet being a developed theory of office. 3:14–17 is the New Testament's most explicit statement about the usefulness of "sacred writings" (in the first instance, the Jewish scriptures Timothy knew as a child). "Inspired by God" (*theopneustos*) has been a load-bearing word in later doctrines of scripture; the verse itself emphasizes usefulness for teaching, reproof, correction, and training in righteousness.

The atmosphere of chapter 4 — a desertion, a finished course, a request for a cloak — is among the most human pages in the Pauline corpus (or in the Pauline tradition). Whether taken as the apostle's last letter or as a later portrait of him, it has taught the church how to imagine ending well.

The named people in chapter 4 make the letter feel like a doorway into a real network: some have gone to other cities, some have failed the prisoner, one cloak is still at Troas. Even if a later author constructed this scene, it is constructed out of the memory of a missionary life that was itinerant, dependent, and unfinished in the world's terms. The "heavenly kingdom" is the last horizon; the books and the parchments are the last errand.

## Themes and reception

Themes include courage, sound teaching, scripture, endurance, and a good death (or a good imprisonment). The letter is often read at ordinations and at funerals. The authorship debate remains; the pastoral voice is unmistakable.
