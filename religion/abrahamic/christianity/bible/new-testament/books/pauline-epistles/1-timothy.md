# 1 Timothy

**Name and meaning.** The First Letter to Timothy, one of the three Pastoral Epistles (with 2 Timothy and Titus). It is presented as Paul's instruction to his younger coworker Timothy, left in Ephesus, about false teaching, public prayer, the conduct of men and women, and the qualifications of overseers and deacons.

**Prerequisites:** [Paul's letters](../../../05-pauls-letters.md) and [Acts](../history/acts.md) on Timothy (16:1–3 and later).

## Dating and authorship

Traditional reading takes all three Pastorals as late letters of Paul, perhaps after the imprisonment narrated in Acts, in the mid-60s. A majority of modern critical scholars regard them as a corpus written in Paul's name in the late first or early second century, because of vocabulary and style, a more settled church office structure, and a different feel from the undisputed letters. Conservative scholarship continues to defend Pauline authorship, sometimes with a secretary. This is one of the sharpest authorship disagreements in the New Testament, and an overview should not hide it. The letters are canonical and pastoral either way; "Paul" in what follows means the letter's speaker.

## Context

The stated situation is Ephesus, with "certain people" teaching myths, genealogies, and a misuse of the law, and with some who have "suffered shipwreck in the faith." The letter is concerned with the health of a household-like church: prayer for rulers, modest and quiet conduct, care in appointing leaders, honor for real widows, and a warning that the love of money is a root of all kinds of evil.

## Outline

- **1.** Charge to Timothy; the law's proper use; Paul's own mercy as a pattern; a commission.
- **2–3.** Prayer for all, including rulers; instruction about men and women in worship; qualifications for overseers (bishops) and deacons; the church as the household of God.
- **4.** Warning against later false teaching (forbidding marriage and certain foods); a young minister's training.
- **5–6.** Widows, elders, slaves; false teachers and wealth; a closing charge.

## Key passages and ideas

2:5–6, "one God and one mediator… the man Christ Jesus, who gave himself a ransom," is a compact creed. 2:8–15, on women learning in silence and not having authority over a man, with a reading of Adam and Eve and a difficult sentence about being "saved through childbearing," is among the most contested passages in the New Testament. Historical reading asks what local false teaching and what household norms are in view; later church practice has ranged from taking the text as a transcultural ban on women teaching to seeing it as a local restriction. Teach the text, the range of reception, and the fact of disagreement.

The qualification lists in chapter 3 are a window on an emerging office structure (overseer, deacon, and, in some readings, women deacons or deacons' wives in 3:11). 3:16 appears to quote an early hymn of incarnation and exaltation. 6:6–10, godliness with contentment, is wisdom for a church already tempted by gain.

The letter's concern for public reputation — a bishop must be "well thought of by outsiders" — shows a church no longer only on the move. It has a household to keep, a table, a list of widows, and a stake in civic peace. Whether one laments that settling or honors it, the Pastorals are indispensable evidence that the movement had to become an institution without, in its own mind, losing the gospel of mercy at its center.

## Themes and reception

Themes include sound teaching, church order, the household of God, and the dangers of controversy and wealth. The Pastorals have been central to later Catholic, Orthodox, and Protestant debates about ministry and, more recently, about gender. Whether or not Paul wrote them, they show what a second (or late first) generation thought a Pauline church should look like as it settled in the world.
