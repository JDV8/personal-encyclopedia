# 1 Thessalonians

**Name and meaning.** The First Letter of Paul to the Thessalonians, almost certainly the earliest surviving Pauline letter, and probably the earliest document in the New Testament. It is a thanksgiving, a defense of Paul's manner of ministry, and a pastoral word about holiness and about those who have died before the Lord's coming.

**Prerequisites:** [Paul's letters](../../../05-pauls-letters.md), [Acts](../history/acts.md) 17, and [Paul's journeys](../../../stories/10-pauls-journeys.md).

## Dating and authorship

Undisputed Paul, usually dated about 50–51 CE, written from Corinth after Timothy returned from a visit to Thessalonica (3:1–6). The Acts account of a short, turbulent founding mission in the city (synagogue preaching, jealousy, a bond posted by Jason) is the usual narrative backdrop, even if the letter implies a slightly longer or more layered stay than a casual reading of Acts suggests.

## Context

The church is young, mainly Gentile (they "turned to God from idols," 1:9), and under some local pressure. A question has arisen about believers who have died: will they miss the Lord's coming? Paul answers with consolation and with a picture of the Lord's descent, the archangel's call, and a meeting "in the air," followed by a reminder that the day comes like a thief and that the children of the day should stay awake.

## Outline

- **1.** Thanksgiving for the Thessalonians' faith, love, and hope, and for their reputation in Macedonia and Achaia.
- **2–3.** Paul's ministry among them: not deceit, not flattery, but a nurse's gentleness and a father's charge; his longing; Timothy's good report.
- **4:1–12.** Holiness, especially in sexual conduct; love of the brothers and sisters; a quiet life of work.
- **4:13–5:28.** The dead in Christ; the day of the Lord; exhortations, a closing wish for sanctification, a charge to have the letter read.

## Key passages and ideas

The triad of faith, love, and hope (1:3) already appears here, earlier than in 1 Corinthians 13. 4:13–18, the consolation about the dead, is among the most influential early Christian eschatological texts. It is pastoral first, not a timetable: "that you may not grieve as others do who have no hope." Later systems of end-times teaching have sometimes pressed the "caught up" language further than the letter's purpose requires. 5:1–11 then balances that hope with sobriety: no one needs a precise schedule if they already live as those who belong to the day.

Paul's self-description in chapter 2 is a first-principles lesson in apostleship: he worked with his hands, he was gentle, he did not make excessive demands. The later conflicts in Corinth and Galatia will make more sense if this earlier self-portrait is remembered.

The letter also shows how quickly a Gentile mission had to invent a way of life. Sexual holiness, a "quiet" work, and love of the brothers and sisters are not afterthoughts to a sermon about the coming. They are how a converted household looks different from the city around it while it waits. That combination — intense hope and ordinary labor — is already the shape of Pauline ethics in this first surviving letter.

## Themes and reception

The letter is short, affectionate, and already theologically dense: election, imitation, holiness, work, and the coming of the Lord are all here in pastoral form. It has been used to reconstruct the earliest Gentile mission and to fuel later debates about the rapture and the day of the Lord. Read it as a first surviving word to a young church, and then read [2 Thessalonians](2-thessalonians.md), which may be a follow-up or a later word in Paul's name about delay.

Themes include hope, holiness, pastoral presence, and the coming of the Lord.
