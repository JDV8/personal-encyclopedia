# Philippians

**Name and meaning.** The Letter of Paul to the Philippians, a warm, often joyful letter from prison to the first European church of the Acts narrative (Acts 16), thanking them for a gift, urging unity, and including the famous hymn (or poem) of Christ's self-emptying in 2:6–11.

**Prerequisites:** [Paul's letters](../../../05-pauls-letters.md), [Acts](../history/acts.md) 16, and [Paul's journeys](../../../stories/10-pauls-journeys.md).

## Dating and authorship

Undisputed Paul, written from imprisonment. The traditional location is Rome (early 60s); alternative proposals include Ephesus or Caesarea, which would move the date earlier. The "imperial guard" (1:13) and "Caesar's household" (4:22) fit Rome well but have been explained otherwise. Some have suggested that Philippians, too, may combine notes; most readers can follow it as one letter.

## Context

The Philippian church has sent a gift through Epaphroditus, who fell ill in his service. The church seems largely healthy, with a local friction between Euodia and Syntyche (4:2) and some danger from rival preachers. Paul, not knowing whether he will live or die, interprets both outcomes as gain and asks the church to live in a manner worthy of the gospel.

## Outline

- **1.** Thanksgiving and prayer; imprisonment advanced the gospel; to live is Christ and to die is gain.
- **2.** The call to humility; the Christ hymn; Timothy and Epaphroditus.
- **3.** A warning against confidence in the flesh; Paul's own résumé and its abandonment; pressing on toward the goal; a contrast of two ways of walking.
- **4.** Appeals for unity and joy; the thank-you for the gift; a closing greeting.

## Key passages and ideas

The hymn of 2:6–11 — Christ in the form of God, not exploiting equality with God, emptying himself, taking the form of a slave, obedient to death on a cross, therefore exalted, with every knee bending — is one of the earliest and densest Christological texts in the New Testament. Scholars debate whether Paul composed it or quoted it; either way, he uses it ethically: "let the same mind be in you." It is not only a doctrine to be believed. It is a pattern to be inhabited.

3:4–11, Paul's dismantling of his own Jewish credentials in order to "gain Christ," must be taught without sliding into contempt for Judaism. Paul's polemic is against a certain use of those credentials in the Gentile mission, and he continues to speak of his people with grief and honor elsewhere (especially Romans 9–11). 4:4–7, the call to rejoice and the peace that guards hearts, has had a long devotional life and belongs in the letter's prison setting.

The gift in chapter 4 is handled with a delicacy that says a great deal about ancient patronage. Paul is grateful and also careful not to become a dependent client; he has learned to be content, and he nevertheless names the Philippians' partnership from the first day until now. Their money is "a fragrant offering," not a transaction that puts him in their debt in the ordinary civic way. Friendship, mission, and worship meet in those last paragraphs. A "joyful" letter is also a politically and socially intelligent one.

 Even the opening greeting, with its unusual mention of overseers and deacons, hints at a church already organized enough to send a gift and to need a word about shared mind rather than about first principles of the gospel itself.

## Themes and reception

Themes include joy, partnership, humility, the mind of Christ, and contentment in plenty and in want. The letter has been a favorite for Christian spirituality and for Christology. Historical questions about the prison and about a possible hymn behind 2:6–11 are real; they do not reduce the text's force.
