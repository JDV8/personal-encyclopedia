# Titus

**Name and meaning.** The Letter to Titus, the third Pastoral Epistle, addressed to a coworker left in Crete to appoint elders in every town and to teach a sound, sober life in a culture the letter itself describes, via a quoted proverb, as difficult.

**Prerequisites:** [1 Timothy](1-timothy.md). Acts 27 mentions Crete in the shipwreck voyage; a Pauline mission on the island is not narrated in Acts and belongs to the letter's (or the tradition's) own picture.

## Dating and authorship

See [1 Timothy](1-timothy.md). Titus appears in the undisputed letters as a trusted Gentile coworker (especially in [Galatians](galatians.md) and [2 Corinthians](2-corinthians.md)). The Pastoral letter uses that name to address church order and household virtue on Crete.

## Context

The charge is to put what remains in order: appoint elders who are hospitable, self-controlled, and able both to preach and to refute, and to silence "rebellious people, idle talkers and deceivers," some of whom are identified as "from the circumcision." The letter is as interested in everyday virtues (older men and women, younger women and men, slaves) as in office, because "the grace of God has appeared, bringing salvation to all," and trains people to live "in the present age" while they wait for the appearing of Jesus Christ.

## Outline

- **1.** Greeting; qualifications of elders; a Cretan proverb; a rebuke of empty talkers and of those who upset households.
- **2.** Teaching soundly to each age and station; the appearing of grace as the reason for a self-controlled, upright, and godly life.
- **3.** Subjection to rulers; a reminder of the community's own former foolishness and of salvation "not because of any works of righteousness that we had done, but according to his mercy," with a compact statement of rebirth and the Spirit; a warning about stupid controversies; practical travel notes.

## Key passages and ideas

2:11–14 and 3:4–7 are among the most gospel-dense paragraphs in the Pastorals: appearance of grace, training in virtue, mercy, washing of rebirth, renewal of the Holy Spirit, justification by grace, hope of eternal life. They should keep readers from treating these letters as mere church-manuals. The household and civic instructions (including 2:9–10 on slaves, and 3:1 on rulers) raise the same questions of cultural location as the codes in Ephesians and Colossians. 1:12 quotes a Cretan poet against Cretans, a rhetorical move that is useful for dating the letter's social world and not automatically useful as a theory of cultures.

The appointment of elders "in every town" (1:5) is a key text for later Presbyterian and episcopal (and, by contrast, congregational) arguments about church government. The letter itself is brief and practical, not a treatise on polity.

Crete, in this letter, is a test case for whether the gospel can produce a recognizable civic virtue in a place the author himself insults. The irony is sharp and should not be missed: a people described as liars are to become models of good works, and they are to do so because of mercy, not because the proverb was fair. Mission, here, includes the re-education of a reputation, including the community's own.

Titus himself, in the undisputed letters, had already been Paul's test case for Gentile freedom (Galatians 2) and his agent of reconciliation in Corinth. The Pastoral letter that bears his name is asking that same kind of coworker to do a slower work: not a crisis visit, but the appointment of leaders and the long teaching of a way of life.

## Themes and reception

Themes include sound doctrine, appointed leadership, household virtue, and salvation by mercy. With 1 Timothy, it has shaped Christian ideas of ministry and of "respectable" public life. Read it with the hymn-like gospel summaries at its center, so that order does not swallow grace.
