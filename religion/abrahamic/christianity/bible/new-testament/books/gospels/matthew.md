# Matthew

**Name and meaning.** The Gospel according to Matthew. Tradition names the apostle Matthew, the tax collector also called Levi in other Gospels. The book itself is anonymous. It is the first Gospel in the New Testament canon, a carefully structured account of Jesus as the Messiah of Israel, teacher of a greater righteousness, and Lord of a mixed community of Jews and Gentiles.

**Prerequisites:** [The four Gospels](../../../02-the-four-gospels.md), [Second Temple Judaism and Rome](../../../01-second-temple-judaism-and-rome.md), and the story lessons beginning with [birth narratives](../../../stories/01-birth-narratives.md).

## Dating and authorship

The early church (Papias, Irenaeus, and later writers) attributed this Gospel to the apostle Matthew and sometimes spoke of a Hebrew or Aramaic original. Mainstream modern scholarship generally dates the Greek Gospel we have to about 80–90 CE, after the destruction of the temple in 70, and treats it as the work of a Greek-writing Jewish-Christian author (or school) who used [Mark](mark.md) and a shared sayings tradition often called Q, plus material of his own (M). Reasons include the book's use of Mark, its concern with a church already in tension with the synagogue, and its assumption that the temple's fall is in the past (see chapter 22's parable of the king's destroyed city, often so read). Conservative scholars who hold apostolic authorship usually date it earlier, sometimes before 70. Both the traditional ascription and the critical consensus should be taught; the text itself does not name its author.

## Context

Matthew writes for a community that knows the scriptures of Israel intimately and is arguing, from inside that heritage, that Jesus fulfills the Law and the Prophets. Gentiles are already in the picture (the magi, the centurion, the Great Commission), but the first sending is "to the lost sheep of the house of Israel." The Pharisees are the chief sparring partners, in a way that later, when Christians were no longer a Jewish minority, would be dangerously easy to misuse as a general verdict on Jews. A first-principles reading keeps the dispute inside first-century Judaism.

## Outline

- **1–2.** Genealogy (Abraham to David to the exile to Jesus, with four named women); birth, magi, flight to Egypt, massacre, Nazareth — see [birth narratives](../../../stories/01-birth-narratives.md).
- **3–4.** John the Baptist; baptism and temptation; the move to Capernaum; first disciples — see [baptism and temptation](../../../stories/02-baptism-and-temptation.md).
- **5–7.** The [Sermon on the Mount](../../../stories/03-sermon-on-the-mount.md).
- **8–10.** [Miracles](../../../stories/04-miracles.md) and the mission discourse.
- **11–13.** Controversy, parables of the kingdom — see [parables](../../../stories/05-parables.md).
- **14–18.** Confession of Peter, transfiguration, community discourse.
- **19–25.** The journey and the Jerusalem teaching, including the Olivet discourse.
- **26–28.** [Passion](../../../stories/07-passion-week.md), empty tomb, and the Great Commission in Galilee — see [resurrection appearances](../../../stories/08-resurrection-appearances.md).

The five large teaching blocks (5–7, 10, 13, 18, 24–25) are often compared to a new Torah, though the book never says that outright.

## Key passages and ideas

The fulfillment citations ("this happened to fulfill…") are Matthew's signature: Hosea 11:1, Isaiah 7:14, Micah 5:2, and many others are read as pointing to Jesus. This is typological and prophetic reading, not modern historical-critical exegesis, and it should be described as such. The Sermon on the Mount (especially the Beatitudes, the antitheses, the Lord's Prayer, and the demand to love enemies) is the book's moral and theological center of teaching. 16:16–18, Peter's confession and the word about the church and the keys, has been a watershed in later Christian debate about Peter and the papacy; the text should be read first in its own setting.

The Passion is marked by the cry from Psalm 22, the torn curtain, and the unique scenes of Judas's end, the guard at the tomb, and the report that the body was stolen. The risen Jesus' last words (28:18–20) send disciples to all nations, with a trinitarian baptismal formula and a promise of presence "to the end of the age."

Matthew's Jesus is also a teacher of a church that must live after Easter among mixed loyalties. Chapter 18's community discourse (the lost sheep, reproof, binding and loosing, forgiveness "seventy-seven times") is as characteristic as the Sermon on the Mount. The eschatological parables of chapters 24–25 (the faithful servant, the bridesmaids, the talents, the sheep and the goats) tie judgment to ordinary faithfulness and to care for "the least of these." A teaching Gospel is not a softer Gospel. It is a Gospel that expects a people to be formed, and then held to account, in the time between resurrection and the coming of the Son of Man.

## Themes and reception

Themes include fulfillment, Jesus as son of David and son of Abraham, righteousness, the kingdom of heaven, the church, and a mission that finally includes the nations. Matthew became the church's teaching Gospel par excellence. It is also, because of its sharp polemic (chapter 23), a text that requires moral care in teaching after the history of Christian anti-Judaism. Historical-critical work on sources (Mark, Q, M) helps explain its relationship to the other Synoptics without deciding, by itself, the question of truth.
