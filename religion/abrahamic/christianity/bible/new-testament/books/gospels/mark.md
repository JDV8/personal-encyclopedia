# Mark

**Name and meaning.** The Gospel according to Mark. Tradition names John Mark, a companion of Paul and Barnabas and, in the Papias tradition, the interpreter of Peter in Rome. The book itself is anonymous. It is the shortest Gospel, the most urgent in pace ("immediately"), and, on the majority scholarly view, the earliest and a source for Matthew and Luke.

**Prerequisites:** [The four Gospels](../../../02-the-four-gospels.md) and [Second Temple Judaism and Rome](../../../01-second-temple-judaism-and-rome.md).

## Dating and authorship

Papias (early second century, as quoted by Eusebius) says Mark wrote down Peter's preaching, not in order. Irenaeus and others locate this in Rome after Peter's death. Mainstream modern scholarship often dates the Gospel to about 65–75 CE, around the Jewish War and the destruction of the temple (chapter 13 is a key text), and leaves the author's name open while not ruling out a Markan tradition. Some conservative scholars defend an earlier date and Petrine origin. The ending of the book is a special textual problem: the best early manuscripts end at 16:8, with the women fleeing from the tomb in fear and saying nothing to anyone; later longer and shorter endings were added. Honest teaching reports 16:8 as the earliest recoverable ending and the later endings as ancient attempts to close the story.

## Context

Mark's Jesus is the [Son of God](../../../../glossary/son-of-god.md) and the suffering [Son of Man](../../../../glossary/son-of-man.md), whose identity is often hidden (the so-called messianic secret) and is only confessed clearly by a Roman centurion at the cross. Disciples repeatedly fail to understand. The implied audience may be a community under pressure, for whom a crucified Messiah and a demand to take up a cross would not have been theoretical. Galilee is the field of ministry; Jerusalem is the place of conflict and death.

## Outline

- **1:1–13.** The beginning: John, baptism, temptation, with no infancy narrative.
- **1:14–8:21.** Ministry in Galilee: calling, exorcisms and healings, controversy, parables, feedings; growing conflict and incomprehension.
- **8:22–10:52.** The turning point: Peter's confession, three passion predictions, teaching on discipleship, the transfiguration, the road to Jerusalem; two healings of the blind frame the section.
- **11–13.** Entry, temple conflict, [Olivet discourse](../../../stories/06-passion-week.md).
- **14–16.** Passion, death, burial, the empty tomb — and, in the earliest ending, silence and fear.

## Key passages and ideas

The opening line already names "Jesus Christ, the Son of God" (in most manuscripts). The baptismal voice, the unclean spirits, and the transfiguration say the same thing, while human characters are slower. 8:27–38 is the hinge: "You are the Messiah," then the first prediction of suffering, then "if any want to become my followers, let them deny themselves and take up their cross." The temple clearing, the widow's offering, and the prediction that not one stone will be left on another belong to a temple theology under judgment and hope.

16:8 as an ending is austere: a announced resurrection, an empty tomb, a promise that Jesus goes before them to Galilee, and frightened women. Later readers added appearances; [Matthew](matthew.md), [Luke](luke.md), and [John](john.md) also tell appearance stories. Mark's early ending should be allowed to stand in its starkness before it is supplemented.

Mark's style is part of its theology. Sentences pile up with "and" and "immediately"; scenes cut quickly; emotions are raw (Jesus is angry, moved with pity, or distressed). This is not primitiveness. It is a way of making the reader feel the press of the kingdom and the approach of the cross. The two-stage healing of the blind man at Bethsaida (8:22–26), just before Peter's half-seeing confession, is a narrative clue: sight comes in stages, and even insiders may see people "like trees walking." The Gentile spaces of the book (the Decapolis, the Syrophoenician woman, the centurion) quietly widen the field of mercy before any formal mission charge is given. Mark has no Great Commission in the earliest ending; it has a promise that the risen one goes ahead, and a dare to follow him to Galilee.

## Themes and reception

Themes include secrecy and revelation, the cost of discipleship, the failure of insiders, the power of Jesus over chaos and demons, and a Messiah who is recognized in suffering. Mark was relatively neglected in liturgy compared with Matthew and John, then rediscovered in modern criticism as a theological narrative, not a crude first draft. The Synoptic Problem (Markan priority versus other solutions, including a minority who still prefer Matthean priority) belongs in any serious introduction.
