# John

**Name and meaning.** The Gospel according to John, often called the Fourth Gospel. Tradition names John son of Zebedee, the beloved disciple. The book itself is anonymous and refers to a "disciple whom Jesus loved" as a witness standing behind the testimony (19:35; 21:24). It is a very different Gospel from the Synoptics: a prologue about the Word, long discourses, "I am" sayings, a ministry structured around festivals and signs, and a high, explicit Christology.

## Dating and authorship

Irenaeus and others in the second century attribute the Gospel to John the apostle, writing from Ephesus in old age. Mainstream modern scholarship often dates it to about 90–100 CE and speaks of a Johannine community or school, with possible stages of composition (a signs source, discourse material, a later ending in chapter 21). Some still defend apostolic authorship and an earlier date. The relationship to the Synoptics is debated: independence, loose knowledge, or deliberate recasting. The book should not be forced into Synoptic chronology; it has its own (including a longer Judean ministry and a different placement of the temple incident).

## Context

John's Jesus moves between Galilee and Jerusalem, often at Jewish festivals (Passover, Tabernacles, Dedication). The phrase "the Jews" in this Gospel sometimes means the hostile authorities, sometimes the people, and has had a tragic afterlife in Christian anti-Judaism. Teaching must say this clearly: the book is a late first-century intra-Jewish and Jewish-Christian conflict text, not a license for hatred. The community behind it may already have experienced expulsion or alienation from the synagogue (see 9:22; 16:2).

## Outline

- **1:1–18.** The prologue: the Word, life, light, incarnation, "we have seen his glory."
- **1:19–12.** The book of signs: John the Baptist, first disciples, Cana, Nicodemus, the Samaritan woman, healings, the bread of life, Tabernacles and the light of the world, the man born blind, the good shepherd, the raising of Lazarus, the entry, the hour that has come.
- **13–17.** The book of glory, in a last supper setting: foot-washing, the new commandment, the farewell discourses, the promise of the Paraclete, the prayer of chapter 17.
- **18–19.** Arrest, Jewish and Roman hearings, crucifixion, death, burial — with distinctive details (the "I am" in the garden, the dialogue with Pilate about truth and kingship, the mother and the beloved disciple, the pierced side).
- **20–21.** Empty tomb; appearances to Mary Magdalene, the disciples, and Thomas; a later Galilee appearance, a catch of fish, Peter's restoration, and a closing word about the beloved disciple.

## Key passages and ideas

The prologue is one of the most influential paragraphs ever written about Jesus: the Word was with God and was God, became flesh, and pitched a tent among us. This is not a denial of Jewish monotheism in the author's mind; it is a daring claim about the one God. The signs (water to wine, the official's son, the lame man, the feeding, walking on the sea, the blind man, Lazarus) are meant to be read, not only gaped at: "these are written so that you may believe" (20:31). The "I am" sayings (bread, light, door, shepherd, resurrection and life, way and truth and life, vine) and the absolute "I am" of 8:58 place Jesus within the name and identity of the God of Exodus.

Nicodemus and new birth, the Samaritan woman's living water, and Thomas's "my Lord and my God" are character-studies in coming to faith. The crucifixion is "the hour" of glorification, not only of shame: in John, Jesus is lifted up, and he hands over his spirit when the work is finished.

Chapter 21, which many scholars read as an epilogue added to a book that could have ended at 20:31, does important pastoral work. Peter, who has denied Jesus, is restored with a threefold "do you love me?" and is told to feed the sheep, and then told by what death he will glorify God. The beloved disciple remains, and a rumor about his not dying is corrected. The last verses of the Gospel admit that the world itself could not contain all the books that might be written. John is therefore both a highly selective "spiritual Gospel" and a book that refuses to pretend it has said everything. Its purpose is not complete reportage. It is faith that Jesus is the Messiah, the Son of God, and life in his name.

## Themes and reception

Themes include incarnation, glory, truth, witness, remaining (abiding), the Spirit-Paraclete, love within the community, and a dualism of light and darkness that must be handled with care. John's Gospel has shaped Christian theology more than any other, from the creeds to modern debates about history and interpretation (the "spiritual Gospel," already so called in antiquity). It should be read both with the Synoptics and as itself.
