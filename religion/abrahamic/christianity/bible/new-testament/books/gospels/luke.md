# Luke

**Name and meaning.** The Gospel according to Luke, the first volume of a two-part work that continues in [Acts](../history/acts.md). Tradition names Luke the beloved physician, a Gentile companion of Paul (Colossians 4:14). The book itself is anonymous, addressed to "Theophilus," and claims to offer an orderly account after investigation of earlier traditions. It is the Gospel most interested in history as a sequence, in the poor and the marginalized, in prayer, and in the Holy Spirit.

**Prerequisites:** [The four Gospels](../../../02-the-four-gospels.md) and [Second Temple Judaism and Rome](../../../01-second-temple-judaism-and-rome.md). This overview should be read with [Acts](../history/acts.md).

## Dating and authorship

The traditional identification with Paul's companion is early (the Muratorian Fragment, Irenaeus, and others) and is supported, for many, by the "we" passages in Acts. Mainstream modern scholarship often dates Luke–Acts to about 80–90 CE (some later, some earlier), after Mark, and treats the author as a highly literate Greek writer of the second Christian generation, possibly but not certainly the historical Luke. He used Mark, the sayings tradition (Q), and a large body of special material (L), including many of the best-loved parables. The preface (1:1–4) is the most explicit statement of method in the Gospels.

## Context

Luke places Jesus in the frame of world and Jewish history (the emperors, the governors, the high priesthood). The story begins and ends in the temple, and it spends its first two chapters in the world of pious Jews: Zechariah, Elizabeth, Mary, Simeon, Anna. The Gospel then follows Jesus through Galilee and, in a long travel narrative (9:51–19:27), toward Jerusalem, where he is taken up, and from which the second volume will send witnesses "to the end of the earth."

## Outline

- **1–2.** Birth of John and of Jesus; canticles (Magnificat, Benedictus, Gloria, Nunc dimittis); the boy in the temple — see [birth narratives](../../../stories/01-birth-narratives.md).
- **3–4.** John's preaching; genealogy back to Adam; baptism, temptation, and the Nazareth sermon.
- **5–9.** Galilean ministry: call of the disciples, sermons, [miracles](../../../stories/04-miracles.md), the sending of the Twelve, Peter's confession, transfiguration.
- **9:51–19.** The journey to Jerusalem: the Samaritan villages, the seventy, many [parables](../../../stories/05-parables.md) unique to Luke (the good Samaritan, the prodigal son, the rich man and Lazarus, the Pharisee and the tax collector).
- **19–24.** Entry, temple teaching, [Passion](../../../stories/06-passion-week.md), crucifixion (with words unique to Luke: "Father, forgive them"; "today you will be with me in Paradise"; "into your hands I commend my spirit"), the Emmaus road, appearances in Jerusalem, and blessing at Bethany — see [resurrection appearances](../../../stories/07-resurrection-appearances.md).

## Key passages and ideas

The infancy canticles are theology in song: God brings down the powerful and lifts up the lowly; the child is a light for the nations and glory for Israel. The Nazareth sermon (4:16–30), using Isaiah 61, announces good news to the poor and already hints at Gentile inclusion (Elijah and Elisha's outsiders), which nearly gets Jesus killed. The "today" of salvation is a Lukan refrain (4:21; 19:9; 23:43).

Luke's Passion is less desolate than Mark's: Jesus remains composed, heals the slave's ear, speaks to the weeping women, and dies with a psalm of trust. The Emmaus story (chapter 24) is a first-principles lesson in Christian reading of scripture: the risen one interprets Moses and the prophets, and is known in the breaking of bread.

Luke's special material also includes a sharper warning about wealth than many casual readers remember. The woes that follow his Beatitudes, the parable of the rich fool, the dishonest manager, and the rich man and Lazarus are not asides. They belong to a Gospel that sings of the lowly being lifted up and then asks whether the church will live as if that song were true. The same author will, in Acts, describe goods held in common in Jerusalem. Whether one takes those scenes as historical report, as ideal type, or as both, Luke–Acts is interested in money, status, and the reversal of both. Theophilus, if he was a real patron of some rank, was being taught as well as informed.

## Themes and reception

Themes include the poor, women as witnesses and patrons, prayer and the Spirit, joy, repentance, table fellowship, and a salvation that is "for all people" without cancelling Israel's story. Luke has been called the Gospel of the poor and of the Spirit. Its historical reliability in details of official titles is often praised; its relationship to Paul's letters (the theology of Acts is not simply a copy of the epistles) is a standard scholarly debate. Read it as volume one.
