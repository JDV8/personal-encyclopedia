# Key stories and parables

**Prerequisites:** [The four Gospels](02-the-four-gospels.md)
**Key ideas:** infancy, baptism, and announcement of the kingdom; parables as a way of teaching; conflict in Jerusalem; last supper, execution, and resurrection; the four gospels do not tell every scene the same way.

This lesson is a map of central episodes, not a gospel harmony. Where the sources differ, the difference is noted rather than silently blended.

## Birth, baptism, and the kingdom

Only **Matthew** and **Luke** tell infancy stories, and they are not the same story. Matthew has wise men, a star, Herod's violence, and a flight into Egypt, with constant scripture fulfillment. Luke has an annunciation to Mary, a census, a manger, and shepherds; his volume also pairs Jesus' birth with John the Baptist's. Mark and John begin without a nativity: Mark at the Jordan, John at the eternal Word.

All four, in their own ways, attach Jesus to **John the Baptist**. Jesus is baptized; a voice from heaven names him (with wording that varies); in the Synoptics he is tested in the wilderness. Then he announces the **kingdom of God** (Matthew often says "heaven"): not first a place one goes after death, but God's reign breaking in, with healings and exorcisms as signs. He calls disciples, eats with the wrong people, touches the unclean, and argues about sabbath and purity — disputes that only make sense in the [Second Temple](01-second-temple-judaism-and-rome.md) world of law-observant Judaism.

## Parables and teaching

A **parable** is a short narrative or extended image that teases the hearer into judgment, often about the kingdom. Mark says that parables both reveal and conceal. Among the most widely known:

- The **Good Samaritan** (Luke): a wounded man is ignored by a priest and a Levite and helped by a Samaritan, a figure many of Jesus' hearers would have distrusted. The question "who is my neighbor?" is turned back on the questioner.
- The **Prodigal Son** (Luke): a younger son wastes his share, returns, and is welcomed by the father, while an older son resents the mercy. It is a story about lostness and joy, and about the cost of welcome.
- The **sower**, the **mustard seed**, the **leaven**, the **lost sheep**, the **workers in the vineyard**, the **ten bridesmaids**: the kingdom as seed, surprise, generosity that offends, and a demand to be ready.

The **Sermon on the Mount** (Matthew 5–7; a related "Sermon on the Plain" in Luke) is not a parable but a block of teaching: blessing on the unlikely, a radicalizing of the law ("you have heard… but I say"), a call to love enemies, a warning against showy piety, and the demand to do the words, not only hear them. Traditional Christian ethics has returned to this sermon constantly; how literally to take "turn the other cheek" or "do not swear" has been an argument of its own.

## Jerusalem, supper, death, and resurrection

The Synoptics structure a last, fatal week in Jerusalem around Passover (John's calendar of the death relative to Passover is a famous discrepancy). Jesus enters the city in a royal-humble procession, disrupts the temple market, and is drawn into conflict with the authorities. At a last meal with the disciples he interprets bread and wine in relation to his body and a covenant (the exact wording varies); in John, he washes their feet and speaks a long farewell. Judas hands him over. He is arrested, examined before Jewish authorities (the details differ), brought to Pilate, and **crucified** under a titulus that names him "king of the Jews." The mode of death is Roman; the charge is, in part, political.

The burial is hurried. On the first day of the week, women (Mary Magdalene foremost, with others depending on the gospel) find the tomb empty. Appearances follow, in different orders and locations: to Mary, to disciples in Jerusalem, to two on the road to Emmaus (Luke), in Galilee (Matthew and John), and, in John, a lakeside meal and a restoration of Peter. As noted earlier, Mark's ending is textually complicated.

Traditional Christian faith takes these events, especially the resurrection, as historical and world-changing: God has vindicated Jesus. Historical scholarship generally agrees that Jesus was a first-century Jew baptized by John, that he taught and was crucified under Pilate, and that his earliest followers claimed to have seen him afterward; it debates how to describe those claims, how far particular sayings and scenes go back to Jesus himself, and how much is later theological shaping. A student can learn the shape of the story, and the shape of that debate, without rushing either.
