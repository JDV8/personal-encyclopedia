# Acts

**Prerequisites:** [The four Gospels](02-the-four-gospels.md), especially Luke, and [key stories](03-key-stories-and-parables.md)
**Key ideas:** Luke's second volume; the Spirit and a movement from Jerusalem outward; Peter, Stephen, and the inclusion of Gentiles; Paul's journeys and the road to Rome; history and theology braided together.

The **Acts of the Apostles** opens by addressing Theophilus again and by taking up where Luke's gospel ended: the risen Jesus is with the disciples for forty days, promises the Holy Spirit, and is taken up. The book is not a complete history of all apostles (James the son of Zebedee dies early; most of the Twelve recede). It is a selective, theological history of how a Jewish messianic sect in Jerusalem became a movement that also included Gentiles and reached the heart of the empire.

## Jerusalem and the Spirit

At **Pentecost**, during a Jewish pilgrimage festival, the Spirit comes with wind and fire, and the disciples speak in ways that a multilingual crowd can hear. Peter preaches that the crucified Jesus has been raised and made Lord and Messiah, and that the promise is for Israel and those far off. Thousands are baptized. The early community in Jerusalem is described as sharing goods, praying in the temple, and holding "the apostles' teaching and fellowship, the breaking of bread and the prayers." Conflict appears immediately: a couple, Ananias and Sapphira, lie about a gift and die; Hellenistic and Hebrew widows raise a dispute about daily distribution; seven are appointed, including **Stephen**.

Stephen's long speech retells Israel's story as a history of resisting the Spirit, and he is stoned — the first martyr in Christian memory. Saul of Tarsus appears as a consenting witness. A persecution scatters many (not, Acts says, the apostles). Philip preaches in Samaria and to an Ethiopian official. The movement is already moving.

## Peter, Paul, and the Gentile question

**Peter** heals, preaches, and, in a carefully told episode, has a vision of unclean animals and is summoned to the house of Cornelius, a Roman centurion. The Spirit falls on Gentiles; Peter baptizes them. The shock is theological: God, it is claimed, has granted to Gentiles a repentance unto life without first making them Jews in the ordinary way.

**Saul** meanwhile is confronted on the road to Damascus by the risen Jesus, blinded, and then baptized. He becomes Paul, a preacher first in synagogues and then more and more to Gentiles. Barnabas brings him into the Antioch church, where the disciples are first called "Christians." A famine relief visit, a missionary journey through Cyprus and Asia Minor, and a crisis over whether Gentile believers must be circumcised lead to the **Jerusalem council** (Acts 15). The decision, as Acts presents it, is that Gentiles need not take on the full yoke of the Mosaic law, but should observe a few restrictions. The historical relationship between this scene and Paul's own account in Galatians is a classic problem of sources: they are not simply identical photographs of one meeting.

## To Rome

Paul travels, argues, is beaten, founds communities, and is finally arrested in Jerusalem after a riot over the accusation that he brought a Gentile into the temple. As a Roman citizen he appeals to Caesar. The last third of the book is travel, trial, shipwreck, and arrival in Rome, where Paul rents a house and speaks "without hindrance" — a last word that is almost certainly deliberate. We are not told of his death.

Traditional authorship identifies the writer with Luke the physician, a companion of Paul, and notes "we" passages as eyewitness traces. Many scholars agree that Acts and the Gospel of Luke share an author, date the work late in the first century, and treat the "we" as a convention or a source; they also note that Acts' Paul and the Paul of the letters do not always match in tone or in the order of events. Acts is our only narrative of this generation on this scale, and it is a narrative with a purpose: the Spirit drives a witness "in Jerusalem, in all Judea and Samaria, and to the end of the earth." Rome, in that geography, is not a defeat. It is a sign of how far the word has gone.
