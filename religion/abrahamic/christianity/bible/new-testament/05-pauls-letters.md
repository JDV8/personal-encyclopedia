# Paul's letters

**Prerequisites:** [Acts](04-acts.md) and [genres](../04-genres.md)
**Key ideas:** letters to real communities, earlier than the written Gospels; undisputed and disputed letters; justification, grace, law, and the crucified Messiah; already and not yet; a Jewish apostle to the Gentiles.

The earliest Christian documents we have are probably not the Gospels but **Paul's letters**, commonly dated from the late 40s through the 50s CE. They are occasional: they answer questions, quarrels, and crises in particular churches. To read a verse without its occasion is to risk turning a letter into a set of slogans.

## Which letters?

Seven letters are almost universally accepted as by Paul: **Romans, 1 and 2 Corinthians, Galatians, Philippians, 1 Thessalonians, and Philemon**. Others written in his name — 2 Thessalonians, Colossians, Ephesians, and the Pastoral Epistles (1–2 Timothy, Titus) — are disputed. Traditional reading takes the headings as sufficient: Paul wrote them. Many scholars argue that some are later works in a Pauline tradition, on grounds of vocabulary, church structure, and theology. Hebrews was sometimes associated with Paul in the West and is not in his voice; almost no modern scholar treats it as his. This lesson takes the seven undisputed letters as the secure core, and treats the others as part of the Pauline heritage without forcing a decision in every sentence.

## The argument in outline

Paul is a Jew, a former persecutor, who becomes convinced that the crucified Jesus has been raised and that this event changes the situation of Israel and the nations. The **cross** is not an embarrassment to be hurried past; it is, he says, the power and wisdom of God, a scandal to Jews and foolishness to Gentiles. A Messiah who is hanged is already a shock inside Jewish expectation; Paul makes that shock the center.

**Justification** (being set right, *dikaiosyne*, a word that also means justice and righteousness) is, especially in Galatians and Romans, not achieved by "works of the law" as a badge or a ladder. It is God's doing, received by faith — and the meaning of that phrase (faith in Christ, or the faithfulness of Christ, or both) is itself a live scholarly debate. **Grace** is the name for this initiative. Abraham, who trusted God's promise before Sinai, becomes a prototype of this trust, so that Gentiles can be included as heirs without first becoming Jews. The law, in this argument, is holy and yet not the last word; it had a role, and in Christ something new has arrived.

This is not, in Paul's own terms, a rejection of Israel. Romans 9–11 is a long, pained, hopeful argument that God has not abandoned the people he chose, and that the mystery includes a future for Israel alongside the incoming of the nations. Later Christian anti-Judaism often failed to stay with that complexity.

## Churches, ethics, and time

The letters are also messy and practical. Corinth is split by factions, status, sexual ethics, lawsuits, and chaotic worship; Paul answers with a theology of the body (the community as a body; bodies as members of Christ) and with the famous hymn to love. Galatia is, in his view, in danger of abandoning the gospel for a circumcision requirement. Philippi receives a letter of affection that includes a hymn-like passage about Christ emptying himself. Philemon is a short, tact-laden appeal about an enslaved man, Onesimus — a text that later readers have used both to soften and, unhappily, to leave slavery in place.

Paul's **ethics** is eschatological: time is short; the community already lives in the new creation, and not yet in its completion. Sexual conduct, money, and the use of spiritual gifts are measured by that horizon and by the good of the body. He can be sharp, occasionally opaque, and, on some social hierarchies, a man of his age. He can also write that in Christ there is neither Jew nor Greek, slave nor free, male nor female — a sentence whose force later generations have tried, in various directions, to live up to.

Acts gives you a narrative of Paul's roads. The letters give you his mind under pressure. Read them as letters, and as the first extended Christian theology we have.
