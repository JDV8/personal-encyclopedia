# 2 Esdras

**Name and meaning.** A composite work, also called 4 Ezra in the core Jewish apocalypse (chapters 3–14), with a later Christian beginning (5 Ezra, chapters 1–2) and ending (6 Ezra, chapters 15–16) in the Latin tradition. The central apocalypse is one of the most important Jewish theological works of the late first century CE: seven visions in which Ezra questions the justice of God after a destruction of Zion, and is answered with a mixture of rebuke, consolation, and secrets of the end. Some Orthodox traditions print the book; it is not in the Catholic deuterocanon, but it is in many Apocrypha collections and was influential in both Jewish and Christian thought.

## Dating and authorship

The Ezra of history lived in the Persian period. 4 Ezra (chs. 3–14) is almost always dated around 100 CE, after the destruction of the Second Temple in 70, using the "thirtieth year after the destruction of the city" and a Babylonian setting as typological cover (as Baruch and others also do). The author is an unknown Jewish theologian of high power. The Christian frame chapters are later. The book's Latin (and other versions: Syriac, Ethiopic, Armenian, Arabic) is a translation of a lost Greek, itself probably from a Hebrew or Aramaic original for the core.

## Context

The crisis is theodicy after catastrophe: if God is just and Israel is chosen, how can Zion be given into the hands of a godless empire? Ezra will not be easily comforted. The angel Uriel, and later God, answer with the limits of human understanding, with a coming end, with a messianic figure, and with a charge to Ezra to write many books (ninety-four, in the famous last vision), some public and some reserved for the wise.

## Outline

- **1–2** (5 Ezra). A Christian prophetic opening, often more like a church sermon, accusing Israel and turning to a new people. Not part of the original Jewish apocalypse.
- **3–14** (4 Ezra). Seven episodes: (1) the first dialogue on injustice; (2) a second dialogue; (3) a third, and a vision of a mourning woman who becomes a built city (Zion); (4) the man from the sea, a messianic figure; (5) the eagle vision (a critique of empire, usually Rome); (6) the writing of the books; (7) last instructions.
- **15–16** (6 Ezra). Christian oracles of woe against the nations, added later.

## Key passages and ideas

Ezra's questions are as important as the answers: they belong with Job and with the psalms of lament, now after 70 CE. The woman-who-is-the-city (chapters 9–10) is a great image of grief turning into a revealed Zion. The eagle vision is political theology in coded beasts, in the family of Daniel 7. The last vision's many books is a theory of scripture and of reserved wisdom. 7:26–44 and related passages include a messiah, a four-hundred-year (or similar) messianic reign in some calculations, death, and a last judgment — an eschatology that should be compared, not harmonized, with Revelation and with later rabbinic and Christian schemes.

Because of the Christian frame, printed editions can give a false impression that the whole is a church book. A first-principles reading isolates 4 Ezra first, then notes the later Christian envelope.

## Themes and reception

Themes include theodicy, law, a remnant, messianic hope, empire, and the making of books. 4 Ezra is a cousin of 2 Baruch and of other post-70 apocalypses. It influenced later Christian thought (and was itself framed by Christians) while remaining, at its core, a Jewish argument with God after the temple's second fall. It is one of the most rewarding "extra" books a serious student of the Bible can read, whether or not it is in their canon.

The numbering (2 Esdras, 4 Ezra, 5–6 Ezra) should be checked whenever a footnote cites "Esdras." This file means the Latin 2 Esdras in the Apocrypha, with the Jewish apocalypse at its heart.
