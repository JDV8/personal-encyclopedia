# Letter of Jeremiah

**Name and meaning.** A short tract, in the form of a letter from Jeremiah to exiles being taken to Babylon, warning them at length not to fear or worship the idols they will see there. In the Septuagint it often stands on its own; in the Vulgate and in many Catholic Bibles it is [Baruch](baruch.md) chapter 6. It is a single, focused argument.

**Prerequisites:** [Jeremiah](../old-testament/books/major-prophets/jeremiah.md) 10 and 29, and [deuterocanon](README.md).

## Dating and authorship

The historical Jeremiah did write a letter to the exiles (Jeremiah 29), about building houses and seeking the city's peace. This later work uses that idea for a different purpose: a sustained expose of idol-making. It was probably written in Hebrew or Aramaic and is preserved in Greek, commonly dated in the Hellenistic period (sometimes earlier, even the late Persian period). A fragment related to the work (or to the same tradition) has been discussed in connection with Qumran. Traditional ascription followed the heading; critical study treats it as pseudepigraphic pedagogy.

## Context

Jews living among impressive cult statues, processions, and temple wealth needed a way to see those images as made things: they tarnish, they have dust wiped from their faces, they cannot save a person from a fire or a thief. The letter is satirical and repetitive on purpose. Its nearest biblical kin are Jeremiah 10, Isaiah 44, and the idol sections of [Wisdom of Solomon](wisdom-of-solomon.md).

## Outline

After a heading that names Jeremiah and the seventy-years frame, the work is a sequence of paragraphs, often ending with a refrain along the lines of "from this you will know they are not gods; do not fear them." The images are taken down, dressed, carried, locked up, and shown to be helpless. A closing comparison says that the righteous person who has no idols is better than the wealthy worshiper of them.

## Key passages and ideas

The letter's method is accumulation of ordinary details: priests selling off offerings, women laying out meals for gods who cannot eat, bats and swallows on the statues, stolen gold. The argument is not metaphysical first. It is empirical and moral. The refrain trains the reader to look, not to be awed. Verse numbering differs by edition because of the Baruch-chapter-6 tradition; use a labeled text.

Theologically, the work assumes that idolatry is the great diaspora temptation and that a letter from Jeremiah is the right authority to name it. It is less interested in the inner life of the exiles than [Baruch](baruch.md) is, and more interested in unmasking a public cult.

Because the work is repetitive, the right way to read it is not to hunt for a new idea in every paragraph but to notice the pedagogy of the refrain. The exiles are being trained to look twice at what impresses the city. In that sense the letter is closer to a catechism than to a prophecy in the classical sense, and its placement beside Baruch makes a kind of curriculum: confess, then learn not to fear the images in the street.

## Themes and reception

Themes include idolatry, diaspora, satire, and the authority of Jeremiah's name. The work is canonical in Catholic and Orthodox Bibles, usually attached to Baruch in Western editions. Protestant and Jewish canons do not include it, but its argument is fully at home in the Hebrew prophets. A first-principles reader should not skip it because it is short or repetitive; the repetition is the pedagogy.

Read it next to Jeremiah 10 and to the Wisdom of Solomon's idol critique, and notice what each one uniquely contributes: prophetic taunt, philosophical analysis, and this letter's almost documentary eye for cultic practice.

The "seventy years" heading ties it to Jeremiah's timetable, already being reused as typological time in Second Temple literature (see also [Daniel](../old-testament/books/major-prophets/daniel.md) 9). The letter is therefore part of the afterlife of Jeremiah as well as a tract about statues.
