**Name and meaning.** Also called the Wisdom of Jesus son of Sirach, or Ecclesiasticus ("the church's book," a Latin nickname that marks its heavy use in Christian lectionaries). It is the largest wisdom book in the deuterocanon: a collection of instruction, poems, and a long "praise of the ancestors," written in Hebrew in Jerusalem in the early second century BCE and translated into Greek by the author's grandson in Egypt.

**Prerequisites:** [Proverbs](../old-testament/books/wisdom-poetry/proverbs.md) and [deuterocanon](README.md).

## Dating and authorship

The book is unusually forthcoming. The grandson's prologue dates his arrival in Egypt to the thirty-eighth year of Euergetes (probably 132 BCE) and says his grandfather Jesus (Hebrew *Yeshua*) son of Eleazar son of Sira wrote the book. The Hebrew original is commonly placed around 190–175 BCE, just before the Maccabean crisis, which the book does not seem to know as a completed event. Large portions of the Hebrew have been recovered from the Cairo Genizah, Masada, and Qumran, so this is not a "Greek-only" work. There are differences among Hebrew and Greek forms.

## Context

Ben Sira is a scribe-teacher in a Jerusalem still under Ptolemaic or early Seleucid influence, offering a conservative, temple-centered wisdom: fear of the Lord, honor of priests, care in speech and friendship, a cautious view of women and of daughters that modern readers will find patriarchal, and a reluctance to speculate about hidden things. He writes as the last of a line, gathering Israel's wisdom before the crisis of Antiochus IV.

## Outline

- **Prologue** (the grandson).
- **1–23.** Instruction: fear of the Lord, humility, friendship, wives, children, speech, table manners, almsgiving, and many other topics, interwoven with poems on Wisdom.
- **24.** Wisdom's speech: she comes from the mouth of the Most High, tours creation, and takes up her dwelling in Israel, identified with the Torah in Zion.
- **25–43.** Further instruction, including medicine, mourning, and a hymn to the works of creation.
- **44–50.** "Let us now praise famous men": from Enoch and Noah through the patriarchs, Moses, Aaron, David, and the prophets, climaxing in the high priest Simon (usually Simon II).
- **51.** A closing prayer and an acrostic poem on the search for Wisdom.

## Key passages and ideas

Chapter 24 is the theological peak: Wisdom is identified with the book of the covenant, the law of Moses. That identification is a major Second Temple move and a background for later Christian and Jewish debates about Wisdom, Torah, and Logos. The praise of the ancestors is a first-principles history of Israel as a gallery of models, ending in a living high priest, and it is one of the sources of later "communion of saints" imagination, though that doctrine is later. 38:1–15 honors the physician, a notable moment in a tradition sometimes caricatured as anti-medical. 15:11–20, on human choice and the origin of sin, is a careful piece of moral theology.

The book's sentences about women (and about slaves) have to be taught as they are, as evidence of an ancient household ethic, not as a timeless charter. The same book is generous about friendship and about the poor.

## Themes and reception

Themes include the fear of the Lord, Torah-Wisdom, scribal pedagogy, temple and priesthood, and memory of the ancestors. Sirach was widely used in synagogues in some periods and then not canonized in Judaism; the rabbis still quote Ben Sira on occasion. The church used it so heavily that "Ecclesiasticus" stuck. Hebrew recovery in the modern period has changed scholarly work on the book. It is the best single window on educated Jerusalem Judaism a generation before the Maccabees.
