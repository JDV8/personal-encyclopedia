# 1 Esdras

**Name and meaning.** Also called 3 Ezra in some Latin numbering (the names of the Ezra books are a famous tangle). 1 Esdras is a Greek retelling of material from 2 Chronicles 35–36, Ezra, and a bit of Nehemiah, plus one major unique story: a contest of three young bodyguards at Darius's court, won by Zerubbabel with a speech about truth. In the Eastern Orthodox canon it stands as scripture; in Protestant Apocrypha it is printed but not, in Catholic Bibles, part of the typical deuterocanon.

**Prerequisites:** [Ezra](../old-testament/books/history/ezra.md), [Nehemiah](../old-testament/books/history/nehemiah.md), [2 Chronicles](../old-testament/books/history/2-chronicles.md), and [deuterocanon](README.md).

## Dating and authorship

Anonymous, composed in Greek or translated from a Semitic original, probably in the second or first century BCE (dates vary). It is not simply a sloppy copy of the canonical Ezra. The author rearranges, omits some lists, and inserts the bodyguard tale, producing a more continuous story from Josiah's Passover to the reading of the law. Josephus used a form of this narrative, which is one reason it matters historically.

## Context

The book belongs to the same restoration horizon as Ezra–Nehemiah: return, temple, and the legitimacy of the Second Temple community. The unique tale (often 3:1–5:6) is a court story in the Daniel–Ahiqar family: three men debate what is strongest — wine, the king, or women — and the third, identified with Zerubbabel, adds that truth is strongest of all. The prize is permission to rebuild. The story is a legendary charter for the same work the canonical Ezra grounds in imperial decrees.

## Outline

- Josiah's Passover and death; the last kings; the exile (from 2 Chronicles).
- The first return and the interruption of the building (from Ezra, with differences).
- The contest of the three bodyguards and Zerubbabel's speech on truth; Darius's support.
- The return and the temple finished; opposition and completion.
- Ezra's arrival and the reading of the law (a form of Nehemiah 8 is included here, which is one of the book's most interesting rearrangements).

## Key passages and ideas

The speech on truth ("Great is truth, and strongest of all," in a famous later Latin form, *magna est veritas et praevalet*) had a long afterlife as a motto, sometimes detached from Zerubbabel. In the book it is a piece of court wisdom that turns into political permission for the temple. The rearrangement that places the law-reading in Ezra's story, not Nehemiah's, is a first-principles clue that the restoration books circulated in more than one edition.

Because the book overlaps so much with material already in the Protestant and Catholic Old Testament, readers sometimes skip it. That is a mistake if one wants to see how Second Temple writers rewrote their own history. Comparison with Ezra–Nehemiah, column by column, is the right exercise.

Josephus's use of this form of the story is a reminder that, for a first-century Jewish historian writing in Greek, 1 Esdras could be *the* restoration narrative. Canonical decisions came after, and they did not all go the same way. When a modern reader meets two slightly different Ezras, the task is not to decide which one is "the real" history first, but to learn how a community could tell the same return more than once, with truth as a slogan in one version and a firman in the other.

## Themes and reception

Themes include restoration, temple, truth, and the authority of Persian kings as unexpected helpers. The Orthodox churches keep it as scripture. Jerome and later Western tradition generally set it aside as a duplicate, which is why it is absent from the Catholic deuterocanon proper. It remains one of the more important "almost Ezra" books for historians of the text.

The numbering muddle (1 Esdras, 2 Esdras, Ezra, Nehemiah, 3 Ezra, 4 Ezra) should be checked in a table when using older citations. This file always means the Greek parallel history with the bodyguard story, not the apocalypse treated here as [2 Esdras](2-esdras.md).
