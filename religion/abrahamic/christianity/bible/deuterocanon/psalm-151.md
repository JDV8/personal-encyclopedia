# Psalm 151

**Name and meaning.** A short psalm, extant in Greek in many Septuagint manuscripts as a supernumerary psalm after the 150, and now also known in a Hebrew form from the Great Psalms Scroll at Qumran (11QPs-a), where related Davidic material appears. It is a first-person poem in which David, the youngest, is taken from the flocks, anointed, and sent against Goliath. Eastern Orthodox Bibles include it; the Catholic deuterocanon typically does not, and Protestant Psalters stop at 150.

**Prerequisites:** [Psalms](../old-testament/books/wisdom-poetry/psalms.md), [1 Samuel](../old-testament/books/history/1-samuel.md) 16–17, and [deuterocanon](README.md).

## Dating and authorship

The psalm is attributed to David in its heading, like many psalms. The Qumran Hebrew (sometimes discussed as two short poems later joined in the Greek) shows that the tradition is Jewish and pre-Christian, not a late Greek invention. Dates are usually Hellenistic, as a poetic midrash on the Samuel story. Traditional Orthodox use follows the Septuagint heading.

## Context

The poem belongs with other Second Temple Davidic psalms and with the tendency, already visible in the biblical Psalter's superscriptions, to tie poems to scenes in David's life. It is both a praise of the unexpected choice of the youngest and a brief victory song over "the Philistine." The Qumran scroll's different shape is a first-principles lesson in how the Psalter itself was not frozen in a single official form in the late Second Temple period.

## Outline

The Greek psalm is very short (about seven verses):

- A heading: "This psalm is ascribed to David as his own composition (though it is outside the number), after he had fought with Goliath."
- David, youngest of his brothers, a shepherd and a player of the harp.
- The Lord did not choose the tall brothers but took David from the sheep.
- The Lord sent his angel (or messenger), anointed him, and sent him against the Philistine, who cursed, and who was beheaded with his own sword.

## Key passages and ideas

The line about being "outside the number" is already a canonical comment in the heading: the scribes knew this psalm was extra. The Qumran form has sometimes been read as two units (one about the brothers and the anointing, one about Goliath), which would explain a slight awkwardness in the Greek join. Either way, the theology is that of 1 Samuel 16: the Lord sees otherwise than humans see, and the musician-shepherd becomes the champion.

For a student of the Psalter, the main point is not to inflate a short poem. It is to notice that "150 psalms" is a decision, a beautiful and early one, and not the only way Jewish tradition remembered David as a poet.

## Themes and reception

Themes include election of the youngest, anointing, and victory. The Orthodox liturgy and Psalter keep the poem; Western Bibles generally print it, if at all, in an appendix or in notes on the Dead Sea Scrolls. The Qumran discovery made it newly important for scholarly work on the shape of the Psalter. Read it after 1 Samuel 16–17 and after a walk through the five books of Psalms, as a small reminder that collections have edges, and that edges sometimes still sing.

Because it is so short, a 600-word overview has to spend time on canon and manuscript history. That is appropriate. Psalm 151 is as much a fact about the Bible's borders as it is a poem.

The heading's honesty ("outside the number") is a model for this whole [deuterocanon](README.md) folder: communities have long known that some beloved or inherited writings stand beside the main list, and they have not always agreed on what to do with them.
