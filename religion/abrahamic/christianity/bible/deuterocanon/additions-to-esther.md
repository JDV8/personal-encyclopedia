# Additions to Esther

**Name and meaning.** Six substantial passages found in the Greek Esther (and in Catholic and Orthodox Bibles) but not in the Hebrew book of [Esther](../old-testament/books/history/esther.md). They include a dream of Mordecai, edicts, and prayers, and they name God explicitly, which the Hebrew book never does. In many editions they are lettered A–F, or placed at the points in the story they comment on.

**Prerequisites:** [Esther](../old-testament/books/history/esther.md) and [deuterocanon](README.md).

## Dating and authorship

The additions were composed in Greek (most of them; some argue for a Semitic original behind one or two) in the Hellenistic period, probably the second or first century BCE, to accompany or expand a Greek translation of Esther. A colophon in the Greek book (Addition F) dates a bringing of "the letter of Purim" to Egypt in a year that has been variously calculated (often late second or early first century BCE). The additions are therefore a Second Temple rereading of an already existing story, not the original form of the Hebrew book.

## Context

Greek-speaking Jews, especially in Egypt, received a tale in which God is never named and in which a Jewish queen of Persia wins survival through court courage. The additions supply what some readers felt was missing: theology spoken out loud, a heavenly dream that frames the plot, the inner prayers of Mordecai and Esther, and royal edicts that mention the God of Israel. Whether one judges that an improvement or a loss of the Hebrew book's subtlety, it is a clear act of interpretation.

## Outline

The usual six pieces, in the order of the Greek narrative (not the lettered order of some Latin Bibles):

- **Mordecai's dream** of two dragons and a river, and his discovery of a plot (often at the beginning).
- **The first edict** of Haman, in officialese, authorizing destruction.
- **Mordecai's prayer** and **Esther's prayer**, including her disgust at the royal bed and her plea for courage (often before she approaches the king).
- **Esther's audience**, expanded, with the king's face first "like a bull in heat" (in some wording) and then gracious.
- **The counter-edict** in the king's name, allowing the Jews to defend themselves and praising their God.
- **The interpretation of the dream** and a colophon about the book's transmission.

## Key passages and ideas

The prayers are the theological center. Esther's prayer, in particular, is a first-principles document of diaspora piety under a pagan court: she hates the "splendor" that has been forced on her, she remembers the exodus, and she asks for eloquent speech before the "lion." The dream-and-interpretation frame makes the whole story a revealed conflict between two lots, not only a court novella. The edicts show how a biblical book can imitate imperial style in order to answer it.

When reading a Catholic Bible that interleaves these additions with the Hebrew scenes, it is worth also reading the Hebrew book straight through, so that the silence and the later speech can both be heard.

A practical warning: when a study Bible prints the additions as chapters 10–16 after the Hebrew ending, the narrative order is no longer chronological. When it interleaves them, the Hebrew silence is harder to hear. The best first exercise is to read the Masoretic story once, the additions once as a block, and only then a combined text, so that the work of the Greek editors stays visible.

## Themes and reception

Themes include named providence, prayer in political danger, and the interpretation of a story that had been more reserved. Jerome placed some of the additions at the end of Esther in the Vulgate, which is why older Catholic chapter numbers can be confusing. Protestant Bibles that include an Apocrypha usually print the additions as a block. The existence of two Esthers is one of the cleanest illustrations of how canon and textual tradition are not the same question.
