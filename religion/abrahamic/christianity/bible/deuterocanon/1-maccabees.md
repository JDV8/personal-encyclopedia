# 1 Maccabees

**Name and meaning.** A sober historical narrative, originally in Hebrew and preserved in Greek, of the Jewish revolt against Antiochus IV Epiphanes and the rise of the Hasmonean (Maccabean) house, from about 175 to 134 BCE. The nickname "Maccabee" ("hammer") belongs first to Judas, son of Mattathias. The book is the main narrative source for [Hanukkah](../glossary/hanukkah.md).

**Prerequisites:** [deuterocanon](README.md), [Daniel](../old-testament/books/major-prophets/daniel.md) (especially if read as a contemporary of the same crisis), and [Exile and return](../old-testament/10-exile-and-return.md).

## Dating and authorship

Anonymous, written in Hebrew probably in the late second century BCE, under a Hasmonean ruler (often dated in the reign of John Hyrcanus, after 134). The author is a court historian in all but name: favorable to the Hasmonean family, interested in legitimacy, alliances, and dates, and reserved about miracle language (contrast [2 Maccabees](2-maccabees.md)). The Hebrew original is lost; the Greek is a fairly literal translation.

## Context

After Alexander's empire split, Judea came under the Seleucids. Antiochus IV's measures — a ban on Jewish law, a desecration of the temple, and a demand for pagan sacrifice — provoked first a rural revolt under the priest Mattathias of Modein, then a war led by his sons Judas, Jonathan, and Simon. The book also records the rededication of the temple in 164 BCE and the later achievement of a measure of political independence.

## Outline

- **1–2.** Alexander's legacy; Antiochus's oppression; the persecution; Mattathias's resistance and last words.
- **3–9.** Judas Maccabeus: battles, the temple's rededication (4:36–59), wars with neighbors, a treaty with Rome, Judas's death.
- **9–12.** Jonathan as leader and high priest; further diplomacy.
- **13–16.** Simon's leadership, a decree honoring him, his death by treachery; a closing notice about John (Hyrcanus).

## Key passages and ideas

Chapter 1 is a classic account of forced Hellenization and of "abomination" in the temple, the same crisis [Daniel](../old-testament/books/major-prophets/daniel.md) 11 sees in a visionary mode. The rededication in chapter 4, with its new altar and eight-day celebration "like the festival of booths," is the origin story of Hanukkah (2 Maccabees adds the oil tradition in a different form; the famous oil miracle of later rabbinic telling is not in 1 Maccabees). The book's diplomacy with Rome and Sparta shows a small people learning to live in a world of great powers — a theme already in the Hebrew Bible, now in a Greek-period key.

The author does not hide the violence of the revolt, including campaigns against fellow Jews who had gone over to the king's policy. A first-principles reading has to hold both the defense of the law and the cost.

The last chapters, which honor Simon in almost royal language and then record his murder at a banquet, already contain the seed of later unease about the Hasmoneans. A family can be deliverers in one generation and a dynasty with ordinary political cruelties in the next. The book itself mostly praises; the history that followed (which Josephus and, later, the Roman conquest would tell) is the commentary no narrator here provides. Read to the end, and then go back to the New Testament's world of parties and memories that this independence helped to form.

## Themes and reception

Themes include zeal for the law, temple, political independence, and the legitimacy of a new priestly-royal house. The book is scripture in Catholic and Orthodox canons. Jewish tradition kept Hanukkah but did not canonize 1 Maccabees; the rabbis had their own reasons to be ambivalent about later Hasmoneans. Protestant Bibles usually include the book, if at all, in an Apocrypha. For New Testament readers, it is essential background to [Second Temple Judaism](../new-testament/01-second-temple-judaism-and-rome.md): parties, temple politics, and the memory of a successful revolt.

The style is deliberately "biblical," echoing the Former Prophets. That is a claim: this history belongs in the same line as Judges and Kings, even if later canons disagreed.
