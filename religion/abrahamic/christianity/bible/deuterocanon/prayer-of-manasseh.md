# Prayer of Manasseh

**Name and meaning.** A short penitential prayer attributed to King Manasseh of Judah, who, in [2 Kings](../old-testament/books/history/2-kings.md), is the most condemned of the southern kings, and who, in [2 Chronicles](../old-testament/books/history/2-chronicles.md) 33, is said to have prayed in exile in Babylon and been restored. The Chronicler notes that the prayer is written in a source; this later text is a composition that supplies such a prayer.

**Prerequisites:** [2 Chronicles](../old-testament/books/history/2-chronicles.md) 33 and [deuterocanon](README.md).

## Dating and authorship

The historical Manasseh ruled in the seventh century BCE. The prayer is a later Jewish (or possibly early Christian-adopted Jewish) composition, usually dated in the Hellenistic or early Roman period, written in Greek or translated into Greek. It is not claimed as Manasseh's own words by modern study. Traditional use followed the attribution. The text is found in some Septuagint manuscripts, in the Didascalia and Apostolic Constitutions, and in some Orthodox canons; it is not part of the Catholic deuterocanon proper, but it appears in many Apocrypha collections and in some Protestant liturgies.

## Context

Chronicles had already revised Kings' portrait of Manasseh by giving him a repentance. A community that believed no sin is beyond mercy would naturally want the words. The prayer is therefore a midrash in the form of a psalm: it fills a gap, and it teaches a doctrine of repentance with the worst king as the test case.

## Outline

The prayer is a single movement, often fifteen verses:

- An address to the Lord, God of the ancestors, creator of heaven and earth, whose mercy is immeasurable.
- A confession that God has appointed repentance for sinners, and that the speaker is the worst of them, with a list of sins (idols, abominations) in the first person.
- A plea not to be condemned, not to be left in the depths, and a request for forgiveness.
- A closing vow of praise for the rest of the speaker's life.

## Key passages and ideas

The theology is concentrated: God's mercy is not a lenient mood but a provision built into the way of sinners — "you have appointed repentance." The speaker does not minimize the crimes. The Chronicler's story (fetters, a foreign prison, a return to Jerusalem, a removal of foreign altars) is the implied narrative. Read the prayer and 2 Chronicles 33 together, and then 2 Kings 21, to see three stages of the Manasseh tradition: the unrelenting verdict, the repentance, and the supplied words.

Liturgically, the prayer has been used in Christian penitential offices, including in some Anglican and Orthodox services, because it is brief, theologically dense, and not tied to a festival. That use can be independent of a decision about canonicity.

## Themes and reception

Themes include repentance, the worst-case sinner, creation, and praise after mercy. The work is a first-principles example of scripture (or near-scripture) as a conversation: later writers did not always leave earlier silences alone. Whether one prays it as canon or reads it as Second Temple piety, it belongs with the psalms of confession and with the Chronicler's theology of return.

Because it is short, editions sometimes bury it in an appendix. It is worth reading slowly, aloud, as it was meant to be prayed, and then returning to the question the Chronicler had already raised: can the king on whom Kings blames the exile also be a model of repentance?

The variation of its canonical status among churches is itself a teaching moment about the [deuterocanon](../glossary/terms/deuterocanon.md): "the" Bible is a family of related lists, not a single identical table of contents.
