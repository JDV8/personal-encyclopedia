# Tobit

**Name and meaning.** A pious tale, preserved in Greek (and in later languages), about Tobit, a righteous Israelite of the northern exile in Nineveh, his son Tobias, Sarah a kinswoman in Media, and the angel Raphael who travels in disguise. The book teaches almsgiving, burial of the dead, endogamous marriage, and a hidden providence.

**Prerequisites:** [Exile and return](../old-testament/10-exile-and-return.md) and [deuterocanon](README.md). Helpful: [Job](../old-testament/books/wisdom-poetry/job.md), another righteous sufferer, of a very different tone.

## Dating and authorship

The book is anonymous. It was likely written in Aramaic or Hebrew (fragments of both were found at Qumran) in the late Persian or Hellenistic period, often dated around 225–175 BCE, though earlier dates are possible. The setting is the eighth-century Assyrian exile, which is a literary past. Two main Greek forms (a shorter and a longer, the longer usually preferred today) show that the story circulated in more than one edition.

## Context

Diaspora life is the book's real world: a family trying to keep the law, bury the neglected dead, and marry within the kin, far from Jerusalem (though the book still loves the temple). Demonic danger (Asmodeus, who has killed Sarah's seven bridegrooms) and angelic help belong to a Second Temple cosmology that the New Testament also assumes.

## Outline

- **1–2.** Tobit's piety, his blindness after burying the dead, and his prayer.
- **3.** Sarah's prayer in Media; both prayers heard, and Raphael sent.
- **4.** Tobit's wisdom speech to Tobias, especially on almsgiving.
- **5–9.** The journey with "Azariah," the fish, the marriage, the binding of the demon, the recovery of a deposit.
- **10–12.** The return, the healing of Tobit's eyes, Raphael's revelation and charge to write the story.
- **13–14.** Tobit's hymn and last words, including a hope for Jerusalem; Tobias's later life and Nineveh's fall.

## Key passages and ideas

The parallel prayers of chapter 3, answered together, are the book's theology of providence in narrative form. Raphael's "I am one of the seven angels who stand ready" (12:15) is an important Second Temple angelological text. The wisdom speech of chapter 4, and the command to not let the wages of those who work for you remain overnight, sits firmly in Israel's social law. The book is also a marriage story: a prayer at the wedding night (chapter 8) became a liturgical resource in later Christian use.

The medical and demonic plot (a fish's gall, heart, and liver used as remedies) belongs to a world in which healing and exorcism are not yet separated into modern departments. Raphael is both physician and angel. The book therefore sits naturally beside later Gospel healing stories, without being their source in a simple sense. It is a cousin in the same cosmos: God sends help under an ordinary face, and a family learns, at the end, whom they have been entertaining.

## Themes and reception

Themes include hidden angels, almsgiving, burial, family loyalty, and hope for Jerusalem from exile. The book is canonical in Catholic and Orthodox churches and is read as scripture there; Jewish and Protestant canons do not include it, though Jews kept related traditions of piety, and the Qumran fragments show that the story was read in Hebrew and Aramaic. Painters loved Tobias and the fish, and the angel in disguise. A first-principles reader can learn diaspora ethics from it whether or not it stands in their canon.

The book's humor (a dog on the road, a hapless father, an angel under a false name) should not be missed. It is serious without being grim.
