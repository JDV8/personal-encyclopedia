# Joseph

**Prerequisites:** [Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob](03-abraham-isaac-jacob.md)
**Key ideas:** a novella of betrayal and recognition; dreams and administrative wisdom; God working through, not around, human wrong; how Israel comes to be in Egypt, which the Exodus will reverse.

Joseph is Jacob's son by Rachel, born late, favored, and given an ornamented robe. He dreams that his brothers' sheaves bow to his sheaf, and that the sun, moon, and eleven stars bow to him. He tells the dreams. His brothers, already jealous, throw him into a pit and sell him to traders (the text again shows some doubling in the details). They dip the robe in goat's blood and let Jacob conclude that a wild animal has killed the boy.

In Egypt Joseph is sold to Potiphar, an official. He rises in the household; Potiphar's wife tries to seduce him, is refused, and accuses him. In prison he again rises, and interprets the dreams of a cupbearer and a baker: restoration for one, death for the other. When Pharaoh dreams of seven fat cows and seven lean, seven good ears of grain and seven blighted, the cupbearer remembers. Joseph says the dreams are one: seven years of plenty, seven of famine. He advises storage. Pharaoh puts him over the land. Joseph receives an Egyptian name and an Egyptian wife; they have two sons, Manasseh and Ephraim.

The famine reaches Canaan. Jacob sends the brothers, except Benjamin, Rachel's remaining son, to buy grain. They bow before Joseph and do not know him. A long, psychologically sharp sequence follows: Joseph tests them, holds Simeon, demands Benjamin, plants a cup in Benjamin's sack, and waits to see whether the brothers will abandon the other favored son as they once abandoned him. Judah offers himself in Benjamin's place. Only then does Joseph disclose himself: "I am Joseph. Is my father still alive?" He frames the past theologically: "You meant evil against me, but God meant it for good, to preserve life."

The family comes down to Egypt and settles in Goshen. Jacob, before he dies, blesses the sons and elevates Ephraim and Manasseh. Joseph buries him in Canaan and forgives the brothers again. The book ends with Joseph's death and a charge to carry his bones up from Egypt when God comes to them — a sentence that already leans toward [Exodus](05-moses-exodus-and-sinai.md).

Among the Genesis stories, Joseph's cycle reads most like a tightly told novella: court tale, recognition scene, irony, a wisdom-hero who can interpret and administer. Traditional reading takes it as the historical bridge into the sojourn in Egypt. Scholarly readers often admire it as a composition and are cautious about pinning it to a particular pharaoh or dynasty; Egyptian color is present, specific datable markers are scarce. Either way, the theological claim is clear. Fraternal violence, last seen in Cain, is not the last word. The promise can travel through betrayal, exile, and foreign power without being canceled — which is a theme the later [exile](10-exile-and-return.md) will need.
