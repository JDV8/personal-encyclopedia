# Creation and Eden

**Prerequisites:** [Old Testament](README.md) and [genres](../04-genres.md)
**Key ideas:** Genesis 1 and Genesis 2–3 are two accounts, not one smooth paragraph; a good creation and a fractured relationship; traditional readings (including "the Fall") and scholarly comparisons with other Ancient Near Eastern origin stories.

The Bible opens with origin stories, not with a philosophy textbook. Still, these chapters became some of the most philosophically worked texts in the library: what the world is, what a human being is, and what has gone wrong.

## Genesis 1: an ordered world

In the first account, God (*Elohim*) creates in six days and rests on the seventh. The pattern is formal and liturgical: "And God said… and it was so… and God saw that it was good… and there was evening and morning." Light is divided from darkness; the dome of the sky is set; land and sea are separated; then come plants, the lights of the sky, fish and birds, land animals, and finally human beings, male and female, made in the image of God, given charge over other living things, and blessed to be fruitful. Everything is "very good." The seventh day is hallowed.

The picture is of a world that exists because it is spoken, not because a god defeated a rival in a last battle. Time is ordered. Matter is not evil. Sabbath is baked into the structure of things.

## Genesis 2–3: the garden and the expulsion

The second account has a different texture and a different order. The Lord God (*YHWH Elohim*) forms a human (*adam*) from the dust of the ground (*adamah*) and breathes life into him. A garden is planted in Eden. The human is placed there to work and keep it, free to eat of every tree except the tree of the knowledge of good and evil. The animals are formed and named; no suitable companion is found; the woman is built from the human's side. They are naked and not ashamed.

A serpent, described as shrewd, asks the woman about the command, and the conversation shifts the wording. She and her husband eat. Their eyes are opened; they know they are naked; they hide. When the Lord God questions them, the man blames the woman (and, implicitly, God who gave her), and the woman blames the serpent. Consequences follow: pain in childbirth, toil from a resistant ground, disrupted relationship, and expulsion from the garden, with the way to the tree of life guarded. They do not die on that very day in a simple biological sense, but a death-bounded, exiled life begins.

## How the two accounts have been read

Traditional Jewish and Christian reading has generally taken both chapters as authoritative origin history (with much internal debate about how literally the "days" should be taken) and has often read them as one sequence: God creates, then the garden episode explains the entry of sin and death. Christian theology, especially after Paul and later Augustine, developed a strong doctrine of a **Fall** and of original sin inherited from Adam. Jewish interpretation has often emphasized exile, responsibility, and the complexity of knowledge and disobedience without that Augustinian frame. These are already different theological uses of the same pages.

Historical-critical scholarship typically sees **two compositional voices** (often called Priestly for Genesis 1, and a Yahwistic narrative for Genesis 2–3), later placed side by side. It also compares these texts with other Ancient Near Eastern works, such as the Babylonian *Enuma Elish* (creation through divine combat) and, for the garden and the later Flood, motifs from Mesopotamian literature. The usual scholarly point is not "the Bible copied a myth, therefore it is empty," nor "there are no comparisons." It is that Genesis speaks in a world of origin stories, and its distinctives (one God, a good material world, humans as images rather than slaves of the gods) show up more clearly against that background.

For this path, the first-principles yield is: the Bible's story begins with a good, ordered creation and a break in trust and obedience that sends human beings east of Eden. Everything that follows happens in that aftermath.
