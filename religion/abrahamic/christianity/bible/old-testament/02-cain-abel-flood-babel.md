# Cain and Abel, the Flood, and Babel

**Prerequisites:** [Creation and Eden](01-creation-and-eden.md)
**Key ideas:** violence enters the human story; the Flood as un-creation and a covenant with the living; Babel as scattered language and scattered power; genealogies linking origins to Abraham.

Genesis 4–11 is sometimes called the "primeval history": a set of stories about early humanity as a whole, before the camera tightens on one family.

## Cain and Abel

Adam and Eve have two sons: Cain, a worker of the ground, and Abel, a keeper of sheep. Both bring offerings; the Lord regards Abel's offering and not Cain's. The text is terse about why. The Lord warns Cain that sin is crouching at the door, and that Cain must rule over it. Cain instead kills his brother in the field.

Asked where Abel is, Cain answers, "Am I my brother's keeper?" The ground that drank Abel's blood will no longer yield its strength to Cain. He is marked, so that he will not be killed, and he becomes a wanderer, founding a city in the land of wandering. The story that began with a garden now includes murder, and the question of responsibility for the brother. A later genealogy also introduces Lamech, who boasts of greater violence — the problem is not solved in the next generation.

## The Flood

Human wickedness fills the earth; "every inclination of the thoughts of their hearts is only evil continually," and the Lord is grieved. Noah, a righteous man, is told to build an ark. He brings animals (the text intertwines "two by two" with a "seven pairs of clean animals" tradition). The floodgates of the sky and the springs of the deep burst: this is a kind of un-creation, the separations of Genesis 1 collapsing. Everything on dry land that has the breath of life dies.

When the waters recede, Noah offers a sacrifice. The Lord promises never again to destroy all flesh by a flood, and sets the rainbow as the sign of a covenant with Noah, his descendants, and "every living creature." Permission to eat meat appears, along with a prohibition on eating blood and on shedding human blood: the image of God is invoked again, now as a reason not to kill. Noah's drunkenness and the cursing of Canaan (through the episode of Ham) follow, a strange, unsettling coda that later readers have sometimes misused.

Traditional reading takes the Flood as a global historical judgment. Scholarly reading notes that the Genesis narrative appears to braid two accounts, and that it stands near other Ancient Near Eastern flood stories, especially the Mesopotamian traditions in the *Atrahasis* epic and the Gilgamesh flood tablet (a favored man warned to build a boat, birds sent out, a sacrifice after landing). Again, comparison highlights both resemblance and difference: in Genesis the emphasis falls on moral grief, on one God's judgment and mercy, and on a covenant with life itself, rather than on gods who cannot sleep for the noise of humans.

## Babel

The whole earth has one language. People settle in Shinar and propose to build a city and a tower with its top in the heavens, to make a name for themselves and to avoid being scattered. The Lord comes down, observes that this is only the beginning of what they will do, and confuses their language. The city is left unfinished; they are scattered; the place is called Babel, with a wordplay on "confusion."

The story is brief and dense. It has been read as a tale of pride, as a tale about imperial building (Babylon looms in the background of later Israelite memory), and as an explanation of the world's peoples and tongues. It also sets up the next move: if humanity's project is to make a name for itself and stay put, God will shortly make a name for one man and send him out.

Genealogies in these chapters (the "table of nations" in Genesis 10, the line from Shem toward Terah) are doing work: they connect the universal story to a particular family. The next lesson leaves "humanity in general" and follows that family.
