# Job

**Name and meaning.** Named for Job (Hebrew *Iyyov*), a blameless sufferer from the land of Uz, outside Israel. The book is the Bible's most sustained argument about innocent suffering, the limits of conventional wisdom, and the difference between speaking about God and being answered by God. In the Tanakh it stands among the Writings; in Christian Bibles it opens the wisdom and poetry section.

**Prerequisites:** [Wisdom literature](../../09-wisdom-literature.md) and [genres](../../../04-genres.md). Helpful: [Proverbs](proverbs.md), whose moral world this book tests.

## Dating and authorship

The book is anonymous. Traditional guesses have ranged widely (Moses is one ancient suggestion; the setting is patriarchal in flavor, with Job as a kind of priest of his household and no mention of Israel, Torah, or temple). Scholarly dates for the poetic dialogues commonly fall in the sixth or fifth century BCE (exilic or post-exilic), with the prose frame sometimes judged earlier or later than the speeches. There is no consensus. The language is difficult, with a high density of rare words. A first-principles reader should not wait on a date; the argument is the book.

## Context

A prose prologue and epilogue surround a long poetic debate. In the prologue, the satan (the accuser, a member of the heavenly court, not yet the later figure of the devil in a full dualistic sense) is permitted to test whether Job's piety depends on his prosperity. Job loses property, children, and health, and refuses to curse God. Three friends — Eliphaz, Bildad, and Zophar — come to comfort him and instead argue that suffering is punishment. A younger speaker, Elihu, appears later. Then the Lord answers Job from a whirlwind, and the prose epilogue restores Job's fortunes.

The book knows the international wisdom tradition (Edomite or Arabian flavor has often been suggested for Uz) and also Israel's covenant theology, which it puts under terrible pressure. It is not a neat theodicy. It is a contest of speeches.

## Outline

- **1–2.** Prologue: the heavenly scenes; the two tests; Job's initial integrity; the friends' seven days of silence.
- **3.** Job's lament: he curses the day of his birth.
- **4–27.** Three cycles of speeches (the third is incomplete or broken) between Job and the three friends.
- **28.** A poem on wisdom's hiddenness ("where shall wisdom be found?").
- **29–31.** Job's final speech: memory of former blessing, present humiliation, an oath of innocence.
- **32–37.** Elihu's speeches.
- **38–41.** The Lord's speeches from the whirlwind, including the portraits of Behemoth and Leviathan.
- **42.** Job's response; the Lord's rebuke of the friends; the epilogue of restoration.

## Key passages and ideas

Job's early "the Lord gave, and the Lord has taken away" (1:21) and "shall we receive good from God, and not receive evil?" (2:10) are not the book's last word; they are the starting piety that the poems will complicate. Job's later speeches demand a hearing, accuse the friends of special pleading, and, at moments, seem to summon God as a legal adversary. The famous line often translated "I know that my redeemer lives" (19:25) is textually and interpretively difficult; Christian tradition has read it messianically, while many modern commentators keep it inside Job's hope for a vindicator of his case. Report both, and do not flatten the Hebrew.

The Lord's speeches refuse the friends' calculus and also refuse to give Job the explanation the prologue has given the reader. God questions Job about the making and governing of the world, about rain on uninhabited land, about creatures that are not made for human use. The answer, if it is an answer, is about the scale and wildness of creation, not about Job's guilt. Job's final words include recantation or, in some readings, a more ironic concession; the Hebrew of 42:6 can be construed more than one way. The epilogue, in which Job prays for his friends and receives a doubled estate, has struck some readers as too neat and others as a necessary restoration of relationship. The book holds the tension.

## Themes and reception

Themes include innocent suffering, the failure of retributive clichés, friendship as a moral test, the hiddenness of wisdom, and the possibility of encounter without explanation. The satan of the prologue should not be silently equated with later Christian or Jewish demonology, though later tradition did make that move.

Jewish reading has midrashim on Job's identity and a place for the book in the Writings; some rabbinic voices are uneasy with Job's challenges. Christian reading has seen in Job a type of Christ, a model of patience (the epistle of [James](../../../new-testament/books/general-epistles/james.md) names that patience), and a text for theodicy. Modern philosophy and literature (from Kierkegaard to more recent trauma studies) keep returning to it. Historical-critical work on the book's layers should not be used to evade the finished argument.
