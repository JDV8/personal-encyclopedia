# Psalms

**Name and meaning.** *Psalms* comes from a Greek word for songs sung to a stringed instrument. In Hebrew the book is *Tehillim*, "praises," though the collection includes lament, thanksgiving, royal psalms, wisdom psalms, and curses as well as praise. It is the Bible's prayer book and hymnbook, and in both Jewish and Christian life it has been the most constantly used biblical book.

**Prerequisites:** [Wisdom literature](../../09-wisdom-literature.md) and [genres](../../../04-genres.md). Helpful: [2 Samuel](../history/2-samuel.md), because so many psalms are tied to David and to Zion.

## Dating and authorship

Superscriptions attribute many psalms to David, others to Asaph, the sons of Korah, Solomon, Moses (Psalm 90), and Ethan. Traditional reading takes the Davidic attributions as authorship. Scholarly study treats the superscriptions as important ancient interpretive claims, not always as historical bylines; the psalms themselves range in date from the monarchy (and perhaps earlier) through the exile and well into the Second Temple period. The book is a collection of collections: for example, the Elohistic Psalter (roughly 42–83) prefers *Elohim*, and there are marked Davidic groups, pilgrimage songs (120–134), and a final doxological sequence (146–150). The five-book structure (1–41, 42–72, 73–89, 90–106, 107–150), each section ending with a doxology, is a deliberate final shape, often compared to the five books of the Torah. Psalm 72:20 ("the prayers of David… are ended") sits inside a larger Davidic collection and is a clue to editorial history.

## Context

Psalms were used in temple worship, in private or household devotion, and, after the temple's destruction, in synagogue and church. Some are clearly communal and liturgical (processions, coronations, national lament); some sound like an individual's cry. The "I" of a psalm can be a king, a representative worshiper, or a later reader who takes the words in hand. Royal psalms (2, 45, 72, 110, and others) outlived the monarchy and were reread messianically in Judaism and Christianity.

## Outline

The five books are the primary structure, but it also helps to know types (a classification associated with Hermann Gunkel, useful if not treated as a rigid science):

- **Laments**, individual and communal: complaint, petition, often a turn to trust (e.g., 13, 22, 44, 88).
- **Thanksgivings** and **hymns of praise** (e.g., 30, 100, 103, 150).
- **Royal psalms** and **Zion psalms** (e.g., 2, 46, 48, 110).
- **Wisdom and Torah psalms** (e.g., 1, 19, 73, 119).
- **Historical rehearsals** (e.g., 78, 105, 106).
- **Songs of ascents** (120–134), for pilgrimage to Jerusalem.
- **Imprecatory psalms**, which call for judgment on enemies (e.g., 69, 109, 137).

Psalm 1 (the two ways) and Psalm 2 (the Lord's anointed king) together form a gateway: Torah piety and royal hope. Psalm 150 is the closing storm of praise.

## Key passages and ideas

Psalm 23 ("the Lord is my shepherd") and Psalm 51 (a plea for mercy, headed as David's after the Bathsheba affair) are among the most memorized. Psalm 22 opens with "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" — words the [Gospels](../../../new-testament/books/gospels/README.md) place on Jesus' lips at the crucifixion — and ends in public praise, a movement many laments share. Psalm 110, "the Lord says to my lord, sit at my right hand," is the Old Testament text most often cited in the New Testament, in arguments about the Messiah's status. Psalm 119 is an acrostic tour of love for the Torah. Psalm 137, "by the rivers of Babylon," ends with a notorious blessing on those who would dash Babylon's infants against the rock — a reminder that the Psalter refuses to be only gentle.

The collection teaches by juxtaposition: praise next to despair, private confession next to coronation liturgy, a curse next to "his mercy endures forever." To extract only the comforting psalms is to misread the book.

## Themes and reception

Themes include the character of God (refuge, judge, shepherd, king), the reality of enemies, the goodness and difficulty of Torah, Zion as the place of the Lord's presence, and the movement from complaint to praise. Hebrew parallelism is the basic poetic engine; good translations try to keep it visible.

Jewish life uses the Psalms daily (and has cycles for completing the book). The Second Temple community, Qumran, and the early church all prayed them. Christian liturgy, monastic offices, and the Reformers' hymnody are unimaginable without this book. Modern scholarship on form, editorial shape, and the "shape and shaping" of the Psalter has been especially rich in recent decades. Read psalms slowly, out loud, and in their neighboring context, not only as isolated devotional fragments.
