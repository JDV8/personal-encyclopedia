# Song of Songs

**Name and meaning.** Also called the Song of Solomon or Canticles. In Hebrew, *Shir Hashirim*, "the song of songs" (a superlative: the greatest song). It is a collection of love poems between a woman and a man, with a chorus of daughters of Jerusalem, rich in garden, body, and countryside imagery. God's name does not appear (except in a possible contracted form in 8:6). In the Tanakh it is one of the Five Scrolls and is associated with Passover; in Christian Bibles it closes the wisdom and poetry section.

**Prerequisites:** [Wisdom literature](../../09-wisdom-literature.md) and [genres](../../../04-genres.md).

## Dating and authorship

The heading "which is Solomon's" (1:1) has traditionally been read as Solomonic authorship, and Solomon appears as a figure in the poems. Scholarly dates vary from the monarchic period to the Hellenistic age; many place the language relatively late while allowing older poetic material. The book may be a cycle of poems rather than a single narrative with a stable plot (though some readings reconstruct a story involving a rustic beloved, a king, and a girl). Authorship is unknown.

## Context

Ancient Near Eastern love poetry (Egyptian songs, for example) offers partial parallels: praise of the body, springtime settings, a blend of sensuality and metaphor. Israel included this poetry in scripture, which forced later communities to explain why. The two main families of reading have been literal (or "natural"): it is about human erotic love, covenantally good; and allegorical: it is about the love of the Lord and Israel, or of Christ and the church, or of God and the soul. Both have ancient pedigree. The allegorical reading dominated synagogue and church for centuries (Rabbi Akiva's famous defense of the Song's holiness belongs here). Modern historical reading recovered the literal sense, sometimes dismissing allegory too quickly. A first-principles approach can say: the poems are genuinely erotic; the decision to canonize them already invites reflection on love, covenant, and holiness; later allegory is reception, not the only original meaning.

## Outline

Because the structure is debated, it is safer to mark movements than a tight plot:

- **1–2.** Mutual desire; spring; the woman as lily and the man as an apple tree; "his banner over me is love."
- **3.** A night search through the city; a royal procession of Solomon.
- **4–5.** A *wasf* (a systematic praise of the beloved's body); a garden fountain; a second night search and a description of the man.
- **6–7.** More praise; a dance; rural and royal imagery mixed.
- **8.** Love as strong as death, jealousy as fierce as Sheol; the seal upon the heart; a little sister; the last appeals.

## Key passages and ideas

The woman's voice is unusually prominent for ancient literature: she speaks first and last, and she seeks, remembers, and commands ("do not stir up love until it wishes"). 8:6–7 — love strong as death, many waters cannot quench it — is the book's nearest approach to an explicit thesis. The garden and vineyard images overlap with Israel-as-vineyard language elsewhere, which helped allegorical readers, but in the poems themselves the garden is also simply a body and a place of meeting.

The city watchmen who beat the woman in 5:7 are a dark note; the Song is not an unbroken idyll. Royal luxury and rural freedom sometimes seem to stand in tension, which is why dramatic readings have multiplied.

## Themes and reception

Themes include desire, mutuality, the senses, waiting, the public and private faces of love, and a celebration of bodies without a fall narrative attached. The book has generated an enormous commentary tradition: Jewish midrash and Targum, Origen, Bernard of Clairvaux's sermons, mystical readings in both traditions, and modern literary and feminist studies. Some religious communities have been nervous of the literal sense; some modern readers have been nervous of allegory. The canon holds a love song, and communities have had to grow up to it in more than one way.
