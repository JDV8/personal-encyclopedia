# Hosea

**Name and meaning.** Named for Hosea son of Beeri (Hebrew *Hoshea*, "salvation"), a prophet to the northern kingdom in the eighth century BCE. The book is built around a marriage metaphor: Israel's unfaithfulness to the Lord is adultery; the Lord's stubborn love is a husband's hesed. It opens the Book of the Twelve.

**Prerequisites:** [The prophets](../../08-prophets.md) and [1–2 Kings](../history/1-kings.md). Helpful: [Amos](amos.md), a near contemporary in the north.

## Dating and authorship

The heading places Hosea in the days of Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah of Judah, and of Jeroboam II of Israel — roughly the mid-to-late eighth century, before and perhaps around the fall of Samaria in 722/721. Traditional reading takes the book as Hosea's own words. Scholarly study generally accepts an eighth-century northern prophet and often sees later Judahite editing (the frequent mentions of Judah, and the survival of the book in the south, are part of that discussion). The order of some oracles is debated, but the marriage cycle in chapters 1–3 is a planned opening.

## Context

The north under Jeroboam II was relatively prosperous and, in the prophets' view, spiritually ruined: calf cult at Bethel, political assassination, and alliances that treated the Lord as one option among others. Hosea's language of "baal" plays on both the Canaanite god and the word for "husband" or "master." The prophet's own marriage, commanded in the book, is the lived parable.

## Outline

- **1–3.** Hosea, Gomer, and the children named Jezreel, Lo-Ruhamah ("not pitied"), and Lo-Ammi ("not my people"); a second cycle of love and discipline in chapter 3; the promise that "not my people" will be called "children of the living God."
- **4–11.** Oracles of lawsuit: "there is no faithfulness or hesed or knowledge of God in the land"; priests and people indicted; the tenderness of chapter 11 ("when Israel was a child, I loved him").
- **12–14.** Jacob traditions used as accusation; a final plea to return, with a promise of healing and a warning that the wise will understand.

## Key passages and ideas

The children's names are theology in a household. Jezreel recalls Jehu's bloodshed and also the "sowing" of future hope. The reversal in 1:10 and 2:23 — "not my people" becoming "my people" — is quoted in [Paul](../../../new-testament/books/pauline-epistles/romans.md) and [1 Peter](../../../new-testament/books/general-epistles/1-peter.md). Hosea 6:6, "I desire hesed and not sacrifice, the knowledge of God rather than burnt offerings," is cited by Jesus in [Matthew](../../../new-testament/books/gospels/matthew.md). Chapter 11 is among the most moving passages in the prophets: God's compassion "grows warm and tender," and the Holy One will not come in wrath as a man would.

How literally to take the marriage command (a prophet told to marry a promiscuous woman, or a woman who later becomes unfaithful?) has been debated since antiquity. The book's point does not depend on solving every biographical detail: covenant love is not sentiment, and it is not cheap.

The book's Hebrew is among the hardest in the prophets: abrupt shifts, images piled on images, and a text that sometimes looks damaged. That difficulty is part of reading Hosea honestly; commentaries disagree, and a student should not pretend every line is smooth. What is not obscure is the argument. Israel's problem is not lack of religion. It is a religion that no longer knows the Lord, and a politics that treats Assyria and Egypt as saviors. The healing promised in the last chapter is therefore a healing of speech and loyalty — "take words with you and return" — not a mere change of fortune.

## Themes and reception

Themes include hesed, knowledge of God, political folly, the tenderness and jealousy of God, and hope after judgment. The northern dialect and rural imagery (with dew, baking ovens, stubborn heifers) give the book a distinctive texture. It taught later readers, Jewish and Christian, to think of idolatry as betrayal of a relationship, not only as a ritual mistake.
