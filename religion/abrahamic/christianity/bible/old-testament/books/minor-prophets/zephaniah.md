# Zephaniah

**Name and meaning.** Named for Zephaniah son of Cushi, with a genealogy traced back to Hezekiah (possibly the king). The book announces a sweeping "day of the Lord" against Judah and the nations, then ends with a song of the Lord's joy over a restored remnant in Zion. It is a small scroll with a large horizon.

**Prerequisites:** [The prophets](../../08-prophets.md) and [2 Kings](../history/2-kings.md) on Josiah. Helpful: [Deuteronomy](../law-torah/deuteronomy.md) and [Amos](amos.md) on the day of the Lord.

## Dating and authorship

The heading places Zephaniah in the days of Josiah (640–609 BCE). Many scholars put his preaching early in that reign, before or in the first stages of Josiah's reform, because of the indictment of foreign dress, Baal, and astral worship in Jerusalem; others allow a later moment in the same reign. Traditional reading accepts the heading. The closing promises (3:9–20) are sometimes judged later by critics, sometimes taken as the prophet's own hope.

## Context

Judah is in the shadow of Assyria's decline and of whatever will replace it. Jerusalem's elites are complacent: "the Lord will not do good, nor will he do harm." Zephaniah announces a day that will search Jerusalem with lamps. The geographic sweep of the oracles (Philistia, Moab, Cush, Assyria) makes the day of the Lord a worldwide passing-through, not a local storm.

## Outline

- **1.** The day of the Lord: sweeping creation language; judgment on idolatry in Judah; a coming cry from the Fish Gate; the day as darkness, trumpet, and distress for the complacent.
- **2.** A call to seek the Lord, seek righteousness and humility, "perhaps you may be hidden"; oracles against the nations; Nineveh a desolation, a lair for wild animals.
- **3.** Woe to the rebellious city; remaining hope for a humble people; a purified speech for the peoples; the Lord's presence as king of Israel; "he will rejoice over you with gladness… he will exult over you with loud singing."

## Key passages and ideas

The "perhaps" of 2:3 is honest eschatology: humility is commanded, safety is not presumed. The portrait of the day of the Lord in chapter 1 (and its later afterlife in liturgy, including the medieval *Dies irae*) is severe. The ending of chapter 3 is correspondingly intimate: the Mighty One in the midst of Zion is not only a warrior but a God who sings over a remnant. That juxtaposition is the book's theology.

The mention of Cush in the genealogy and in the oracles has generated discussion about the prophet's possible African connections; the evidence is suggestive, not conclusive, and should not be overclaimed.

The geography of chapter 2 is a map of humility. Philistia on the west, Moab and Ammon on the east, Cush in the far south, and Assyria in the north are all brought into the same "day." Judah is not the spectator of other people's judgment; it is named first. The remnant hope is therefore not a national boast. It is "a people humble and lowly" who take refuge in the Lord's name (3:12). When the closing song says that the Lord will exult over Zion with loud singing, the singer has already been through that narrowing. Joy is the last word, not the first.

## Themes and reception

Themes include the day of the Lord, complacency, humility, a remnant, the nations, and divine joy. Christian Advent and later apocalyptic imagination have used Zephaniah's day; Jewish reading keeps it among the Twelve as a Josiah-era word with a Zion ending. Historical study ties it to the religious politics of the late seventh century, the same world as the early Deuteronomic reform.
