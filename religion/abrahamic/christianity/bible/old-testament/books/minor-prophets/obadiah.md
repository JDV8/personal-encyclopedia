# Obadiah

**Name and meaning.** The shortest book in the Hebrew Bible, named for Obadiah ("servant of the Lord"). It is a single oracle against Edom, the neighbor and kin-people southeast of Judah, for violence in the day of Jerusalem's disaster, and a promise that the kingdom shall be the Lord's.

**Prerequisites:** [The prophets](../../08-prophets.md), [Lamentations](../major-prophets/lamentations.md), and [Exile and return](../../10-exile-and-return.md). Edom's ancestor is Esau; see [Genesis](../law-torah/genesis.md) and the Jacob stories.

## Dating and authorship

The book has no royal date. The situation it describes — strangers carrying off Jerusalem's wealth, Edom standing aside or participating, fugitives cut off at the crossroads — most naturally fits the Babylonian destruction of 586 BCE (see also Psalm 137:7 and the accusations in Lamentations and Ezekiel). Some scholars have argued for an earlier Edomite incursion. The prophet himself is otherwise unknown; the name is common. Traditional lists simply accept the heading.

## Context

Edom and Israel are brothers in the ancestral narrative (Esau and Jacob) and often rivals in the historical books. After 586, Edom's behavior — gloating, looting, or handing over fugitives — became a wound in Judah's memory. Obadiah is that memory turned into a prophetic lawsuit, ending in a reversal: Edom will be brought down from its high places, and exiles will possess land, including Esau's mountain.

## Outline

The twenty-one verses move in two waves:

- **1–14.** A report of a messenger among the nations: Edom will be made small. Pride in the clefts of the rock will not save it. Violence against a brother on "the day of his misfortune" is itemized in a series of "you should not have…" charges.
- **15–21.** The day of the Lord is near upon all nations; as you have done, it shall be done to you. Mount Zion will be a refuge; the house of Jacob will possess its possessions; saviors will go up, "and the kingdom shall be the Lord's."

## Key passages and ideas

The kinship charge is the moral center. Edom's crime is not only geopolitics; it is betrayal of a brother on a day of distress. The "clefts of the rock" fit Edom's mountainous territory (later associated with Petra in popular reading, though the book's geography is not a tourist caption). The closing "the kingdom shall be the Lord's" is a theological last word larger than a border dispute.

Obadiah shares language with [Jeremiah](../major-prophets/jeremiah.md) 49's Edom oracle, a relationship usually explained as common tradition or reuse. The shortest prophet is already an interpreter of other prophecy as well as a witness.

Because the book is so short, it is easily skipped or treated as a footnote to Edom's later disappearance. That would miss its place in the Twelve. It stands between Amos, who already names Edom's "pity" as a transgression (Amos 1:11), and Jonah, who will force the question of pity in the opposite direction, toward Nineveh. Obadiah's measure-for-measure theology is not the canon's only word about enemies, but it is a real word: there is a kind of watching a neighbor bleed that the Lord will not treat as neutrality. The "day" that came on Jerusalem will, the prophet says, come on the gloating as well.

## Themes and reception

Themes include pride, brotherhood, the ethics of watching a neighbor's fall, and the day of the Lord as measure-for-measure justice. Later readers have sometimes universalized Edom as a type of arrogant empire or of Rome; the first task is to hear Edom as Edom. The book's brevity is a teaching: a complete prophetic argument can be spoken in a page, and it still needs historical context.
