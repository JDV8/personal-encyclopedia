# Nahum

**Name and meaning.** Named for Nahum the Elkoshite (*Nachum*, "comfort"). The book is an oracle against Nineveh, capital of Assyria: a vision of the city's fall, written as a taunt, a dirge, and a theophany. Comfort, in this book, is comfort for Judah through the end of an oppressor.

**Prerequisites:** [The prophets](../../08-prophets.md) and [Jonah](jonah.md). Historical frame: [2 Kings](../history/2-kings.md) and the Assyrian crisis.

## Dating and authorship

Nineveh fell to a Median–Babylonian coalition in 612 BCE. The book assumes Assyrian power as a known cruelty and looks ahead to (or celebrates) the fall, so it is usually dated in the late seventh century, after the sack of Thebes in 663 (alluded to in 3:8) and at or near 612. Traditional reading takes Nahum as the prophet named. Scholarly study generally agrees on this historical window. The tone is unrelenting; there is no call for Nineveh to repent, which is why the book is often read as a counterpart, not a sequel, to Jonah.

## Context

Assyria had destroyed the northern kingdom, devastated Judah in 701, and built an empire famous (in its own inscriptions as well as in the Bible) for terror. For a small people under that shadow, an oracle of Nineveh's end is gospel of a severe kind. The book's poetry is some of the most vivid in the prophets: chariots, flashing swords, a city like a pool whose water drains away, lion's dens, and a wounded empire exposed.

## Outline

- **1.** A psalm-like theophany: the Lord is jealous and avenging, slow to anger and great in power; good news for Judah ("look, on the mountains the feet of one who brings good tidings").
- **2.** The assault on Nineveh: crimson shields, the palace melts, the city is stripped.
- **3.** A woe to the "city of bloodshed"; a comparison with Thebes; the famous image of the people scattered on the mountains, with no one to gather them; "all who hear the news of you clap their hands."

## Key passages and ideas

The opening theophany refuses to make God a tribal mascot, even as it announces judgment on Judah's enemy: the same Lord who is "good, a stronghold in a day of trouble" is also the one before whom rocks are broken. 1:15 (2:1 in Hebrew), the messenger of good news, is close to Isaiah 52:7 and belongs to a shared language of consolation. The book does not celebrate cruelty for its own sake; it celebrates the end of a cruel empire. Modern readers who are uneasy with oracles against nations should still hear the political experience behind the poetry: this is the voice of the trampled, not of a safe observer.

Read alongside Jonah, Nahum raises a first-principles question rather than a contradiction. Jonah's Nineveh repents and is spared; Nahum's Nineveh falls and is not invited to repent. The canon keeps both. One way to hear them together is historical: a city may turn, and an empire may later return to bloodshed and be judged. Another is literary: mercy and uncompromising justice are both claimed as the Lord's, and neither book is allowed to erase the other. A reader who likes only Jonah, or only Nahum, is preferring a simpler God than the Twelve will give.

## Themes and reception

Themes include the justice of God against empire, the poetry of siege, and comfort as the fall of a tyrant. Reception has been thinner than for Jonah or Micah, partly because the book is hard to preach in a moralizing key. It remains a first-principles witness that biblical prophecy includes not only "repent" but also "it is enough." Historical study of 612 and of Assyrian records gives the book a firm external anchor.
