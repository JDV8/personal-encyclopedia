# Leviticus

**Name and meaning.** *Leviticus* is the Latin form of a Greek title meaning "pertaining to the Levites." In Hebrew the book is *Vayikra*, "And he called," from its first word. Levites and priests are not the only subject, but the book is the Torah's manual of sacrifice, purity, and holiness: how a people lives with a holy God who now dwells among them.

**Prerequisites:** [Exodus](exodus.md), especially the tabernacle chapters, and [genres](../../../04-genres.md) on law. The story path does not retell Leviticus at length; read it here, then return to [Numbers](numbers.md).

## Dating and authorship

Traditional attribution is Mosaic, a set of speeches from the tent of meeting at Sinai. Historical-critical study assigns most of Leviticus to Priestly writers (P), with chapters 17–26 often singled out as the Holiness Code (H). Many scholars date the Priestly material to the exilic or Persian period, while others argue that priestly tradition is older and was edited later. The book's ritual system may preserve practices from the First Temple even if the prose was shaped later. As usual, traditional and scholarly frames should be held together, not used to cancel each other.

## Context

Leviticus assumes the tabernacle of Exodus and almost no travel. The people are still at Sinai. The question is no longer "Will God deliver?" but "How does a holy God live in the midst of a people that can become unclean, guilty, or profane?" The book answers with offerings, priestly duties, a calendar, rules about bodies and households, and a call to holiness that reaches daily life.

Ancient Near Eastern temples also had sacrifice, purity rules, and priestly guilds. Leviticus is distinctive in tying the whole system to one God, to covenant, and to ethics: the neighbor, the poor, the resident alien, and honest measures appear inside the same holiness speech as ritual rules. Whether one dates that connection early or late, it is a fact of the book as we have it.

## Outline

- **1–7.** The main offerings: burnt, grain, well-being (peace), purification (sin), and reparation (guilt), with priestly portions and procedures.
- **8–10.** Ordination of Aaron and his sons; the death of Nadab and Abihu, who offer "strange fire."
- **11–15.** Purity: clean and unclean animals; childbirth; skin disease and household mold; bodily discharges.
- **16.** The Day of Atonement (*Yom Kippur*): the holy place, the two goats, the sending away of the goat for Azazel.
- **17–26.** The Holiness Code: blood and slaughter; sexual and family law; neighbor-love; festivals; sabbath and jubilee years; blessings and curses.
- **27.** Vows and dedications, a coda on dedicated things.

## Key passages and ideas

Leviticus 16 is the ritual center of the year in later Jewish life. One goat is offered to the Lord; the other, the scapegoat in older English, is sent into the wilderness bearing the people's confessed sins. The chapter assumes that uncleanness and guilt accumulate and must be purged if God is to remain in the camp.

Leviticus 19 places cult and ethics side by side: reverence for parents, sabbath, a ban on idols, leaving gleanings for the poor and the alien, honest trade, and the command "you shall love your neighbor as yourself" (19:18). In the [Gospels](../../../new-testament/books/gospels/README.md) Jesus cites this command together with the love of God from Deuteronomy 6. The book's holiness refrain — "you shall be holy, for I the Lord your God am holy" — is not only about ritual separation. In this code it includes justice in the field and the court.

The Jubilee legislation in chapter 25 (release of debts and landed property in a fiftieth year) is among the most socially radical programs in the Torah. How fully it was ever practiced is debated; that it was written as law is not.

## Themes and reception

Themes include holiness, atonement, purity and impurity (which are not simply synonyms for morality and immorality), priesthood, sacred time, and the land as the Lord's. Blood is treated as life and is reserved for the altar, not for food.

Jewish reception made Leviticus a core of priestly and then rabbinic halakhah; tractates of the Mishnah unfold its concerns. Christian reception has been more uneven: some streams read the sacrificial system as fulfilled or replaced in Christ (especially via [Hebrews](../../../new-testament/books/general-epistles/hebrews.md)); others have ignored the book; more recent Christian reading has tried to recover holiness, justice, and the logic of atonement without treating Jewish ritual as a mere shadow. Historical study works on the priestly institutions of ancient Israel and on how purity maps a world. A first-principles reader should learn the system's own grammar before rushing to later theology.
