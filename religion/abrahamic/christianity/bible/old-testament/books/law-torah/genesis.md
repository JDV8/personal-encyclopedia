# Genesis

**Name and meaning.** *Genesis* is the Greek title used in the Septuagint, from a word for "origin" or "birth." In Hebrew the book is called *Bereshit*, "In the beginning," after its first word. It is the first book of the Torah / Pentateuch, and the opening of both the Tanakh and the Christian Old Testament.

**Prerequisites:** [What the Bible is](../../../01-what-the-bible-is.md), [canon](../../../02-canon-and-hebrew-bible-vs-old-testament.md), and [genres](../../../04-genres.md). The narrative lessons [Creation and Eden](../../01-creation-and-eden.md), [Cain, the Flood, and Babel](../../02-cain-abel-flood-babel.md), [Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob](../../03-abraham-isaac-jacob.md), and [Joseph](../../04-joseph.md) retell the main arcs.

## Dating and authorship

Traditional Jewish and Christian teaching attributes the Pentateuch to Moses, with a few later lines (such as the report of Moses' death in Deuteronomy) sometimes allowed as Joshua's or a later scribe's. In that frame Genesis is Mosaic prologue: origins before Israel exists as a people at Sinai.

Modern historical-critical study does not treat Genesis as a single Mosaic composition. For more than a century the dominant model was the Documentary Hypothesis: strands conventionally labeled J (Yahwist), E (Elohist), D (Deuteronomist, more visible in Deuteronomy), and P (Priestly) were woven together, with P often dated to the exilic or early Persian period and J earlier in the monarchy. Many scholars now speak instead of a Priestly compositional layer and a non-Priestly narrative, or of a long supplementary process, rather than four complete documents. Dates commonly proposed for the book's present form cluster in the mid-first millennium BCE, though older traditions and poems are widely thought to stand behind it. There is no scholarly consensus on a single year or a single author. The honest yield is: Genesis is both a unified book as we read it and a text with a history of composition.

## Context

Genesis is Israel's origin story set on a wider stage. Chapters 1–11 (often called the primeval history) move from creation to a populated, fractured world of nations. Chapters 12–50 narrow to one family: Abraham and Sarah, Isaac and Rebekah, Jacob (Israel) and his wives and sons, then Joseph in Egypt. The book ends with the family in Egypt, which is where [Exodus](exodus.md) begins.

It speaks in a world of Ancient Near Eastern origin stories and ancestor traditions. Comparison with Mesopotamian texts such as the *Enuma Elish* and the flood story in the *Epic of Gilgamesh* is a standard scholarly move. The point of the comparison is contrast as much as resemblance: Genesis affirms one God, a good material world, and human beings as images of God rather than slaves of the gods. Those are claims, not neutral description, and they are clearer when the neighboring literature is in view.

## Outline

- **1:1–2:3.** Creation in six days and sabbath rest. God (*Elohim*) speaks an ordered world into being; human beings, male and female, are made in God's image.
- **2:4–3:24.** The garden, the human pair, the prohibition, the serpent, expulsion east of Eden. The divine name is *YHWH Elohim* (the Lord God). This is a second account, not a smooth continuation of chapter 1; see [Creation and Eden](../../01-creation-and-eden.md).
- **4–11.** Cain and Abel; genealogies; the flood and Noah's covenant; the tower of Babel; the line that leads to Abram. See [Cain, the Flood, and Babel](../../02-cain-abel-flood-babel.md).
- **12–25.** The call of Abram; the covenant of promise (land, people, blessing to the nations); Hagar and Ishmael; the covenant of circumcision; Sodom; the binding of Isaac; the burial of Sarah; Isaac's marriage. See [Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob](../../03-abraham-isaac-jacob.md).
- **25–36.** Jacob and Esau; the stolen blessing; Jacob's flight, wives, and renaming as Israel; the return and the wound at the Jabbok; the violence at Shechem.
- **37–50.** Joseph sold into Egypt; prison and Pharaoh's court; famine; the family's descent into Egypt; Jacob's blessings; Joseph's death and the request that his bones return. See [Joseph](../../04-joseph.md).

## Key passages and ideas

The opening sentence — in the beginning, God created the heavens and the earth — became a cornerstone of later Jewish, Christian, and Islamic theology. Genesis 1's refrain that creation is "good," and finally "very good," is a claim about the status of the material world. The sabbath of the seventh day is built into the structure of time, not added as a later ritual footnote.

The promise to Abraham in 12:1–3 (a people, a land, and blessing that reaches other families of the earth) is the hinge on which the rest of the Hebrew Bible turns. Covenant is then given signs and rites: the smoking firepot and torch in chapter 15; circumcision in chapter 17. The binding of Isaac in chapter 22 is among the most interpreted scenes in the whole library — in Jewish tradition as the *Aqedah*, in Christian typological reading as a figure of a son's offering, in modern moral philosophy as a test case for religious obedience.

Joseph's story is a long, nearly novelistic close: hidden identity, reunion, and a theological summary in 50:20, that what the brothers meant for harm God meant for good. The book ends with a coffin in Egypt, not with possession of the land. Promise is in force; fulfillment is not yet.

## Themes and reception

Recurring themes include blessing and curse, brotherly rivalry, barrenness and unexpected birth, divine promise under threat, and the tension between human scheming and God's purpose. Genealogy is not decoration; it is how the book maps peoples and keeps the promise line visible.

Jewish reading takes Genesis as Torah: it is studied with rabbinic commentary as instruction, not only as narrative. Christian reading often emphasizes creation, fall, and promise as the preface to the [Gospels](../../../new-testament/books/gospels/README.md) and to [Paul](../../../new-testament/books/pauline-epistles/README.md)'s Adam–Christ contrast. Historical-critical reading asks about sources, Ancient Near Eastern parallels, and what kind of history (if any) the patriarchal stories are. This path keeps those frames in view rather than collapsing them.
