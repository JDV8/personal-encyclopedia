# Exodus

**Name and meaning.** *Exodus* is the Latin form of the Greek title, "a going out." In Hebrew the book is *Shemot*, "Names," from its opening words about the names of the sons of Israel who came to Egypt. It is the second book of the Torah and the narrative heart of Israel's memory: deliverance from bondage, covenant at a mountain, and the beginning of a life under law and worship.

**Prerequisites:** [Genesis](genesis.md), and the story lesson [Moses, the Exodus, and Sinai](../../05-moses-exodus-and-sinai.md). Foundations: [languages, dating, and composition](../../../03-languages-dating-and-composition.md).

## Dating and authorship

Traditionally the book is Mosaic, a first-person-adjacent record of events Moses led. Scholarly models again see a composite text: Priestly material is especially visible in the tabernacle chapters (25–31 and 35–40) and in some plague and passover legislation; covenantal and narrative strands are often assigned to earlier or non-Priestly tradition. The present form is commonly placed in the first millennium BCE, with debate over how much older memory it preserves.

The historical question — was there an exodus, of what size, and when — is not the same as the literary one. Proposed dates have included a fifteenth-century and a thirteenth-century BCE setting (the latter often tied to the mention of the store-cities Pithom and Rameses, and to Egypt's Nineteenth Dynasty). Direct Egyptian evidence for a mass Israelite departure is lacking, and archaeology of early Israel is variously read. Many scholars think a smaller escape or a group experience was later told as the story of a people. Traditional reading takes the narrative as historical deliverance. This overview reports both the story and the debate; it does not pretend the debate is settled.

## Context

Exodus follows Genesis without a smooth historical footnote. A new king arises who does not know Joseph. The family has become a people, and that people is enslaved. The book is Israel's answer to the question "Who are we?" We are the people the Lord brought out of Egypt and bound to himself at Sinai.

The setting assumes New Kingdom Egypt as a place of brick, store-cities, and royal power, and a wilderness south of Canaan as the space between bondage and the land. Neighboring treaty forms (especially Hittite suzerainty treaties) have been compared with the covenant structure of Sinai and, still more, with [Deuteronomy](deuteronomy.md): preamble, stipulations, blessings and curses. Whether or not one accepts a direct literary borrowing, the comparison helps a reader see that biblical covenant is political and religious at once.

## Outline

- **1–4.** Oppression in Egypt; the birth and flight of Moses; the burning bush; the revelation of the divine name; Moses' return with Aaron.
- **5–15.** The confrontation with Pharaoh; the plagues; Passover and the death of the firstborn; the departure; the sea crossing and the song of Moses and Miriam.
- **16–18.** Wilderness provision (manna, water, quail); Amalek; Jethro's advice about judges.
- **19–24.** Arrival at Sinai; the Decalogue; the Covenant Code; the covenant ceremony with blood and a shared meal.
- **25–31.** Instructions for the tabernacle, its furnishings, and the priesthood.
- **32–34.** The golden calf; Moses' intercession; broken and renewed tablets; the proclamation of the Lord's name as merciful and just.
- **35–40.** The tabernacle is built; the cloud and glory fill it.

## Key passages and ideas

The divine name revealed at the bush (3:14, often rendered "I AM WHO I AM" or "I will be what I will be") is one of the most discussed sentences in the Bible. It is tied to the four-letter name [YHWH](../../../glossary/yhwh.md), which Jewish tradition does not pronounce and Christian Bibles often render as "the Lord."

The Passover (chapter 12) becomes a permanent rite: a lamb, unleavened bread, a night of watching, a story told to children. Christian liturgy will reread it through the [Passion](../../../new-testament/stories/06-passion-week.md) and the Last Supper; Jewish liturgy keeps it as the festival of freedom. Both rereadings depend on the Exodus text itself being a story that already commands its own remembrance.

The Decalogue (chapter 20, with a close parallel in Deuteronomy 5) and the Covenant Code (21–23) are Israel's first large legal collections in narrative sequence. Law here is not a later appendix to religion; it is how a delivered people lives with the God who delivered them. The golden calf episode shows how quickly worship can be turned into a controllable image, and how covenant can be broken and, at Moses' pleading, restored. The book ends not with the land but with a mobile sanctuary: God dwells in the midst of a traveling people.

## Themes and reception

Major themes include oppression and release, the name and character of God, covenant, law, presence, and the danger of idolatry. Pharaoh is a political and theological figure: a hard heart, a claim to power that will not acknowledge the Lord.

In Jewish life Exodus is the foundation of identity and of Passover. In Christian theology it is a pattern of redemption (bondage, lamb, passage through water) that the New Testament applies to Jesus and to baptism, without simply erasing the Jewish reading. Liberation theologies have taken the slaves' cry and the departure from Egypt as a political as well as a spiritual pattern. Historical study keeps asking what can be known about the event behind the story. All of those receptions start from a book that is itself already theology in the form of narrative.
