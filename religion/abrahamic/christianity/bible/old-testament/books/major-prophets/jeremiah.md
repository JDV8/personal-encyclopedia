# Jeremiah

**Name and meaning.** Named for Jeremiah son of Hilkiah (Hebrew *Yirmeyahu*), a priest from Anathoth who prophesied in the last decades of Judah, from Josiah's reign through the fall of Jerusalem and after. The book is the most biographical of the prophetic scrolls: oracles, sermons, stories of conflict, and the prophet's own laments (the "confessions").

**Prerequisites:** [The prophets](../../08-prophets.md), [Deuteronomy](../law-torah/deuteronomy.md), [2 Kings](../history/2-kings.md), and [Exile and return](../../10-exile-and-return.md).

## Dating and authorship

The book names Jeremiah as the source of its words and Baruch son of Neriah as the scribe who wrote them down (chapter 36 is the classic scene: a scroll burned by King Jehoiakim and rewritten). Traditional reading takes that claim at face value. Scholarly study generally accepts a historical Jeremiah in the late seventh and early sixth centuries BCE, and also sees substantial later editing and expansion. The Greek Septuagint text of Jeremiah is shorter than the Masoretic Hebrew and is arranged differently, which is hard evidence that the book circulated in more than one edition. Poetic oracles, prose sermons in a Deuteronomistic style, and biographical narratives are often distinguished as layers or genres within the scroll.

## Context

Jeremiah's ministry spans Josiah's reform, the death of Josiah at Megiddo, the brief reigns of Jehoahaz, Jehoiakim, Jehoiachin, and Zedekiah, the first deportation in 597, the destruction of 586, and a forced flight to Egypt. The international field is Egypt versus Babylon; Jeremiah's unpopular counsel is to submit to Babylon as the Lord's instrument. He is accused of treason, beaten, imprisoned, and thrown into a cistern. After the fall he is taken to Egypt, against his advice, with a remnant of the people.

## Outline

The Hebrew order (followed here) is not strictly chronological:

- **1–10.** Call (a prophet to the nations, a mouth touched, visions of an almond rod and a boiling pot); temple sermon; early oracles of judgment; "the fountain of living water."
- **11–20.** Covenant broken; plots in Anathoth; the confessions; the potter; the broken flask; conflict with Pashhur.
- **21–29.** Oracles to kings; the righteous branch; the letter to the exiles (chapter 29); conflict with the prophet Hananiah.
- **30–33.** The "book of consolation": restoration, a new covenant written on the heart (31:31–34), a future for David's line and for the Levitical priests.
- **34–45.** Narratives of the last years, the burned scroll, the fall, Gedaliah, the flight to Egypt; a word to Baruch.
- **46–51.** Oracles against the nations, ending with a long oracle against Babylon.
- **52.** A historical appendix close to 2 Kings 24–25.

## Key passages and ideas

The temple sermon (chapters 7 and 26) attacks trust in the temple as a talisman: "the temple of the Lord" is not enough if justice is neglected and other gods are served. Shiloh, Jeremiah says, is the warning. The letter to the exiles (29:4–14) tells them to build houses, plant gardens, and seek the peace of the city where they have been carried; the famous "plans to give you a future and a hope" belongs in that setting of a seventy-year wait, not as a free-floating slogan.

The new covenant of 31:31–34 is one of the load-bearing texts of both later Judaism and the New Testament (the [Last Supper](../../../new-testament/stories/06-passion-week.md) and [Hebrews](../../../new-testament/books/general-epistles/hebrews.md) take it up). In Jeremiah it is a promise after judgment: the law internalized, knowledge of the Lord widespread, sins remembered no more. The confessions (for example 12, 15, 20) show a prophet who curses the day of his birth and feels the word as a fire in the bones. Prophecy here is not a calm career.

## Themes and reception

Themes include the word as a burden, false versus true prophecy, the temple, political realism under empire, suffering, and hope on the far side of judgment. The book's textual history (Hebrew and Greek) is a first-principles lesson in how scripture was transmitted.

Later tradition linked Jeremiah with [Lamentations](lamentations.md) (not a settled scholarly conclusion). Jesus' question, "Who do people say that I am?" includes the answer "Jeremiah" in [Matthew](../../../new-testament/books/gospels/matthew.md), a sign of how the prophet's suffering and judgment were remembered. The new-covenant language became central to Christian self-understanding and remains a Jewish eschatological hope. Historical study of the last years of Judah leans heavily on this book and on the Babylonian chronicles.
