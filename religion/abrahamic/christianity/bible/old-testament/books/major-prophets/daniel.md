# Daniel

**Name and meaning.** Named for Daniel, a Judean taken to Babylon, who becomes a court interpreter of dreams and, in the second half of the book, a seer of visions. In the Christian Old Testament Daniel is a major prophet; in the Tanakh he stands among the Writings, a signal that the book's genre and date have long been thought about carefully. The Hebrew/Aramaic book is the basis here. The Greek [additions](../../../deuterocanon/additions-to-daniel.md) (the Prayer of Azariah and the Song of the Three, Susanna, Bel and the Dragon) are treated in the deuterocanon.

**Prerequisites:** [Exile and return](../../10-exile-and-return.md), [genres](../../../04-genres.md) on apocalyptic, and [2 Kings](../history/2-kings.md). Helpful later companion: [Revelation](../../../new-testament/books/prophecy/revelation.md).

## Dating and authorship

Traditional reading takes the book as the sixth-century work of the historical Daniel, with chapters 7–12 as genuine predictive prophecy of later empires down to the Greek period and beyond. The mainstream of modern historical-critical study dates the book, at least in its final form, to the 160s BCE, during the persecution under Antiochus IV Epiphanes, because chapter 11 tracks Seleucid history with great precision up to that crisis and then seems to diverge from what we know of Antiochus' death. On this view, the court tales (1–6) may be older diaspora stories, and the visions (7–12) are apocalyptic encouragement for people facing the loss of temple and Torah under a Hellenistic king. The book is written partly in Hebrew and partly in Aramaic (2:4b–7:28), another clue to a complex history. Both the traditional and the critical accounts should be taught; they disagree about date and genre, not about the book's power to demand faithfulness under empire.

## Context

The narrative setting is the Babylonian and Persian courts. The political horizon of the visions is a sequence of kingdoms, usually identified as Babylon, Media, Persia, and Greece (or, in some traditional schemes, Babylon, Medo-Persia, Greece, and Rome). The crisis behind the later chapters, on the critical dating, is the Maccabean revolt, also narrated in [1 Maccabees](../../../deuterocanon/1-maccabees.md). The genre of chapters 7–12 is apocalyptic: symbolic visions, angelic interpretation, a heavenly book, and a hope that does not depend on ordinary political recovery alone.

## Outline

- **1–6.** Court tales: Daniel and his companions refuse the king's food; Nebuchadnezzar's dream of a statue of four metals; the fiery furnace; the tree dream and the king's humiliation; Belshazzar's feast and the writing on the wall; Daniel in the lions' den under Darius.
- **7.** Four beasts from the sea; the "Ancient of Days"; "one like a son of man" coming with the clouds.
- **8.** The ram and the goat: Medo-Persia and Greece, and a little horn that profanes the sanctuary.
- **9.** Daniel's prayer of confession; the seventy weeks.
- **10–12.** A final vision of kings of the north and south; a time of anguish; resurrection of many; sealed words.

## Key passages and ideas

The tales teach fidelity in diaspora: names, food, prayer toward Jerusalem, and refusal of idolatry, with deliverance that is real but not guaranteed in advance (the three companions' "even if he does not" in chapter 3 is the book's moral center as much as the miracle is). The writing on the wall (chapter 5) is a judgment on imperial blasphemy.

The "one like a son of man" in 7:13–14, given dominion and a kingdom that will not pass away, is a key text for later messianic and apocalyptic hope, and for Jesus' own use of "[Son of Man](../../../glossary/son-of-man.md)" in the [Gospels](../../../new-testament/books/gospels/README.md). Interpretations vary: a collective symbol of the holy ones, a heavenly individual, the Messiah. Chapter 12 contains the Hebrew Bible's clearest statement of a double resurrection: some to everlasting life, some to shame. The seventy weeks of chapter 9 have generated a long history of chronological schemes in both Jewish and Christian interpretation; humility is in order, given the history of confident and conflicting calculations.

## Themes and reception

Themes include faithfulness under empire, the limits of royal power, divine sovereignty over history, apocalyptic hope, and resurrection. The book shaped later Jewish apocalyptic and the [Revelation](../../../new-testament/books/prophecy/revelation.md) of John. It has also been a playground for predictive timetables, which is a reception history worth studying and a caution worth heeding. Canonical placement (Prophets versus Writings) already encodes a difference of emphasis: prophet of future empires, or wisdom and story for a people under foreign rule.
