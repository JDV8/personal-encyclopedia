# Isaiah

**Name and meaning.** Named for Isaiah son of Amoz (Hebrew *Yeshayahu*, "the Lord is salvation"), a prophet in eighth-century Judah. The book that bears his name is the longest and most theologically dense of the Latter Prophets: judgment and hope for Judah, oracles concerning the nations, and, in its later chapters, some of the most influential consolation literature in the Bible. In the Tanakh it opens the Latter Prophets; in Christian Bibles it opens the major prophets.

**Prerequisites:** [The prophets](../../08-prophets.md), [1–2 Kings](../history/1-kings.md), and [Exile and return](../../10-exile-and-return.md).

## Dating and authorship

Traditional Jewish and Christian teaching treats the book as the work of the eighth-century Isaiah, with later chapters as genuine prediction of exile and restoration. Modern historical-critical study, since the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries (and in a standard form associated with Bernhard Duhm), commonly distinguishes at least three major phases:

- **Isaiah 1–39** (often "First Isaiah"): rooted in the ministry of Isaiah of Jerusalem in the reigns of Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah (see 1:1), during the Assyrian crisis of the eighth century BCE.
- **Isaiah 40–55** ("Second Isaiah" or Deutero-Isaiah): anonymous prophecy of consolation addressed to exiles in Babylon, speaking of Cyrus by name (44:28; 45:1) as the Lord's anointed, usually dated near the end of the exile (mid-sixth century).
- **Isaiah 56–66** ("Third Isaiah" or Trito-Isaiah): oracles that seem to address a returned community struggling over temple, fasting, and inclusion, often dated to the early Persian period.

Many scholars also see later additions inside 1–39 (for example, some apocalyptic material in 24–27) and editorial shaping that binds the whole into one scroll. The Dead Sea Scrolls (notably the Great Isaiah Scroll) already present a single book. Traditional reading hears one prophet's voice. Critical reading hears a school or a tradition that continued to speak in Isaiah's name. This path reports both and reads the finished book as a unity of argument as well as a history of composition.

## Context

First Isaiah's world is Judah under Assyrian pressure: the Syro-Ephraimite war, Ahaz's fatal trust in Assyria, the fall of the northern kingdom, and Sennacherib's invasion in 701 BCE (also told in 2 Kings 18–19). The prophet's critique of empty worship, social injustice, and foolish foreign policy belongs to that crisis. Second Isaiah's world is exile: "comfort, comfort my people," a highway in the wilderness, and a monotheism stated with unusual sharpness against the idols of Babylon. Third Isaiah's world is a disappointing restoration, with insiders and outsiders, and a vision of a house of prayer for all peoples.

## Outline

- **1–12.** Accusations against Judah; the temple vision of chapter 6; the Immanuel sign (7); promises of a Davidic shoot (9, 11); a hymn of thanksgiving.
- **13–23.** Oracles against the nations (Babylon, Moab, Damascus, Egypt, Tyre, and others).
- **24–27.** A wider, often called "apocalyptic," vision of cosmic judgment and deliverance.
- **28–39.** Further woe and hope; Hezekiah narratives overlapping 2 Kings; the Babylonian envoys and the announcement of exile.
- **40–55.** Consolation; the uniqueness of God as creator and redeemer; Cyrus; the Servant poems (42:1–9; 49:1–6; 50:4–11; 52:13–53:12).
- **56–66.** Ethics, sabbath, inclusion of foreigners and eunuchs; critique of failed leadership; a new heavens and a new earth.

## Key passages and ideas

Isaiah 6 — the vision of the Lord in the temple, the trisagion ("holy, holy, holy"), the prophet's unclean lips, and a sending that will not be heard — is a template for prophetic vocation. The Immanuel oracle (7:14) is a sign to Ahaz in the eighth century; [Matthew](../../../new-testament/books/gospels/matthew.md) rereads it of Jesus' birth. Both contexts belong in view. The child-and-throne poems of chapters 9 and 11 became central to Jewish and Christian messianic hope.

The Servant of 52:13–53:12, who suffers, is pierced, and bears the sins of many, is the most contested passage in the book. Jewish interpretation has often identified the servant as Israel, or as a righteous remnant, or in some cases as an individual (a prophet, a teacher). Christian interpretation has read the poem of Jesus' Passion. The poem itself does not name the servant. Honest teaching presents the text, the main lines of reception, and the fact of disagreement.

"Comfort" (40:1), the highway (40:3, taken up by the Gospels of John the Baptist), the ridicule of idols, Cyrus as *mashiach* (anointed) in 45:1, and "a house of prayer for all peoples" (56:7) are further load-bearing texts. The book's monotheism in the exilic chapters is not a new god but a newly explicit claim: besides the Lord there is no savior.

## Themes and reception

Themes include holiness, justice, trust versus political anxiety, remnant, messianic hope, monotheism, suffering and witness, and inclusion. Isaiah is quoted or echoed constantly in the New Testament. Jewish liturgy and later prophecy also live in its language. Historical-critical division of the book should not prevent a reader from hearing how 1–39's judgment and 40–66's comfort were meant to be read as one scroll of warning and hope.
