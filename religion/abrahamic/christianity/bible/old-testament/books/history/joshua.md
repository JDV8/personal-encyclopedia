# Joshua

**Name and meaning.** The book is named for Joshua son of Nun, Moses' successor, whose name (Hebrew *Yehoshua*, "the Lord saves") is later shortened to Yeshua — the same name that comes into Greek as *Iesous*, Jesus. In the Tanakh it opens the Former Prophets; in the Christian Old Testament it opens the historical books.

**Prerequisites:** [Deuteronomy](../law-torah/deuteronomy.md) and the story lesson [Joshua and Judges](../../06-joshua-and-judges.md).

## Dating and authorship

Traditional attribution sometimes names Joshua himself, or Samuel, as writer. Scholarly study usually treats Joshua as part of a Deuteronomistic history (Joshua–Kings) edited in the late monarchy and exile, using older conquest and tribal traditions. The book's ideology — complete conquest as obedience to Deuteronomy — sits in tension with its own notices that much land remained unconquered, and with [Judges](judges.md), which assumes a slower, incomplete occupation. Archaeology of Late Bronze and early Iron Age Canaan is a major debate: some sites show destruction in a plausible window, many do not, and alternative models (gradual settlement, peasant revolt, mixed immigration) are widely discussed. Traditional reading takes the campaigns as historical. This overview reports the book's own story and the critical questions around it.

## Context

The setting is the entry into the land after Moses' death, from the crossing of the Jordan to a covenant ceremony at Shechem. The land is already inhabited. The book is not a modern campaign history; it is a theological claim that the Lord gave Israel the land, and that obedience, herem (the ban), and covenant loyalty are the terms of that gift.

## Outline

- **1–5.** Commission of Joshua; spies and Rahab in Jericho; crossing the Jordan; circumcision and Passover at Gilgal; the commander of the Lord's army.
- **6–12.** Jericho and Ai; the covenant reading at Mounts Ebal and Gerizim; the Gibeonite treaty; southern and northern campaigns; a list of defeated kings.
- **13–21.** Allotment of the land by tribe; Caleb's inheritance; cities of refuge and Levitical cities; the eastern tribes' altar and the dispute that almost becomes civil war.
- **22–24.** Joshua's farewells; covenant at Shechem; Joshua's death; Joseph's bones buried at last.

## Key passages and ideas

Jericho (chapter 6) is the iconic scene: walls, seven days, ram's horns, a city devoted to destruction, and Rahab's household spared because she hid the spies and confessed the Lord's power. The story of Ai (7–8) ties defeat to Achan's taking of devoted things: the land gift is morally conditional even in the moment of conquest. Joshua 24 at Shechem gathers the tribes to choose whom they will serve, with Joshua's "as for me and my house, we will serve the Lord." The book begins and ends in covenant speech.

The violence of herem cannot be softened away. Later Jewish and Christian readers have interpreted it typologically, morally, historically, or as a problem to be wrestled with. A first-principles reading should first hear what the book itself claims: the land is the Lord's to give, and the campaigns are narrated as an act of that giving, not as ordinary imperialism.

## Themes and reception

Themes include succession after Moses, the gift of the land, obedience and memory, and the incomplete character of "completion." The name Joshua/Jesus made typological reading almost inevitable in Christian tradition (a Joshua who brings the people into rest, as in [Hebrews](../../../new-testament/books/general-epistles/hebrews.md)). Jewish reading keeps the book among the Prophets and as the start of Israel's life in the land. Historical study tests the conquest model against settlement archaeology. All three belong in view.
