# Nehemiah

**Name and meaning.** Named for Nehemiah son of Hacaliah, cupbearer to a Persian king (usually identified as Artaxerxes I), later governor of Judah, who rebuilds Jerusalem's wall and, with Ezra, leads a covenant renewal. In Hebrew tradition this is the second part of Ezra–Nehemiah.

**Prerequisites:** [Ezra](ezra.md) and [Exile and return](../../10-exile-and-return.md).

## Dating and authorship

A first-person "Nehemiah memoir" is one of the most distinctive voices in the Bible: pragmatic, prayerful, and concerned with credit ("remember me, O my God, for good"). The memoir was compiled with lists, a covenant document, and the Ezra materials. Nehemiah's mission is often dated to 445 BCE and a second visit some years later. See [Ezra](ezra.md) for the chronological debate.

## Context

Nearly a century after the first return, Jerusalem's walls are still broken. A city without a wall in the ancient world is not secure and is not quite a city. Nehemiah obtains a commission, surveys the ruins by night, and organizes a rebuild under armed opposition from Sanballat, Tobiah, and others. The book is as much about internal social reform (debt, Sabbath commerce, mixed marriages, temple support) as about masonry.

## Outline

- **1–2.** News from Jerusalem; Nehemiah's prayer; the request to the king; the night inspection.
- **3–7.** The wall built, with a roster of workers; opposition and the workers armed; social reform during the famine of chapter 5; plots; the wall finished; a census list (parallel to Ezra 2).
- **8–10.** The public reading of the Torah by Ezra; the festival of Booths; a penitential prayer; a sealed covenant.
- **11–13.** Resettlement of the city; dedication of the wall; Nehemiah's second reform (temple rooms, tithes, Sabbath, marriages).

## Key passages and ideas

Nehemiah 8 is a foundational scene for later Judaism: the people gather, the book of the law is opened, it is read and explained, and the hearers weep, then are told to rejoice. Torah here is public, interpreted, and communal. The festival of Booths is kept with a comment that it had not been done so since the days of Joshua.

Chapter 5 shows Nehemiah confronting Judahite nobles who were exacting interest and enslaving their own people. He is not only a wall-builder. Chapter 13, with its Sabbath enforcement and harsh treatment of mixed marriages (including a grandson of the high priest), raises the same identity questions as Ezra 9–10, in a more personal, memoir-like voice.

The memoir's prayers are part of its politics. Nehemiah constantly interrupts the narrative to ask God to remember him, or to remember his opponents, which can sound unsaintly and is historically precious: we hear a governor interpreting his own work as service that should be counted. The wall dedication (chapter 12) with two thanksgiving choirs marching in opposite directions on the wall is the memoir's liturgical climax. It also makes a point the lists have been making all along: priests, Levites, and lay families have to live in the city, not only visit it, if Jerusalem is to be more than a shrine in ruins. Restoration, in this book, is demographic and economic as well as religious.

## Themes and reception

Themes include leadership, prayer, opposition, the wall as a sign of restored peoplehood, Torah reading, and social justice inside the community. The book has been a favorite of practical leadership literature, sometimes at the cost of its harder ethnic and religious boundaries. Historical study uses it as a prime source for mid-fifth-century Yehud, alongside what is known of the Persian western provinces. Read it with Ezra, and with the post-exilic prophets, as one conversation about what the restored community should be.
