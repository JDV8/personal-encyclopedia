# Ruth

**Name and meaning.** The book is named for Ruth the Moabite, its central figure. In the Christian Old Testament it stands after Judges, because it is set "in the days when the judges ruled." In the Tanakh it is among the Writings, one of the Five Scrolls (*Megillot*), and is read at the festival of Weeks (Shavuot).

**Prerequisites:** [Judges](judges.md). The story is short enough to read in one sitting alongside this overview.

## Dating and authorship

The book is anonymous. Traditional guesses have included Samuel. Scholarly dates range from the early monarchy (because of its interest in David's ancestry) to the post-exilic period (because a story that honors a Moabite great-grandmother of David can be read as a reply to later, stricter marriage policies in [Ezra](ezra.md) and [Nehemiah](nehemiah.md)). Hebrew style and legal custom have been used on both sides. The Davidic genealogy at the end is clearly a reason the story was kept.

## Context

A famine in Judah drives Elimelech's family to Moab. After the deaths of Elimelech and his two sons, his widow Naomi starts home to Bethlehem. One daughter-in-law, Orpah, returns to her people; Ruth clings to Naomi. The rest is a harvest story of gleaning, protection, a kinsman-redeemer (*goel*), and a marriage that places a Moabite woman in the line of [David](../../../glossary/david.md).

Moab is not a neutral origin in the Torah: Moabites are often rivals, and Deuteronomy 23 restricts their entry into the assembly. Ruth's loyalty and the community's eventual blessing therefore carry a quiet argument about the scope of belonging.

## Outline

- **1.** Famine, death, and the return: Ruth's speech of allegiance to Naomi, Naomi's people, and Naomi's God.
- **2.** Gleaning in Boaz's field; kindness within the law of gleaning.
- **3.** The threshing-floor scene: Ruth's request that Boaz spread his cloak over her as redeemer.
- **4.** The legal scene at the gate; the nearer kinsman declines; marriage; the birth of Obed; the genealogy down to David.

## Key passages and ideas

Ruth 1:16–17 — Ruth's refusal to leave Naomi, and her "your people shall be my people, and your God my God" — is one of the most quoted pledges in the Bible. It is a vow of solidarity, not a romance line, though Christian and later Jewish retellings have often emphasized the Boaz–Ruth marriage. The *goel* institution (the kinsman who redeems land and name) is the legal engine of chapters 3–4. The book is careful about reputation, witnesses, and the shoe-exchange custom at the gate.

The closing genealogy (4:18–22) ties the story to Perez (and so to Genesis' Judah stories) and to David. [Matthew](../../../new-testament/books/gospels/matthew.md) will include Ruth in Jesus' genealogy, one of the few women named there.

The legal and social texture is unusually precise for so short a tale. Gleaning is not charity invented for the plot; it is the Torah's provision (Leviticus 19; Deuteronomy 24) made visible in a field. Boaz's instructions to his workers, the public negotiation with the nearer kinsman, and the blessing of the elders at the gate all show a community whose ordinary procedures still work — the opposite of Gibeah in Judges 19. The nearer kinsman's refusal, because redeeming the land would jeopardize his own inheritance, keeps the cost of hesed in view. Loyalty here is not costless feeling. It is public, legal, and expensive, and the book honors it.

## Themes and reception

Themes include hesed (loyal kindness) practiced by a foreigner and by a Bethlehem landowner, the vulnerability of widows, the Torah's harvest provisions actually being used, and the unexpected means by which David's line arrives. The book is a deliberate contrast to the chaos at the end of Judges: here there is no king yet, but there is integrity in a small town.

Jewish liturgy reads it at Shavuot, when the harvest and the giving of Torah are both in view. Christian reading has emphasized Davidic (and so messianic) ancestry and the inclusion of a Gentile. Literary readers admire its construction. Historical readers debate its date and its stance toward intermarriage. None of those uses replaces reading the four chapters themselves.
