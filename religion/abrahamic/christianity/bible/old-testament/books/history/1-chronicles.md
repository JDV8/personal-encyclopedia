# 1 Chronicles

**Name and meaning.** In Hebrew, 1 and 2 Chronicles are *Divrei Hayamim*, "the events of the days." The Greek title *Paraleipomena* means "things left out" or "things alongside," as if Chronicles were a supplement to Samuel–Kings. It is better read as a retelling with its own purpose. In the Tanakh it stands at the end of the Writings; in Christian Bibles it follows Kings.

**Prerequisites:** [2 Samuel](2-samuel.md) and [1 Kings](1-kings.md), so that the retelling is audible, and [Exile and return](../../10-exile-and-return.md).

## Dating and authorship

The book is anonymous. Traditional ascription sometimes names Ezra. Scholarly consensus places Chronicles (often with Ezra–Nehemiah as a related or subsequent work) in the Persian period, commonly the fourth century BCE, though dates a century earlier or later are argued. The author, often called "the Chronicler," used Samuel–Kings and other sources (genealogies, Levitical lists) and reshaped them for a community living after the exile, with a rebuilt temple and no independent Davidic king.

## Context

The implied audience is post-exilic Judah (Yehud) under Persia: a people that needs to know who belongs to Israel, how worship is to be ordered, and how the Davidic and temple traditions still matter when the political empire is gone. The northern kingdom is almost offstage. Sin is not denied, but the accent falls on repentance, proper cult, and immediate blessing or disaster.

## Outline

- **1–9.** Genealogies from Adam to the post-exilic community: tribes, especially Judah, Levi, and Benjamin; Saul's line; resettled Jerusalem.
- **10.** Saul's death, treated briefly as prelude.
- **11–20.** David's rise, his mighty men, the ark, wars — with Bathsheba and the court scandals largely omitted.
- **21–29.** The census and plague; the site of the temple; extensive preparations, Levitical organization, and David's speeches charging Solomon and the people; David's death.

## Key passages and ideas

The genealogies are the book's first message: this community is continuous with Israel from Adam, and Levites and temple personnel have a documented place. Skipping them is like skipping the wiring diagram of a rebuilt house.

David is idealized as the founder of the temple system, even though he does not build the house. The Chronicler's David organizes singers, gatekeepers, and priests. Chapter 21 retells the census story, with a notable difference: "Satan" (or "an adversary") stands up against Israel, where 2 Samuel 24 had said the Lord incited David. The purchase of the threshing floor is again the hinge toward the temple.

David's prayer and the people's offerings in chapters 28–29 present a willing, well-ordered transition to Solomon. The book's hope is cultic and communal more than military.

Comparison with Samuel is the right method, not a suspicion. The Chronicler is not hiding the fact that other stories exist; he is writing for a different need. A community that already knows about Bathsheba and Absalom may need, more urgently, to know how the singers are organized and why the temple site is holy. The long lists of gatekeepers and musicians are a claim that worship is a craft with a pedigree, not an improvisation of the Persian period. When the book says that the Lord "broke through" David's enemies, or that seeking the Lord brings success, it is teaching a pattern for life after exile, when there is no empire of David to lean on and there is a sanctuary to maintain.

## Themes and reception

Themes include continuity, the temple and its personnel, immediate retribution, and a purified memory of David. Compared with Samuel, Chronicles is less tragic and more liturgical. That is a choice, not a defect.

Jewish tradition kept it as scripture but generally studied Samuel–Kings more for the narrative. Christian lectionaries have often neglected it. Modern study has recovered the Chronicler as a serious theologian of restoration, not a mere copyist. Read it after Samuel–Kings, not instead of them.
