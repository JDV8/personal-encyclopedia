# 1 Samuel

**Name and meaning.** In Hebrew, 1 and 2 Samuel are a single book, later divided. The title comes from Samuel, the prophet and king-maker who dominates the first half. The narrative then shifts to Saul and to the rise of David. In the Tanakh this is among the Former Prophets; in Christian Bibles it is a historical book.

**Prerequisites:** [Judges](judges.md) and [Ruth](ruth.md). Story companion: [Saul, David, and Solomon](../../07-saul-david-solomon.md).

## Dating and authorship

Traditional ascriptions name Samuel for the early chapters and then other prophets (Gad, Nathan) for the rest. Scholarly study sees a long compositional history: sources such as an ark narrative, pro- and anti-monarchic traditions about the demand for a king, and a history of David's rise, edited in a Deuteronomistic frame and perhaps again later. The present division into two books is Greek and Latin. The events, if taken in a historical framework, belong to the eleventh and early tenth centuries BCE; the prose as we have it is later.

## Context

Israel is under Philistine pressure. The old order of judges and a sanctuary at Shiloh is failing. The people ask for a king "like the nations." The book is theologically double-minded in the best sense: monarchy is both a rejection of the Lord's kingship and a gift the Lord can use. Saul is the first anointed king; David is the unexpected second.

## Outline

- **1–7.** Hannah's prayer and the birth of Samuel; Eli's corrupt sons; the call of Samuel; the ark captured and returned; Samuel as judge.
- **8–15.** The demand for a king; Saul anointed and confirmed; Saul's failures (unauthorized sacrifice; the Amalek command) and his rejection.
- **16–31.** David anointed; David and Goliath; David at court; the friendship with Jonathan; Saul's jealousy; David's years as a fugitive; Saul and the medium at Endor; the death of Saul and Jonathan on Mount Gilboa.

## Key passages and ideas

Hannah's song (chapter 2) already sounds the book's theme: the Lord raises the poor and brings down the mighty. Mary's song in [Luke](../../../new-testament/books/gospels/luke.md) will echo it. The debate over kingship in chapter 8 is indispensable first-principles political theology: a king will take sons, daughters, fields, and tithes. Then the Lord grants the request anyway.

David's anointing (chapter 16) and the Goliath story (chapter 17) establish a pattern of unexpected election. The covenant friendship of David and Jonathan, Saul's increasing isolation, and David's repeated refusal to kill "the Lord's anointed" when Saul is in his power, are character studies as much as political history. The Endor scene (chapter 28) is strange and bleak: Samuel, summoned from death, has only judgment left to speak.

The arc of Saul is tragic in a nearly classical sense. He is genuinely chosen, genuinely gifted, and then genuinely rejected, without the narrator making him a simple villain. His first victory at Jabesh-gilead is clean; his later fear of the people, his rash oath, and his jealousy of David are written as a slow unmaking. David's early career is equally careful: the text can praise him and still show him among the Philistines, collecting wives, and leaving Amalekite raids in his wake. Readers who want a clean moral diagram will be frustrated. The book is interested in the Lord's freedom to choose, and in the human wreckage that can accompany that choice, long before the explicit promise of 2 Samuel 7.

## Themes and reception

Themes include vocation, the ark and the presence of God, the ambiguity of monarchy, envy, loyalty, and the slow rise of the one who will receive the promise in [2 Samuel](2-samuel.md). The book is also a literary masterpiece of dialogue and irony.

Jewish and Christian tradition both read it as sacred history and as a moral mirror for rulers. Modern literary study (Alter and others) has made its narrative art newly visible. Historians debate the size and nature of an early united monarchy; that debate belongs beside the text, not in place of it.
