# 1 Kings

**Name and meaning.** 1 and 2 Kings (a single book in Hebrew, *Melakhim*) tell the story of the monarchy from David's last days to the exile of Judah. 1 Kings runs from Solomon's accession through the division of the kingdom to the reigns of Ahab in the north and Jehoshaphat in the south, and introduces the prophet Elijah.

**Prerequisites:** [2 Samuel](2-samuel.md) and [Saul, David, and Solomon](../../07-saul-david-solomon.md). The prophetic background is in [the prophets](../../08-prophets.md).

## Dating and authorship

Traditional ascription sometimes names Jeremiah. Scholarly study treats Kings as the climax of the Deuteronomistic History, with a major edition in the exile (explaining the fall of Jerusalem) and possible earlier editions in the times of Hezekiah or Josiah. Each king is evaluated by a standard: did he worship at the high places, or walk in the way of David, or (in the north) follow the sin of Jeroboam? Sources named in the text include the book of the acts of Solomon and the chronicles of the kings of Israel and Judah.

## Context

The setting is the tenth to ninth centuries BCE in the book's own chronology: a united kingdom that splits after Solomon into Israel (north, capital eventually Samaria) and Judah (south, capital Jerusalem). International players include Egypt, Tyre, Aram-Damascus, and, by the end of 1 Kings, the shadow of Assyria. The theology is Deuteronomic: exclusive worship of the Lord at the chosen place, and the land as a contingent gift.

## Outline

- **1–2.** The succession: Adonijah versus Solomon; David's last charge; the settling of old scores.
- **3–11.** Solomon: the request for wisdom; administration and wealth; the temple built and dedicated; the queen of Sheba; foreign wives, high places, and the announced split.
- **12–16.** The division under Rehoboam and Jeroboam; the golden calves at Bethel and Dan; a rapid series of northern and southern kings; the rise of Omri and Ahab.
- **17–22.** Elijah: the drought; the widow of Zarephath; Carmel; Horeb; Naboth's vineyard; Micaiah; the death of Ahab.

## Key passages and ideas

Solomon's temple dedication (chapters 8–9) is a high point of royal theology and also a warning: if Israel turns away, this house will become a heap. The prayer holds together the presence of God and the admission that heaven itself cannot contain God. The book's later chapters make good on the warning.

Jeroboam's calves (chapter 12) become "the sin of Jeroboam," the standard indictment of northern kings: alternative sanctuaries, alternative worship. Elijah's contest on Carmel (chapter 18) and his flight to Horeb (chapter 19) — wind, earthquake, fire, and a thin silence — are among the most influential prophetic scenes in the Bible. Naboth's vineyard (chapter 21) shows kingship preying on a smallholder, and a prophet standing in the way.

The book's architecture rewards slow reading. Solomon's wisdom is displayed in the midwife-and-child judgment, in administrative lists, and in international reputation, and then is undercut by the very internationalism that made him famous: alliances sealed by marriage, and high places for foreign gods. After the split, the narrator will often treat a northern reign and a southern reign in tandem, dating each by the other, so that the reader cannot forget they are two halves of one story. The Elijah cycle, which can feel like a new book beginning at chapter 17, is the narrator's way of saying that when kings fail the covenant, the word of the Lord does not. Prophecy is not a later biblical genre waiting in another section; it is already the engine of this history.

## Themes and reception

Themes include wisdom and its limits, the temple, the split of the kingdom, dynastic politics, and the prophet as the Lord's counterweight to the king. The Deuteronomistic evaluations can feel repetitive; they are the book's moral argument, not a failure of style.

Later Jewish and Christian readers took Solomon as the type of wisdom and of later failure, and Elijah as the typological forerunner (appearing in Malachi's hope and in the [Gospels](../../../new-testament/books/gospels/README.md) at the transfiguration and in debates about John the Baptist). Archaeology and ancient Near Eastern records (the Mesha Stele, Assyrian inscriptions) occasionally intersect Kings; they also complicate it. Read the theology and the history as the book interweaves them.
