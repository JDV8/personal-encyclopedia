# 2 Chronicles

**Name and meaning.** The second half of Chronicles, from Solomon's temple-building to the edict of Cyrus that permits a return and a rebuilt house. It is a history of Judah's kings, written for a post-exilic temple community.

**Prerequisites:** [1 Chronicles](1-chronicles.md) and [1–2 Kings](1-kings.md).

## Dating and authorship

See [1 Chronicles](1-chronicles.md). 2 Chronicles follows the southern monarchy almost exclusively, expanding some episodes (especially Hezekiah's and Josiah's reforms, and speeches by prophets and kings) and omitting most of the northern story except where it touches Judah.

## Context

Same Persian-period setting as 1 Chronicles. The question is: how did we lose the land and the first temple, and what does faithfulness look like now? The book answers with a pattern of seeking the Lord, humility, and proper worship, often more quickly rewarded or punished than in Kings.

## Outline

- **1–9.** Solomon: wisdom, the temple's construction and dedication, international fame; a smoother portrait than 1 Kings 11, with less emphasis on his fall.
- **10–12.** The split; Rehoboam; Shishak's invasion.
- **13–28.** A sequence of Judahite kings, with distinctive treatments of Abijah, Asa, Jehoshaphat, Uzziah, Ahaz, and others; prophetic speeches; the unique account of Manasseh's captivity and repentance (absent from Kings).
- **29–32.** Hezekiah's great Passover and reform; the Assyrian crisis.
- **33–35.** Manasseh, Amon, Josiah's Passover.
- **36.** The last kings; the exile; Cyrus' decree, which opens the door to [Ezra](ezra.md).

## Key passages and ideas

The temple dedication (chapters 5–7) reprises 1 Kings 8, with 7:14 — a call for God's people to humble themselves, pray, seek the Lord's face, and turn from wicked ways — becoming a much-quoted verse in later Christian piety, sometimes detached from its temple setting. In context it is about the land, the house, and covenant restoration.

Manasseh's repentance (chapter 33) is the clearest example of the Chronicler's theology of return: even the king whom Kings blames for the exile can be restored if he humbles himself. Whether one treats this as historical memory or theological revision, it is the book's distinctive claim.

The book ends not with Jehoiachin at table but with a Persian emperor commissioning the rebuilding of the temple. History, for the Chronicler, opens again at the sanctuary.

Several episodes appear here in much fuller form than in Kings, and they show the Chronicler's hand. Abijah's speech against Jeroboam (chapter 13) makes the Davidic and priestly claim explicit in battle. Jehoshaphat organizes teaching missions and a kind of court reform (chapters 17–20) and goes to war with a choir in front of the army. Uzziah's pride in the temple, Asa's reliance on physicians and on Aram rather than on the Lord, and Ahaz's disastrous alliances are all told as sermons in narrative form: seek the Lord and live; forget and fall. The expansions are not merely more facts. They are a post-exilic education in how to read royal history as a guide for a temple people.

The ending in Cyrus' decree is a literary and theological hinge. By closing with the same words that open Ezra, the Chronicler (or a final editor) binds the restored temple to the whole sweep from Adam's genealogy to the exile. The seventy years of rest for the land, named in 36:21 with a nod to Jeremiah, interpret the exile not as a gap in the story but as sabbath for a land that had been denied its rest. Hope, in this book, is not a new monarchy first of all. It is a reopened house and a people invited to "go up."

## Themes and reception

Themes include the temple, repentance, prophetic warning, and a Judah-centered memory of the past. The book has been used as a resource for reform movements (Hezekiah, Josiah) and for theologies of revival. Historical study compares it carefully with Kings and with what is known of Persian Yehud. It should not be harmonized silently with Kings; the differences are the point.
