# Moses, the Exodus, and Sinai

**Prerequisites:** [Joseph](04-joseph.md)
**Key ideas:** a people formed by deliverance and then by covenant; the name of God; plague, Passover, and sea; law given in the wilderness; traditional Mosaic authorship versus a long compositional history.

A new king arises in Egypt who does not know Joseph. The Israelites have become numerous; they are enslaved and their baby boys are ordered killed. One child is hidden, placed in a basket on the Nile, and taken up by Pharaoh's daughter. She names him Moses. He grows up, kills an Egyptian who was beating a Hebrew, and flees to Midian, where he becomes a shepherd and marries Zipporah, daughter of the priest Jethro.

## Call and Name

At a burning bush that is not consumed, the Lord calls Moses to bring the people out of Egypt. Moses resists: who am I, who are you, they will not believe, I cannot speak. God gives the name **I AM WHO I AM** (or "I will be who I will be"; the Hebrew is deliberately dense) and is identified with the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. The name YHWH, later treated as too holy to pronounce in Jewish practice, is tied to this moment. Aaron is given as a spokesman.

## Plagues, Passover, and the sea

Moses and Aaron confront Pharaoh: "Let my people go, that they may serve me." Pharaoh hardens his heart, or the Lord hardens it — the text says both, a tension readers have never fully tidied. A sequence of plagues strikes Egypt: water turned to blood, frogs, gnats, flies, livestock disease, boils, hail, locusts, darkness, and finally the death of the firstborn. Before the last plague the Israelites mark their doorposts with a lamb's blood, eat in haste with unleavened bread, and the Lord "passes over" the marked houses. **Passover** becomes an annual memory of this night.

They leave. Pharaoh pursues. At the sea (the "Red Sea" of many English Bibles; "Sea of Reeds" is a closer rendering of the Hebrew), the waters divide, Israel crosses on dry ground, and the pursuing army is drowned. The song that follows (Exodus 15) is among the oldest-feeling poems in the Bible: the Lord is a warrior who has thrown horse and rider into the sea.

Wilderness comes next: bitter water made sweet, manna and quail, water from a rock, a battle with Amalek. The people complain; Moses intercedes. Deliverance does not automatically produce trust.

## Sinai and the covenant

At Mount Sinai the people encamp. The mountain smokes and shakes. The **Ten Commandments** (or "ten words") are proclaimed: exclusive loyalty to this God, no images, the name not misused, sabbath, honor of parents, and prohibitions of murder, adultery, theft, false witness, and coveting. A longer covenant code follows. The people agree: "All that the Lord has spoken we will do." Blood is dashed, a meal is eaten, and Moses is on the mountain forty days.

Meanwhile the people, anxious, make a golden calf. Moses shatters the tablets, the camp is judged, and Moses pleads again. New tablets are cut. Instructions for the **tabernacle** — a portable sanctuary — occupy much of the rest of Exodus, and Leviticus develops a priestly world of sacrifice, purity, and holiness ("you shall be holy, for I am holy"). Numbers carries the people through a generation of wilderness failure; a census, spies who fear the land, and forty years of wandering mean that the generation of the Exodus, Moses included, will mostly not enter. Deuteronomy is Moses' long farewell speech on the plains of Moab, rehearsing the law and setting before them life and death, blessing and curse.

Traditional Jewish and Christian teaching holds **Moses as the author** of these five books (with a small allowance, often, for the account of his death). This is the bedrock of "the Law of Moses." Historical-critical study generally sees the Pentateuch as a work that reached its present form much later, combining older traditions, including a Deuteronomic voice associated by many scholars with a seventh-century reform, and priestly material often dated to the exile or after — though debates about dating remain active. On the Exodus as a historical event, archaeology and Egyptology have not produced a consensus picture that simply confirms the biblical scale and date; some scholars argue for a smaller historical core, others for a founding myth of liberation shaped in later Israel. The story's force in Jewish and Christian memory does not wait on that consensus: a people is a people because they were brought out, and because they stand under a covenant given in the wilderness, before they ever held a king.
