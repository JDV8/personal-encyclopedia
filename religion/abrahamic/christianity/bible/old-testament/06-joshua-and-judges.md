# Joshua and Judges

**Prerequisites:** [Moses, the Exodus, and Sinai](05-moses-exodus-and-sinai.md)
**Key ideas:** entry into the land as promise and as violence; Joshua's relatively unified conquest versus Judges' messy cycle; "there was no king in Israel"; the moral and historical problems of conquest.

Moses dies on the edge of the land, seeing it but not crossing. Joshua, his assistant, is commissioned: be strong, keep the law, the Lord will be with you.

## Joshua

The book of Joshua tells a relatively swift, coordinated conquest. Spies enter Jericho and are hidden by Rahab, a Canaanite woman who will be spared and who will enter Israel's memory (and, later, a Gospel genealogy). The people cross the Jordan in a scene that echoes the Sea: water piles up, the ark leads, memorial stones are set. At Jericho the walls fall after a ritual march and a shout. Ai is taken after an initial failure caused by Achan's seizure of devoted goods. At Shechem, covenant is renewed. The sun is said to stand still in a battle with a coalition of kings. The land is allotted among the tribes. The book ends with Joshua's charge: choose this day whom you will serve.

The military ban (*herem*) — the devotion of some cities to total destruction — is one of the hardest moral features of the Hebrew Bible. The text presents it as a command and as a safeguard against Israel adopting Canaanite worship. Later Jewish and Christian readers have wrestled with it, allegorized it, restricted it as unique to that command, or judged it as ancient war ideology that cannot be a model. An honest first pass should not smooth it away.

Historical-critical and archaeological discussion often notes a tension inside the Bible itself: Joshua describes a thorough conquest, while Judges (and even some passages in Joshua) describes incomplete occupation, with Canaanites remaining. Many scholars see a more gradual, mixed process of settlement and ethnogenesis, with the conquest stories doing theological and national work — perhaps written or edited much later, looking back. Traditional reading takes the book as historical campaign narrative. Both agree on what the book *claims*: the land is a gift tied to covenant loyalty, not a mere prize of arms.

## Judges

Judges has a bleaker rhythm. After Joshua's generation dies, the people worship the Baals. The Lord gives them into the hands of enemies. They cry out. The Lord raises a **judge** (*shoftet*): not mainly a courtroom figure, but a deliverer and sometimes a local leader. There is rest; then the cycle begins again. The refrain that grows toward the end is: "In those days there was no king in Israel; all the people did what was right in their own eyes."

The judges are an uneven gallery. **Deborah**, a prophet, with Barak, defeats Sisera; Jael kills Sisera in her tent; the Song of Deborah celebrates this. **Gideon** tears down an altar of Baal, defeats Midian with a reduced force so that the victory cannot be credited to numbers, then refuses a crown — but makes an ephod that becomes a snare. **Jephthah** wins a victory and keeps a rash vow that costs his daughter's life. **Samson**, set apart as a Nazirite, is a border raider of enormous strength and poor judgment; betrayed through Delilah, he dies pulling down a Philistine temple. The book ends with the grotesque civil violence of the Levite's concubine and the war against Benjamin — some of the darkest pages in the canon.

If Joshua is a picture (perhaps an idealized one) of a people united under faithful command, Judges is a picture of fragmentation. It also does political work: it can be read as making the case that kingship is needed, or as showing what happens when covenant is ignored, or both. The next lesson will not treat kings as a simple cure.
