# 8. Funding and conflicts of interest

**Prerequisites:** [Research integrity](../06-research-integrity/README.md) and [Journals and peer review](../07-publishing/README.md).

Who pays for research and how that matters: what a conflict of interest is, the evidence that funding shapes results, the limits of disclosure, how to weigh conflicts without ad hominem dismissal, non-financial conflicts, and research funded by activists and litigants. The section aims to be even-handed: industry funding has the best-documented record of distorting evidence, but no funder is interest-free.

## Contents

1. [Who pays for research](01-who-pays-for-research.md): Research is funded by governments, industry, charities, institutions, advocacy groups and law firms.
2. [What a conflict of interest is](02-what-a-conflict-of-interest-is.md): A conflict of interest is a situation where a secondary interest risks distorting judgement about a primary interest.
3. [How funding shapes results](03-how-funding-shapes-results.md): Systematic reviews consistently find that industry-sponsored studies have more favourable results and conclusions.
4. [Disclosure and its limits](04-disclosure-and-its-limits.md): Disclosure is the standard response to conflicts of interest and is necessary.
5. [Weighing conflicts without ad hominem](05-weighing-conflicts-without-ad-hominem.md): Dismissing research because of its funder is an ad hominem fallacy; ignoring conflicts is also an error.
6. [Non-financial conflicts](06-non-financial-conflicts.md): Non-financial conflicts include theory, career, reputation, relationships, ideology and advocacy.
7. [Activist and litigation-funded research](07-activist-and-litigation-funded-research.md): Litigants, advocacy groups, competitors and campaigns also fund research and have interests in results.

## Key studies & sources

- Lundh A, Lexchin J, Mintzes B, Schroll JB, Bero L. Industry sponsorship and research outcome. *Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews* 2017;MR000033. [doi](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.MR000033.pub3). 75 papers; industry-sponsored studies more often had favourable results (RR 1.27) and conclusions (RR 1.34), not explained by standard risk-of-bias measures.
- Bekelman JE, Li Y, Gross CP. Scope and impact of financial conflicts of interest in biomedical research: a systematic review. *JAMA* 2003;289:454–465. [doi](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.289.4.454). Pooled OR 3.60 for pro-industry conclusions.
- Barnes DE, Bero LA. Why review articles on the health effects of passive smoking reach different conclusions. *JAMA* 1998;279:1566–1570. [doi](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.279.19.1566). Tobacco affiliation was the only predictor of conclusions.
- Stelfox HT, Chua G, O'Rourke K, Detsky AS. Conflict of interest in the debate over calcium-channel antagonists. *NEJM* 1998;338:101–106. [doi](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM199801083380206).
- Bes-Rastrollo M, Schulze MB, Ruiz-Canela M, Martinez-Gonzalez MA. Financial conflicts of interest and reporting bias regarding the association between sugar-sweetened beverages and weight gain. *PLoS Medicine* 2013;10:e1001578. [doi](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1001578).
- Cain DM, Loewenstein G, Moore DA. The dirt on coming clean: perverse effects of disclosing conflicts of interest. *Journal of Legal Studies* 2005;34:1–25. [doi](https://doi.org/10.1086/426699). Laboratory evidence that disclosure can increase biased advice.
- Kesselheim AS, Robertson CT, Myers JA, et al. A randomized study of how physicians interpret research funding disclosures. *NEJM* 2012;367:1119–1127. [doi](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMsa1202397).
- Cope MB, Allison DB. White hat bias: examples of its presence in obesity research and a call for renewed commitment to faithfulness in research reporting. *International Journal of Obesity* 2010;34:84–88. [doi](https://doi.org/10.1038/ijo.2009.239). Note: Allison has received industry funding, as critics have pointed out.
