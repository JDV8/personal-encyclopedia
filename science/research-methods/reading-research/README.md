# Reading scientific research

**Prerequisites:** None. School arithmetic is enough; the statistics section builds everything else from scratch.

A course on how to read, question and weigh scientific research, especially in medicine and health, from first principles. It covers what science is, the statistics you need to read results, study designs and the evidence hierarchy, causation and bias, the anatomy of a paper, research integrity and the replication crisis, journals and peer review, funding and conflicts of interest, how research becomes news, ten case studies and a set of worked examples on real papers.

The course tries to be even-handed. It shows how industry funding has distorted evidence (tobacco, sugar, Vioxx, Study 329), and also how activist, litigation and non-financial interests can do the same (Wakefield, Séralini, white hat bias). The goal is not to distrust everything, or to trust authorities blindly, but to judge each claim by its methods, its corroboration and the interests that might have shaped it.

## Contents

1. [Foundations](01-foundations/README.md) (6 lessons): What science is, why it works, and why reading research is harder than it looks.
2. [Statistics for readers](02-statistics/README.md) (19 lessons): The statistical ideas you need to read results sections: distributions, averages and spread, sampling, confidence intervals, p-values and their misuse, power, effect sizes, relative and absolute risk, NNT, base rates and Bayes, diagnostic tests, multiple comparisons, regression to the mean, adjustment, and odds and hazard ratios.
3. [Study designs and the evidence hierarchy](03-study-designs/README.md) (16 lessons): The main kinds of study, from case reports to randomised trials and meta-analyses, what each can and cannot show, and how evidence is graded.
4. [Causation and bias](04-causation/README.md) (10 lessons): How to get from association to causation: confounding, reverse causation, selection and survivorship bias, healthy-user effects, measurement and recall bias, time-related biases, the Bradford Hill considerations and causal diagrams.
5. [Anatomy of a paper](05-anatomy-of-a-paper/README.md) (12 lessons): A guided tour of a research paper, section by section: title and abstract, introduction, methods, results and Table 1, graphs, forest plots, Kaplan-Meier curves, funnel plots, the discussion and spin, supplementary material, and reporting checklists such as CONSORT, STROBE and PRISMA.
6. [Research integrity and the replication crisis](06-research-integrity/README.md) (9 lessons): How the research record gets distorted, and what is being done about it: preregistration, trial registries, outcome switching, p-hacking, HARKing, publication bias, the replication crisis, fraud and misconduct, and open science.
7. [Journals, peer review and publishing](07-publishing/README.md) (6 lessons): How research gets published and how far that process can be trusted: how journals work, peer review, impact factors and other metrics, preprints, predatory journals, and retractions and corrections.
8. [Funding and conflicts of interest](08-funding-and-conflicts/README.md) (7 lessons): Who pays for research and how that matters: what a conflict of interest is, the evidence that funding shapes results, the limits of disclosure, how to weigh conflicts without ad hominem dismissal, non-financial conflicts, and research funded by activists and litigants.
9. [From lab to headline](09-from-lab-to-headline/README.md) (6 lessons): How findings change on the way from the laboratory to the news: press releases and science journalism, surrogate endpoints, animal and cell studies, subgroups and secondary analyses, consensus and dissent, and how much weight a single study deserves.
10. [Case studies](10-case-studies/README.md) (10 lessons): Ten real episodes that show the course's ideas in action: industry-manufactured doubt (tobacco, sugar), surrogate endpoints (CAST), observational studies overturned by trials (HRT), hidden harms and data (Vioxx, Study 329, Tamiflu), fraud with undisclosed litigation interests (Wakefield), the replication crisis (power posing) and unverifiable data (Surgisphere).
11. [Practice](11-practice/README.md) (7 lessons): Putting it all together: how to find and access papers, a step-by-step checklist, and five worked examples applying it to real papers, a randomised trial (PREDIMED), a cohort study (MMR and autism), a meta-analysis (aspirin for primary prevention), a news story (chocolate and Nobel prizes) and a case-control study (Doll and Hill).
- [Glossary](glossary/README.md): 193 terms, A–Z.

## Suggested reading order

1. Sections 1–4 in order: foundations, statistics, study designs and causation. These are the core.
2. Sections 5–7 (the paper, integrity, publishing) in order.
3. Section 8 (funding and conflicts) and section 9 (from lab to headline).
4. Section 10's case studies in any order, as illustrations.
5. Section 11: use the [checklist](11-practice/02-a-step-by-step-checklist.md) on the worked examples, then on papers you care about.

**Short path (about two hours):** [what science is](01-foundations/01-what-science-is.md) → [misreading p-values](02-statistics/09-misreading-p-values.md) → [relative and absolute risk](02-statistics/12-relative-and-absolute-risk.md) → [the evidence hierarchy](03-study-designs/01-the-evidence-hierarchy.md) → [confounding](04-causation/02-confounding.md) → [publication bias](06-research-integrity/06-publication-bias.md) → [weighing conflicts without ad hominem](08-funding-and-conflicts/05-weighing-conflicts-without-ad-hominem.md) → [the checklist](11-practice/02-a-step-by-step-checklist.md).
