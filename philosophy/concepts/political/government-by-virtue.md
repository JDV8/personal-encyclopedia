# Government by virtue

In the [*Analects*](../../history/ancient/chinese/texts/analects.md), government at its best works by *De*, moral power: lead with virtue and ritual, and the people will have a sense of shame and correct themselves. Punishments alone make people evasive. The ruler's cultivated person is itself a political cause. [Mencius](../../history/ancient/chinese/thinkers/mencius.md) extends this into a right to remove a ruler who destroys *ren*, and into the material conditions of a "constant heart."

This is the Confucian bet against [Legalism](fa-shi-shu.md): persons and examples first, not only standards and handles. It is also not a modern democracy, and it can be sentimentalized. The first-principles question is where you put your trust when order fails — in the formed person, or in the machine of law and position.

## Working the idea

The Confucian political hope is that a well-cultivated person in a well-ordered role radiates an effect: the virtue of the ruler rectifies the court, the household, and, at the limit, the realm, as the north star holds its place and the other stars turn around it. This is government by *de*, moral power or virtue, rather than by force and minute regulation. The *Analects*, the *Great Learning*, and the *Mencius* all, in different idioms, treat personal cultivation as a political act. Ritual, music, and the rectification of names are not ornaments of rule. They are how a shared public world is kept in tune.

The obvious objection is effectiveness. What happens when the man of virtue has no position, or when a well-mannered court faces cavalry? Legalists press this; history presses it too. Confucian replies vary. Sometimes the claim is long-run: a state that relies only on fear will eventually fail, because it trains no loyalty and no shame. Sometimes the claim is about legitimacy: a tyrant who has lost the people's hearts has already, in a sense, lost the mandate, even before the fall. Mencius is unusually blunt about the people's role in that judgment. None of this is a modern liberal theory of rights, and it should not be rewritten as one.

Work the idea by watching a concrete institution in the texts — mourning, remonstrance, the appointment of the worthy, the lightening of punishments — and asking how it is supposed to *work* as a mechanism, not only as a moral preference. Then put the Legalist objection beside it, and only then compare it with Plato's philosopher-rulers or with Aristotle's education of citizens. The family resemblance is "character first." The differences (household as the root of the state, ritual versus a Greek civic education, the role of law) are as important as the resemblance.

## Key ideas

- Confucian politics trusts moral power and ritual more than punishment.
- Mencius adds livelihood and the loss of the Mandate.
- The contrast with Legalism is the sharpest intra-Chinese political disagreement on this path.
