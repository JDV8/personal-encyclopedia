# Philosophy

**Prerequisites:** none. This is the entry point for the philosophy branch.
**Key ideas:** philosophy as reasoned inquiry into fundamental questions; five standing branches (metaphysics, epistemology, ethics, logic, political philosophy); history as a way in, not a substitute for the questions themselves.

Philosophy is the attempt to ask, and to answer carefully, questions that sit underneath more specialized work: what there is, what we can know, how we should live, what follows from what, and how we should live together. The word is Greek (*philosophia*, "love of wisdom"), but the activity is not a Greek monopoly. China, India, and other traditions developed rigorous ways of posing the same kinds of question, often in a different vocabulary and with different starting problems.

This folder does not begin with a biography of a famous thinker, and it does not begin with "Greek philosophy" as if that were the whole subject. It begins with the branches. Those branches are the first-principles map. Historical schools then become case studies in how people actually worked those questions. The [concepts](concepts/README.md) folder unpacks the branches at lesson length; the [glossary](glossary/README.md) is the lookup list; the [history](history/README.md) folder is the narrative path.

## Core branches

**Metaphysics** asks what reality is like at the most general level. Is there one kind of thing or many? Is change real? What is a cause? What is a person, a mind, or a god? Early Greek debates about the *arche* (originating principle) and the problem of change are metaphysics. So are Chinese arguments about the *Dao*, *qi*, and whether heaven (*Tian*) is a moral agent.

**Epistemology** asks what knowledge is, how it is possible, and how it differs from opinion or illusion. Plato's distinction between knowledge and belief, the later Mohist interest in standards of judgment, and the Greek Skeptics' suspension of judgment all belong here.

**Ethics** asks how one should live, what is good or right, and what sort of person is worth becoming. This includes virtue, duty, consequence, ritual, and cultivation. Aristotle's mean, the Stoic claim that virtue is the only good, Confucius's *ren* and *li*, and Mencius's "sprouts" of moral feeling are different ethical programs, not just different cultures.

**Logic** asks what makes an argument valid, what follows from what, and how language can clarify or mislead. Aristotle's syllogistic is one early systematic logic. The later Mohists developed their own tools for names, kinds, and inference. Logic is not a Greek invention in the sense that only Greeks reasoned carefully; it is a family of techniques for making reasoning inspectable.

**Political philosophy** asks what justifies authority, what a good political order is, and what we owe strangers and rulers. Plato's *Republic*, Aristotle's *Politics*, Confucian government by virtue, Mozi's impartial care, and Legalist statecraft are all answers to political questions, sometimes sharply opposed.

These branches overlap. A claim about human nature is metaphysical and ethical at once, and it usually has political consequences. The point of naming the branches is not to keep them in separate rooms. It is to notice *which question* a text is primarily answering.

## References & further reading

- Julia Annas, *Ancient Philosophy: A Very Short Introduction* (Beginner). A short, reliable map of the Greek questions these branches name.
- Bryan W. Van Norden, *Introduction to Classical Chinese Philosophy* (Beginner). The matching first map for the Chinese side.
- Pierre Hadot, *What Is Ancient Philosophy?* (Intermediate). Argues that ancient philosophy was a way of life, not only a set of doctrines.
- A. C. Graham, *Disputers of the Tao* (Advanced). The best single English history of early Chinese argument.
- Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* and *Metaphysics*, standard translations (Intermediate). Still the densest source for several of the branches as later Europe named them.
