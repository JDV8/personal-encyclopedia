# Ancient Greek philosophy

**Prerequisites:** [Philosophy](../../../README.md) and [Ancient philosophy](../README.md)
**Key ideas:** a shift toward giving natural and argumentative accounts of the world; the problem of change as a through-line; Socrates, Plato, and Aristotle as a hinge; Hellenistic schools as practical philosophies of life.

"Greek philosophy" here means philosophy written in Greek from the sixth century BCE through the Hellenistic period (and a little beyond, in the Roman-era Stoics and Skeptics). It begins on the Ionian coast of Asia Minor, not in Athens. Athens becomes central only with Socrates and his successors.

A useful first-principles question, before any biography, is this: what would it take to explain the world without stopping at "a god did it"? That is the impulse often attributed to the earliest Milesians. They did not stop being religious, and later Greek philosophy is full of gods. But they began looking for an *arche* — a principle or originating stuff — and for an account (*logos*) that could be examined.

The next question is the one that organizes so much of what follows: **how is change possible?** If everything is always in flux, how is knowledge possible? If being is one and unchanging, how can the world of ordinary experience be real? Heraclitus and Parmenides pose that dilemma with unusual sharpness. Plato and Aristotle are, in large part, two different attempts to resolve it.

Socrates turns the same energy on ethics and the care of the soul. Plato builds a metaphysics of Forms and a political vision around that turn. Aristotle builds a comprehensive science of substances, causes, living things, argument, virtue, and the polis. After Alexander, the Hellenistic schools — Stoics, Epicureans, Skeptics — treat philosophy as a way of life aimed at a settled mind in an uncertain world.
