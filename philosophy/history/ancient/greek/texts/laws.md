# *Laws*

**Prerequisites:** [*Republic*](republic.md)

## Dating and authorship

Plato's last and longest work, almost certainly left not fully polished. Socrates is absent. Three old men — an unnamed Athenian, a Spartan, and a Cretan — walk toward the cave of Zeus and design a "second-best" city, Magnesia, in conversation. The absence of Socrates and the greater role of written law, religion, and mixed institutions mark a late political turn, or at least a different project from the *Republic*.

## Outline and key ideas

The early books examine the educational systems of Sparta and Crete, the aims of law (virtue, not only victory), and the use of wine and music in a genuine paideia. Later books legislate in detail: marriage, property (a compromise short of the *Republic*'s guardian communism), magistracies, a "nocturnal council," religious cult, and a famous theological argument in Book X against atheism, including a cosmology of soul as first mover. The city is to have a preamble to its laws, persuading and not only threatening — law as a teacher, not only a whip.

Compared with the *Republic*, philosopher-kings recede; god, law, and habit do more of the work. Some readers see a disillusioned Plato; others see a complementary "second sailing" for a city that will not have sages on thrones. Both the religiosity and the punitive severity of Book X have troubled modern admirers of the earlier dialogues.

## Themes and reception

The *Laws* is the ancestor of much later thinking about mixed constitutions, legal preambles, and civic education. It is less read than the *Republic* because it is long, legislative, and less metaphysically dazzling. It is also the book in which Plato, more than anywhere else, treats law as the realistic form of reason in a city of ordinary people. [Aristotle](../thinkers/aristotle.md)'s *Politics* argues with both this work and the *Republic*.

## Key ideas

- A second-best city, designed in law, without Socrates and without philosopher-kings as in the *Republic*.
- Education, persuasion, and theology are part of legislation, not extras.
- Soul as a self-moving first cause is the theological climax of Book X.
