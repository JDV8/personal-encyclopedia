## Dating and authorship

A middle-period dialogue, still starring Socrates on the day of his death, but now clearly a vehicle for Platonic metaphysics: the Theory of [Forms](../../../../concepts/metaphysics/the-forms.md), the immortality of the soul, and [recollection](../../../../concepts/epistemology/anamnesis.md). How much of the arguments and the calm is historical Socrates is doubtful. The scene of the death itself has been enormously influential as literature.

## Outline

Friends, including the title character, gather in the prison (Plato is said, in the dialogue, to have been absent through illness). Socrates, unchained, discusses suicide, the philosopher's attitude to death, and a series of arguments that the soul survives: cyclical generation of opposites; recollection of Forms as proof that the soul existed before birth; the soul's kinship with the invisible and incomposite; and, after a serious objection by Simmias and Cebes (the soul as a harmony; the soul as a coat worn out after many bodies), a final argument tying the soul to the Form of Life. A myth of the afterlife and the earth follows. Socrates drinks the hemlock, asks that a cock be paid to Asclepius, and dies.

Along the way he recounts his disappointment with [Anaxagoras](../thinkers/anaxagoras.md): mind was promised as a cause and then unused, and Socrates now wants explanations that refer to the Good and to Forms, not only to bones and sinews.

## Themes and reception

The *Phaedo* is one of the founding texts of Western dualism and of the idea that philosophy is a "practice for death." Its arguments for immortality have been endlessly criticized (the harmony objection is especially sharp) and endlessly reused. The literary power — the jail, the cup, the friends — can make the arguments look more conclusive than they are. Read both: the myth and the drama, and the places where the proof is admitted to need another start. Together with the *Meno*, it is also a key text for recollection and for Forms as the objects that make knowledge possible.

## How to read it

The *Phaedo* is a death scene and a set of arguments for the soul's immortality, and it should be read as both. The arguments (opposites, recollection, affinity, the final "more subtle" argument about the soul and life) are not equally strong, and Simmias and Cebes are there to say so. Plato also gives you the form-theory in a living context, a critique of purely material explanation, and a myth of the afterlife. The last pages, the cup of hemlock, are part of the philosophical claim about how a life and a death can hang together.

A first reading can follow the drama. A second should outline each argument and the objections, and should notice what is myth, what is argument, and where Plato signals the difference. Read it after the *Apology* and *Crito*, and before or with the *Republic*'s psychology. Aristotle will not accept this as a finished science of the soul; later Platonism will live in this dialogue. You can learn from it without pretending that every argument succeeds.

## Key ideas

- Forms, recollection, and several arguments for the soul's immortality, of uneven force.
- A critique of merely material causes, aimed at Anaxagoras, in favor of explanation by the Good.
- A death scene that shaped the image of the philosopher more than any treatise could.
