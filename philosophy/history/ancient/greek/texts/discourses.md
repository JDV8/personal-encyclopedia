## Dating and authorship

Four surviving books (originally more) of informal lectures, written up by Arrian from Epictetus's school at Nicopolis. The voice is more expansive, funny, and severe than the *Handbook*, and it is the fuller document of the teaching.

## Outline and key ideas

Themes of the *Handbook* are here in their native length: *prohairesis* as the true self; the use of impressions; the roles of a son, a citizen, a guest; the kinship of humans under Zeus; the shame of fearing exile, office, or death if those things were never yours. Epictetus baits students, imagines excuses, and keeps returning to freedom as a state of the will, spoken by a man who had been enslaved. The *Discourses* also show more of the school's theology and of its running fight with Epicureans, Academics, and lazy would-be philosophers.

## Reception

Marcus Aurelius knew this work. It is the main long Stoic text in Greek that a modern reader can still inhabit at length, now that Chrysippus is lost.

## How to read it

Epictetus' *Discourses*, as written up by Arrian, are the living classroom of a former slave who became a Stoic teacher: informal, repetitive, sometimes harsh, aimed at changing the prohairesis (the moral purpose) of the hearer. Read a run of discourses, not only the most anthologized ones, and listen for the regular moves — distinguishing what is up to us, examining impressions, treating roles (citizen, brother, guest) as assigned duties. The tone is more combative than Marcus and less literary than Seneca.

The text is incomplete (four books survive of a larger set) and is our best extended access to Epictetus. Use a complete translation with book and chapter numbers. The *Enchiridion* can be a map; these talks are the terrain. A good second step is to take one practical topic (anger, or the fear of death, or social embarrassment) and follow it through a few discourses and then into the *Handbook*, to see how the same medicine is administered at two lengths.

## Key ideas

- The living lecture record behind the *Handbook*.
- Freedom, roles, and the use of impressions, at full length.
- A formerly enslaved teacher's theology of the inner *logos*.

Epictetus' classroom is also a window onto Roman-era social life: slavery and manumission, family duty, the lure of imperial office, the fear of being shamed at a banquet. The philosophy is meant to work *there*, not only in a quiet study. When a discourse seems merely to repeat a rule, look at the social scene it is disarming; the repetition is often a response to a new occasion of the same temptation.
