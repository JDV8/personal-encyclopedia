## Dating and authorship

Aristotle's main work of natural philosophy, a treatise (or course) in eight books on nature, change, place, time, the infinite, and the first unmoved mover. It is "physics" in the Greek sense: the study of *physis*, of things that have a principle of motion and rest in themselves — not a synonym for modern mathematical physics.

## Outline and key ideas

Nature is a principle of motion and rest in a thing *as such*. To understand natural things is to use the [four causes](../../../../concepts/metaphysics/the-four-causes.md), including ends. Change is the actualizing of a [potential](../../../../concepts/metaphysics/potentiality-and-actuality.md) as such — Aristotle's reply to [Parmenides](../thinkers/parmenides.md) and [Zeno](../thinkers/zeno-of-elea.md). The infinite exists potentially (you can always take another step) but not as a completed actual magnitude. Place is the inner boundary of the containing body; the void of the [atomists](../thinkers/democritus-and-leucippus.md) is rejected. Time is the number of motion with respect to before and after. The last book argues to a first unmoved mover, as the only way to stop a regress of moved movers — a conclusion taken up again in the [*Metaphysics*](metaphysics.md).

## Reception

This book, more than any other, was the framework of pre-modern natural science in the Islamic and Latin worlds, and the target of the early modern scientific revolt (on void, projectile motion, and teleology). Reading it now is not a substitute for physics, and it is not a museum piece. It is the most complete ancient attempt to make change intelligible without either denying it or reducing it to rearrangement in a void.

## How to read it

The *Physics* is Aristotle's fundamental inquiry into nature (*physis*) as an inner principle of motion and rest. Read it for the account of the four causes in a natural setting, for the analysis of motion, place, time, and the infinite, and for the argument that there is no beginning of motion without an unmoved mover (Book VIII). The work is not "physics" in a modern laboratory sense, and it should not be graded as a failed preview of Newton. It is a conceptual map of changeable being.

A first reading can take Books I–II carefully (principles and causes), sample the discussions of time and the continuum, and look at the close of Book VIII. The later *De Anima* and the biological works extend the same style of explanation into life. Use Bekker numbers. When early modern philosophers reject Aristotelian physics, they are often rejecting this book's authority over motion and final causes; knowing what they reject makes that history sharper.

## Key ideas

- Nature is an inner principle of motion; explanation is four-causal and often teleological.
- Potentiality and actuality, and a potential infinite, answer Eleatic and Zenoian puzzles.
- An unmoved mover closes the account of motion.
