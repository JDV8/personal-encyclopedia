## Dating and authorship

A substantial early-to-middle Platonic dialogue on rhetoric, power, justice, and the good life. It is among the most dramatic of the dialogues: three successive interlocutors, each harder than the last, and a Socrates who is more combative than in the *Apology*.

## Outline

Socrates questions the famous rhetorician **Gorgias** about what rhetoric is: a craft of persuasion about the just and unjust, able to produce conviction without knowledge. Gorgias's younger companion **Polus** is forced toward the claim that the orator is powerful because he can do what he wants, including injustice with impunity. Socrates inverts the values: it is worse to do injustice than to suffer it, and worse still to go unpunished. **Callicles** then drops the polite mask. He defends a *physis* ethic: conventional justice is a trick of the weak; the strong by nature should take more; philosophy is a charming pastime for youth and a disgrace in a grown man who can be slapped in the courts with no ability to defend himself. (The shadow of Socrates' future trial is obvious.)

Socrates argues that the soul has an order, that oratory as practiced is a knack for flattery rather than a true craft of the good, and that the tyrant's unrestrained desire is a leaking jar, not a happy life. A closing myth of post-mortem judgment puts the same claim in a different register.

## Themes and reception

The *Gorgias* is Plato's most sustained fight with rhetoric as a political technology, and one of his clearest statements that the good is not power or pleasure. Callicles is too strong a character to be a mere straw man; many readers have felt the pull of his speech. The dialogue is also a study in what argument can and cannot do with someone who will not admit a shared standard. It looks forward to the [*Republic*](republic.md) (justice, the soul's order, the myth) and sideways to the [*Protagoras*](protagoras.md) (can virtue be taught? what is the sophist's art?).

## How to read it

The *Gorgias* is a fight about rhetoric, power, and the good life, staged as three increasingly hard conversations: with Gorgias, with Polus, and with Callicles. Stay with the movement. What begins as a question about the nature of oratory becomes a question about whether it is better to suffer injustice than to do it, and about whether the tyrant's life is the happy one. Socrates' long closing myth should be read as part of the argument, not as an optional flourish.

The dialogue is among Plato's most uncompromising moral texts, and it is also a document of his quarrel with a certain picture of politics as the skill of dominating others. Read it before or after the *Republic*, and with the *Protagoras* if you want a different tone of engagement with sophists. A practical method: after each conversation, write down what the interlocutor wants from rhetoric and what Socrates has made expensive about that want. The dialogue is long enough that a single tired sitting will flatten Callicles; give it time.

## Key ideas

- Rhetoric, as flattery, is contrasted with a craft that knows the good of the soul.
- Doing injustice is worse than suffering it — a thesis tested against Polus and Callicles.
- Nature versus convention is argued in its most aggressive fifth-century form.
