## Dating and authorship

A middle-period dialogue, a series of after-dinner speeches in praise of [*eros*](../../../../glossary/greek/eros.md) at a party in Agathon's house, with a famous interruption by Alcibiades. It is as much literature as argument, and it should be read as a whole, not only for "Diotima's ladder."

## Outline

Several speakers offer competing portraits of Love: a god, a cosmic force, a spur to virtue in lovers of boys and men (Phaedrus, Pausanias), a physician's cosmology of harmony (Eryximachus), a comic myth of split humans seeking their other halves (Aristophanes — the most memorable speech, and not simply a joke), and Agathon's pretty praise of a beautiful young god. Socrates, claiming ignorance, reports the teaching of **Diotima** of Mantinea: *eros* is not a god but a daimonic intermediary, a lack that seeks the beautiful, climbing from beautiful bodies to beautiful practices, to laws, to knowledge, to the Beautiful itself, and to a birth in true virtue. Alcibiades then bursts in and praises Socrates as a Silenus figure: ugly, ironic, and, inside, full of divine images — a living image of the erotic philosopher, and also a political disaster waiting offstage.

## Themes and reception

The *Symposium* is Plato's great work on desire as something that can be educated, not merely suppressed or indulged. It is also a study in how speech, comedy, myth, and argument share a table. Later Christian and Islamic readers sometimes allegorized the ladder; modern readers sometimes isolate Aristophanes or Alcibiades. The first-principles yield is that ethics, for Plato, is a shaping of *eros* toward a stable object (the Beautiful, close to the Good of the *Republic*), and that the philosopher's life is itself an erotic one.

## How to read it

Read the *Symposium* in order, as a sequence of speeches at a party, and resist the urge to skip to Socrates (and to Diotima) on a first pass. Each speech — Phaedrus, Pausanias, Eryximachus, Aristophanes, Agathon — states a real view of *erōs* that the next speech, or Socrates, will recast. Aristophanes' myth of the split humans is unforgettable and is not the dialogue's last word. Diotima's ladder, from one beautiful body to the Beautiful itself, is the philosophical climax, and Alcibiades' drunken entrance is a test: what does the theory look like when a particular, disastrous love walks in?

The work is literature at a high level and should be read in a translation that keeps some of the humor. Pair it with the *Phaedrus*. Do not reduce it to a doctrine of "Platonic love" in the later, often desexualized, sense, and do not ignore the Athenian erotic conventions the speakers take for granted. A second reading can ask how the form of a symposium (praise speeches, drink, competition) both enables and threatens the philosophical claim.

## Key ideas

- *Eros* is interpreted many ways; Diotima's ascent is the philosophical climax, not the only voice.
- Love is a lack and a mediator, not a perfect god.
- Alcibiades' speech incarnates the theory and exposes its political fragility.
