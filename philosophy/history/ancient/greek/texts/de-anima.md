# *On the Soul* (*De Anima*)

**Prerequisites:** [Aristotle](../thinkers/aristotle.md); helpful: [*Physics*](physics.md)

## Dating and authorship

Aristotle's central treatise on *psyche*, the principle of life, in three books. The Latin title *De Anima* is conventional. It belongs with the biological works as well as with the metaphysics: a soul is not, first of all, an immortal passenger. It is the form of a living body.

## Outline and key ideas

Aristotle reviews predecessors (including [Democritean](../thinkers/democritus-and-leucippus.md) soul-atoms and [Plato](../thinkers/plato.md)'s accounts) and then defines the soul as the first actuality of a natural body that has life potentially — the [form](../../../../concepts/metaphysics/form-and-matter.md) of a living organized body. Capacities are layered: nutrition and reproduction (plants), perception and locomotion (animals), and, in humans, thought (*[nous](../../../../glossary/greek/nous.md)*). Perception is the reception of form without matter, a difficult and much discussed doctrine. Imagination, memory, and practical mind are treated as parts of a unified psychology, not as a ghost in a machine.

Book III's treatment of **active or productive intellect** — whether it is separate, immortal, and one for all humans — is among the most disputed pages in the corpus, and it drove a long medieval argument (Alexander, Themistius, Averroes, Aquinas). Aristotle is clearer that the human being is a hylomorphic composite than he is about the exact status of that intellect.

## Reception

This is the founding work of philosophical psychology in the Greek-to-medieval line. Descartes will start from a very different split of mind and body; much contemporary Aristotle scholarship is an effort to recover a non-dualist, biological soul without anachronism. For this path, *De Anima* shows what [hylomorphism](../../../../glossary/greek/hylomorphism.md) looks like when applied to life and thought.

## Key ideas

- Soul is the form and first actuality of a living body, with nested capacities.
- Perception is reception of form without matter.
- The status of intellect in Book III is genuinely difficult and historically explosive.
