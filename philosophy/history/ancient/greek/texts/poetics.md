## Dating and authorship

A short, incomplete treatise on poetic art, mainly on tragedy (a second book, on comedy, is lost — a fact that later became a literary joke of its own). It is a work of philosophy of art and of literary analysis, not a handbook of how to write verse in the style of a modern creative-writing guide.

## Outline and key ideas

Poetry is a form of **[mimesis](../../../../glossary/greek/mimesis.md)** (imitation or representation), more philosophical than history in one famous remark, because it deals with what might happen according to probability or necessity, not only with what has happened. Tragedy is the imitation of a serious, complete action, in language seasoned in various ways, in the form of action not narrative, effecting through pity and fear a **[catharsis](../../../../glossary/greek/catharsis.md)** of such emotions — a sentence whose exact meaning is still argued. The *mythos* (plot) is more important than character; a good plot has a beginning, middle, and end, and often a reversal (*peripeteia*) and recognition (*anagnorisis*). The tragic hero is better than average but not perfectly just, and falls through a [*hamartia*](../../../../glossary/greek/hamartia.md) (an error or missing of the mark, often not a Victorian "tragic flaw"). Homer and the tragic poets are the evidence.

## Reception

After a relatively quiet ancient life, the *Poetics* became, in the Renaissance and after, *the* theory of tragedy, sometimes turned into rigid "rules" Aristotle did not quite write (the three unities, for example, are largely a later construction). It remains the starting point for talking about plot, recognition, and what representation is for.

## How to read it

The *Poetics* as we have it is a compressed, partly incomplete set of notes on poetic art, above all tragedy (the section on comedy is lost). Read it for the account of mimesis, plot as the soul of tragedy, the reversal and recognition, and the difficult word *katharsis*. Aristotle is doing philosophy of art with one eye on the *Republic*'s suspicion of poetry: he wants to say what tragedy is and what it is for, in a more classificatory and less legislative key than Plato.

The work is short enough to read twice. The second time, take a single play you know (often *Oedipus Tyrannus*, which Aristotle himself favors) and test the vocabulary on it. Later European "Aristotelian" rules (unities, and so on) are a reception, often a narrowing. Use a translation that does not hide the gaps and technical terms. The *Rhetoric* is a companion inquiry into a different art of speech.

## Key ideas

- Tragedy as mimesis of a complete action, working pity and fear toward a catharsis.
- Plot over character; reversal, recognition, and *hamartia*.
- The book we have is incomplete; comedy is missing.
