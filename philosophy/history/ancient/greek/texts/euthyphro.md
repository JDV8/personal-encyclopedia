# *Euthyphro*

**Prerequisites:** [Socrates](../thinkers/socrates.md)

## Dating and authorship

A short early Platonic dialogue, generally treated as close to the historical [Socrates](../thinkers/socrates.md) in method, and as Plato's own writing. It is often grouped with the [*Apology*](apology.md) and [*Crito*](crito.md) as a "trial" sequence: Socrates is on his way to the porch where the indictment will be processed.

## Context and outline

Socrates meets Euthyphro, a religious specialist who is prosecuting his own father for impiety — a shocking use of piety, and a mirror of the charge Socrates himself faces. Socrates asks the question the dialogue never quite answers: **what is piety?** (*to hosion*, the pious or holy).

Euthyphro offers a series of definitions: piety is what he is doing now; it is what the gods love; it is what *all* the gods love. Socrates presses the famous fork: **is the pious loved by the gods because it is pious, or is it pious because it is loved by the gods?** If the gods love it because it is already pious, we still do not know what piety is. If piety is only "being loved," then piety is a passion in the gods, not a character of the act, and we have a tautology rather than a standard. Further attempts (piety as a part of justice, as care or service of the gods, as a commercial exchange of gifts and honors) also collapse. Euthyphro finds a reason to leave. The dialogue ends in [*aporia*](../../../../concepts/epistemology/elenchus-and-aporia.md).

## Themes and reception

The dialogue is a model of the [elenchus](../../../../glossary/greek/elenchus.md) and a first lesson in asking for a definition that can survive cases and consequences. The "Euthyphro dilemma" has a long afterlife in philosophy of religion, often restated as a question about God and the good. Plato does not, here, give a Theory of Forms as the answer; he shows why a certain kind of answer fails. Read it before the *Apology* to feel the irony: a man who cannot get a religious expert to define piety is about to be tried for impiety.

## Key ideas

- Piety cannot be reduced to "what the gods love" without a further standard, or without emptying the term.
- The dialogue is an *aporia*, not a treatise on religion.
- It frames Socrates' own trial as a problem about knowledge of the holy.
