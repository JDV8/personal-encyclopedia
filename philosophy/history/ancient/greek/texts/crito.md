# *Crito*

**Prerequisites:** [*Apology*](apology.md)

## Dating and authorship

A short early dialogue, a companion to the *Apology*. Socrates is in prison, days from execution. Crito, a loyal friend, has arranged an escape. The dramatic question is practical; the argument is about justice, gratitude, and whether a citizen may harm the city that has raised him.

## Outline and key ideas

Crito offers the ordinary reasons: money is ready; Socrates' death will look, to many, like a failure of his friends; his children will be abandoned; the official verdict was unjust. Socrates sets those considerations aside and asks only whether escape would be *just*. He personifies the **Laws of Athens**, who argue that he has been the beneficiary of the city's order, that he has had a lifetime to leave if he found the laws bad, and that to break a lawful (even mistaken) verdict now would be to destroy the laws as far as he is able, and to return injustice for injustice.

The argument is not "always obey the state." It is more nearly: this man, who has chosen to live here, who has just refused in the *Apology* to propose exile, may not now sneak away. The Laws appeal to an implicit agreement and to the parental metaphor of the city. Both moves have been admired and attacked — as a founding text of political obligation, and as too quick to bind a person to an unjust outcome.

## Themes and reception

Read next to the *Apology*, the *Crito* is startling. The same Socrates who defies the jury's wish that he be silent now submits to the legal process. The consistency he claims is that he has never agreed to do injustice, and escape would be injustice to the laws. Later contract theories, civil disobedience debates, and readings of "Socratic citizenship" all pass through this short text. It should not be flattened into either simple quietism or a complete theory of the state.

## Key ideas

- The only question is whether escape is just, not whether it is convenient or good for one's reputation.
- The speech of the Laws claims an agreement and a debt to the city's order.
- The dialogue is a study in obligation after an unjust verdict, not a full political philosophy.
