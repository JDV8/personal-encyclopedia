# Zeno of Citium

**Prerequisites:** [Diogenes and the Cynics](diogenes-and-the-cynics.md) and the [Hellenistic schools survey](../06-hellenistic-schools.md)

## Name and dates

Zeno (c. 334–262 BCE) came from Citium in Cyprus, arrived in Athens, studied with the Cynic Crates and with Megarian and Academic teachers, and began to teach at the Painted Stoa (*Stoa Poikile*), from which the school takes its name. He is the founder; the system's classic shape is often credited more to later "old Stoics," especially Chrysippus (c. 279–206 BCE), who wrote at enormous length and almost none of whose work survives intact. To study "Zeno" is, in practice, to study early Stoicism, with Chrysippus as the great systematizer in the same folder of ideas.

## Sources

Zeno's own books are lost. We reconstruct early Stoicism from later reports (Diogenes Laertius, Cicero, Plutarch, Stobaeus), from fragments, and from the Roman Stoics — [Seneca](seneca.md), [Epictetus](epictetus.md), [Marcus Aurelius](marcus-aurelius.md) — who are our readable voices and who already stand downstream. Distinguishing Zeno from Chrysippus from later popular Stoicism is a scholarly task, not something a first overview can finish.

## Thought

Stoicism is a single system in three parts: **logic**, **physics**, and **ethics**. Logic (including a serious propositional logic and a theory of impressions) trains the mind to give or withhold assent. Physics describes a cosmos that is a living, rational whole: a divine [*logos*](../../../../glossary/greek/logos.md), often identified with a designing fire or with Zeus, pervades nature. Everything that happens is in some sense fated as part of that order. Human beings have a share of that reason. There is no Epicurean void and no swerve; the world is a continuum of [*pneuma*](../../../../glossary/greek/pneuma.md) in tension.

Ethics follows. The only genuine good is **virtue**, a well-ordered rational soul. Health, wealth, and even the lives of those we love are "preferred [indifferents](../../../../glossary/greek/adiaphora.md)": they may be reasonably selected, but they do not make a life good. Vice is the only genuine evil. The passions, on the Stoic analysis, are faulty judgments — treating an indifferent as if it were truly good or evil. The aim is [*apatheia*](../../../../glossary/greek/apatheia.md): not numbness, but not being enslaved by those judgments.

[Oikeiosis](../../../../glossary/greek/oikeiosis.md), "appropriation" or familiarization, explains how concern extends from one's own constitution to family, city, and, in principle, the whole of humanity. **Cosmopolitanism** is not a slogan added at the end; it follows from a physics in which we are parts of one rational world. Zeno is even credited with a *Republic* of his own, more radical and Cynic-flavored than Plato's, of which only reports remain.

## Reception

The Stoa became the most influential philosophy of the Hellenistic and Roman elite, then a resource for Christian and modern writers, then a contemporary popular ethics often detached from the physics. Reading Zeno's school well means putting the ethics back next to the *logos*-cosmos that was supposed to support it, and remembering how much of the early writing is gone.

## Key ideas

- Logic, physics, and ethics are one system, not three hobbies.
- Virtue is the only good; externals are preferred or dispreferred indifferents.
- A rational, providential cosmos grounds both *apatheia* and cosmopolitan concern.
