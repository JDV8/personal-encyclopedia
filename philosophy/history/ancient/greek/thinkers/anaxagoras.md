# Anaxagoras

**Prerequisites:** [Parmenides](parmenides.md) and [Empedocles](empedocles.md)

## Name and dates

Anaxagoras of Clazomenae (c. 500–428 BCE) brought Ionian natural philosophy to Athens. He was associated with Pericles, and he was eventually tried, or at least threatened, on charges that included impiety — in particular, the claim that the sun is a fiery stone rather than a god. He left Athens for Lampsacus. The episode is an early sign that cosmology in the city could become a political and religious problem, a generation before [Socrates](socrates.md)' trial.

## Sources

He wrote a book in prose, of which fragments survive, especially through Simplicius. The fragments are denser and more abstract than textbook summaries suggest. Plato's [*Phaedo*](../texts/phaedo.md) and Aristotle's reports are crucial for his reception, and also slightly distorting, because both wanted something from "mind" that Anaxagoras may not have given.

## Thought

Like Empedocles, Anaxagoras refuses generation from nothing. His solution is more radical than four roots. **In everything there is a portion of everything**, except [*nous*](../../../../glossary/greek/nous.md) (mind). Apparent stuffs — gold, flesh, bone — are names for mixtures in which one kind predominates. There is no smallest bit; matter is infinitely divisible, and even a tiny seed contains all the qualities that can later be extracted. Coming-to-be is the separating-out of what was already there.

What starts the separating is **Mind**: unlimited, unmixed, self-ruling, the finest of things, which knows and orders. In the beginning, all things were together; *nous* set the mixture rotating, and the cosmos began to differentiate. This is the first full appearance, in our path, of mind as a cosmological principle — not a god in a story, but a reason-like cause of order.

Socrates, in the *Phaedo*, says he was excited by this and then disappointed: he hoped Anaxagoras would explain why things are arranged *for the best*, and found instead a story of vortices and mixtures, with mind mentioned and then left unemployed. Whether that is fair is a standing debate. Anaxagoras does give mind a beginning role; he may not give it a thoroughgoing teleology. The disappointment itself became historically productive: it sharpened [Plato](plato.md)'s demand for a full [teleological](../../../../glossary/general/teleology.md) explanation, and [Aristotle](aristotle.md)'s account of [final cause](../../../../concepts/metaphysics/the-four-causes.md).

## Reception

He is a hinge between Ionian physics and the Athenian argument about reason and the good. Later ancient writers remember the impiety charge and the "everything in everything" slogan. Modern readers often emphasize the infinitely divisible mixture and the unusual status of *nous* as the one unmixed thing. Read him as a serious reply to Parmenides and as the thinker who put mind on the cosmological table without quite doing what Plato wanted with it.

## Key ideas

- Everything contains a portion of everything; apparent kinds are predominances.
- *Nous* is unmixed and initiates the ordering of the cosmos.
- Socrates' disappointment in the *Phaedo* shapes how later teleology is motivated.
