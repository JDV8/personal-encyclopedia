# Marcus Aurelius

**Prerequisites:** [Epictetus](epictetus.md)

## Name and dates

Marcus Aurelius (121–180 CE) was Roman emperor from 161 until his death, last of the "five good emperors" in a later shorthand, and a Stoic who wrote notes to himself in Greek while on campaign and at court. The reign included plague, wars on the northern and eastern frontiers, and the difficult elevation of his son Commodus. The book we call the [*Meditations*](../texts/meditations.md) was not composed as a treatise for the public.

## Sources

The *Meditations* (the Greek title is closer to "To himself") survive in a manuscript tradition that surfaces late; the text is divided into twelve books, the first of which thanks teachers and family, including the influence of Stoic teachers and a mention of [Epictetus](epictetus.md)'s *Discourses* via a friend. There is no reason to treat the work as a complete Stoic system. It is a notebook of exercises, reminders, and sometimes raw fatigue. That is its value.

## Thought

The recurring moves are Stoic and personal. See things as they are, briefly and without the added story: a robe is sheep's wool dyed with shellfish blood; a roast is a dead animal. Remember that you are a part of a rational whole, a citizen of the city of Zeus as well as of Rome, and that what happens is either providential or, at least, bearable as nature's process. Do the work in front of you — justice, cooperation, the "present task" — without a hunger for fame, which is a noise that dies. Do not be angry at the people you govern; they err from ignorance of the good, a Socratic note. Death is a dissolution or a change, and in either case not an evil for a mind that has understood what it is.

The book's tension is obvious and should not be tidied away. Its author is the most powerful man in the Roman world, writing about insignificance and the emptiness of imperial purple. Sometimes this reads as hard-won discipline; sometimes as a man talking himself back to his school in the middle of a grim job. The physics of a permeating *logos* is assumed rather than proved. The ethics is a practice of attention, closer to [Epictetus](epictetus.md) than to Chrysippus's lost logic.

## Reception

The *Meditations* became, much later, one of the most widely read ancient philosophical books in modern languages, often by people who do not read any other Stoic. That popularity has a cost (quotes detached from the school) and a benefit (a real ancient voice of self-examination is still in the room). For this path, Marcus is not the founder or the systematizer. He is evidence of what Stoicism looked like when it was used, daily, by someone who could not leave the world he was supposed to be indifferent to.

## Key ideas

- A private notebook of Stoic exercises, not a published system.
- Impartial description, cosmic citizenship, present work, and the stripping of fame and anger are the repeated drills.
- The emperor's situation is part of the meaning of the book, not an irony to ignore.
