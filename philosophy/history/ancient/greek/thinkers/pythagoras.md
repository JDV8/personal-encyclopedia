# Pythagoras

**Prerequisites:** [Foundational concepts](../01-foundational-concepts.md) and the [Presocratics survey](../02-presocratics.md)

## Name and dates

Pythagoras of Samos (c. 570–490 BCE) left Samos, in the stories, for a life in Magna Graecia, especially Croton in southern Italy. There he founded a community that was at once a philosophical school, a religious society, and, for a time, a political force. He became a legend almost immediately: a man with a golden thigh, a soul that had lived before, a teacher whose students were forbidden to speak of him by name. The legend is part of the historical problem.

## Sources and the "Pythagorean question"

Pythagoras wrote nothing that survives, and probably nothing at all. Early evidence is sparse (Xenophanes, Heraclitus, and Herodotus mention him). The rich accounts come centuries later, from the Hellenistic and Roman periods, when Pythagoras had become a sage-hero and a convenient ancestor for later Platonism. Scholars therefore distinguish, as carefully as they can, between the historical Pythagoras, the early Pythagorean community, and late "Pythagoreanism." Claims about number, harmony, the transmigration of souls, dietary taboos, and a divided school of "acousmatics" (hearers of maxims) and "mathematicians" cannot all be pinned on one man with equal confidence.

## Thought

Two clusters of ideas, at least, belong to this tradition early enough to matter for the rest of the path.

**Number and harmony.** The discovery that musical intervals correspond to simple numerical ratios (octave 2:1, fifth 3:2, fourth 4:3) suggested that audible beauty has a mathematical structure. From there it is a short, daring step to the claim that the world's order (*[cosmos](../../../../glossary/greek/cosmos.md)* — a word later tradition associates with this circle) is at bottom numerical, or that things "are" numbers. Even if that last slogan is later, the shift of attention from a material *arche* (water, air) to **structure and ratio** is the first-principles point. It is an ancestor of the mathematical side of [Plato](plato.md)'s Academy, and, much later, of the idea that nature is written in mathematics.

**Soul and purification.** Pythagoreans are associated with [*metempsychosis*](../../../../glossary/greek/metempsychosis.md), the passing of the soul through different bodies, human and animal, and with practices meant to purify it: dietary rules, silence, remembrance of former lives, and a way of life (*bios*) as much as a set of doctrines. The soul becomes something that can be stained or freed, something whose care outruns a single lifetime. [Socrates](socrates.md) and Plato will take up that care in a different key, and Plato's *Phaedo* and *Meno* will give it a theory of [recollection](../../../../concepts/epistemology/anamnesis.md).

A note of caution: later writers also attached to Pythagoras a long list of taboos and a cosmology of the "harmony of the spheres." Some of this may be early; some is almost certainly retrojection. The safe core is a community that treated mathematics, music, and the fate of the soul as one project.

## Reception

Plato is the great heir (the *Timaeus* is unthinkable without this atmosphere). Aristotle discusses Pythagorean number-metaphysics with mixed respect and criticism. In late antiquity, Neopythagorean and [Neoplatonic](plotinus.md) authors remade Pythagoras as a nearly divine founder. For this encyclopedia, he is the hinge between Milesian questions about stuff and a philosophy in which form, ratio, and the soul's education become first-class topics.

## Key ideas

- Structure (number, ratio, harmony) can be more fundamental than a material element.
- The soul is a subject of a way of life, possibly across lives.
- The sources are late and layered; distinguish man, early community, and legend.
