# Anaximenes

**Prerequisites:** [Anaximander](anaximander.md)

## Name and dates

Anaximenes of Miletus (active in the mid-sixth century BCE, dying perhaps around 525) is the third of the Milesian trio. Later writers make him Anaximander's pupil. We know even less of his life than of the other two. His importance is not biographical. It is that he tries to keep a single, nameable stuff and still explain how the many different things arise from it.

## Sources

No complete work survives. The later tradition, again through Theophrastus and the doxographers, attributes to him the claim that the *arche* is **air** (*aer*), and that other stuffs are air at different densities. As always, we are hearing a position through later categories.

## Thought

Why air? It is less "already a thing among things" than water in one sense — invisible when even, evident when it becomes wind or breath — and it is closely associated with life. Several reports connect cosmic air with breath and soul: as our soul, being air, holds us together, so breath and air enclose the world. The microcosm–macrocosm analogy will have a long afterlife, including in [Stoic](zeno-of-citium.md) physics.

The mechanism is what matters most for this path. **Rarefaction and condensation** (or a closely related pair) turn air into fire when it is thinned, and into wind, cloud, water, earth, and stone as it is packed more tightly. This is a single-stuff theory with a *how*. [Thales](thales.md) names water; [Anaximander](anaximander.md) names a source beyond the elements; Anaximenes names air *and* a process that generates qualitative difference from quantitative change — thinner and thicker.

That is a decisive conceptual step. If you can explain apparent kinds as the same thing in different states, you have a model that later atomists, and much later science, will radicalize. Anaximenes does not have a quantitative physics. He has the idea that difference of degree can do the work that a list of separate elements would otherwise have to do.

## Reception

He can look like a step back from Anaximander's *apeiron* toward a more ordinary element. Read alongside the mechanism, he is a step sideways and forward: a definite *arche* plus a theory of transformation. Later writers sometimes ran the three Milesians together as "material monists." That label is partly Aristotle's. It is still a useful way to see what they share, and what Anaximenes adds.

## Going further

Anaximenes is easy to reduce to a single word, "air," and then to skip. The more useful question is what the move from Anaximander's indefinite to a *determinate* principle was supposed to gain: a stuff that can rarefy and condense, and so explain change without leaving the world of ordinary materials. Whether that is a philosophical improvement or a failure of nerve is already an ancient kind of debate. Read him as a third Milesian step, not as a lesser repeat of Thales, and then compare the later atomist and Aristotelian criticisms of purely material principles.

## Key ideas

- The *arche* is air, associated with breath and life.
- Rarefaction and condensation are the mechanism that produces the many from the one.
- Difference of degree is used to explain difference of kind.
