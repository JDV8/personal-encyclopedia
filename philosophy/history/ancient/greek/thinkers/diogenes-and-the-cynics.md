# Diogenes and the Cynics

**Prerequisites:** [Socrates](socrates.md)

## Names and dates

Cynicism (*kynikos*, "dog-like") is a way of life more than a school with a curriculum. Its traditional founder is Antisthenes, a companion of [Socrates](socrates.md). Its emblem is Diogenes of Sinope (c. 404–323 BCE), who lived in Athens and Corinth with a deliberate poverty that later stories turned into theater: a tub for a house, a lamp in daylight looking for a human being, a request to Alexander the Great to stand out of his sun. Many anecdotes are later and should be read as moral emblems, not as reportage. Other names in the line include Crates of Thebes (teacher of [Zeno of Citium](zeno-of-citium.md)) and, later, popular preachers such as Dio Chrysostom in a related vein.

## Sources

No complete early Cynic treatise survives. We have anecdotes (especially in Diogenes Laertius), some letters later attributed to Cynics, and the imprint of the movement on Stoicism, comedy, and Roman satire. The sources are hostile, amused, or admiring by turns. The historical core is a public, abrasive practice of self-sufficiency, not a lost metaphysics.

## Thought

The Cynics take a Socratic idea — that virtue is the only thing worth serious care, and that convention can be a prison — and strip it of almost all institutional clothing. **Live according to nature**, they say, where nature means the needs of a hardy animal, not a theory of *logos* in the cosmos. Wealth, reputation, refined food, civic honors, and shame about the body are treated as [*nomos*](../../../../glossary/greek/nomos.md), convention, to be laughed at. [*Autarkeia*](../../../../glossary/greek/autarkeia.md) (self-sufficiency) and *askesis* (training) are the methods: reduce wants until fortune has little to take.

"Defacing the currency" (*paracharaxis*) is a slogan associated with Diogenes: take the city's stamped values and strike them through. The shamelessness (eating, sleeping, even sexual gestures in public, in the stories) is meant as a demonstration, not as a private vice. The "dog" is loyal to nature and indifferent to etiquette. Cosmopolitanism appears here in a rough form: asked where he was from, Diogenes is said to have called himself a citizen of the world.

There is little patience, in this stream, for logic as a formal science or for physics as a system. Argument exists, but the argument is often a life performed in the marketplace. That is why later philosophers found the Cynics both indispensable (as a test of seriousness) and insufficient (as a theory).

## Reception

[Zeno of Citium](zeno-of-citium.md) studied with Crates; Stoicism can be read as Cynic ethics given a physics, a logic, and a less theatrical public style. Roman writers used Diogenes as a stock figure of frank speech (*parrhesia*). Modern readers sometimes see a proto-anarchism, sometimes a performance art of poverty. For this path, the Cynics keep the Socratic "care of the soul" from becoming merely academic: if virtue is the only good, what would it look like to live as if that were true?

## Key ideas

- A way of life: train wants down, strike through conventional values, live in public.
- Self-sufficiency and "nature" matter more than a formal system.
- Anecdotes are often later emblems; the practice, not the punchline, is the doctrine.
