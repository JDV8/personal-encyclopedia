## Name and dates

Empedocles of Akragas (Agrigento, Sicily; c. 495–435 BCE) was a poet, a political figure, and, in later stories, a wonder-worker who leapt into Etna. The legend is late and should be held lightly. The poetry is the thing we can still read in fragments: two poems, or one poem later divided by scholars, often titled *On Nature* and *Purifications*.

## Sources

Hundreds of lines survive, enough to hear a voice. He writes in hexameter, like [Parmenides](parmenides.md), and he answers Parmenides without pretending the senses are worthless. Later reports (Aristotle, the doxographers, and the luck of papyrus finds, including the Strasbourg papyrus) help fill gaps, but the fragments themselves are the primary source.

## Thought

Empedocles accepts that what-is cannot come from nothing or perish into nothing. Change, therefore, is not genuine generation or destruction. It is the mixing and separating of four root things (*rhizomata*): **earth, air, fire, and water**. These roots are eternal. What we call a plant, a bone, or a person is a temporary recipe.

Two forces drive the cycle: **Love** (*Philia*), which draws unlike things together into a unified sphere, and **Strife** (*Neikos*), which pulls them apart. Cosmic history is a rhythm between a world of perfect mixture and a world of complete separation, with our own age somewhere on the path. Living forms, in some fragments, arise through stages and failed combinations — a striking early picture of order emerging from trial, not a modern theory of natural selection, but not a simple craftsman-myth either.

The religious side is not an afterthought. *Purifications* speaks of a fallen *daimon* that has stained itself with blood and must wander through bodies — plant, animal, human — before it can be restored. Meat-eating and blood sacrifice are treated as crimes against kin, because souls (or *daimones*) migrate. This is close to the [Pythagorean](pythagoras.md) atmosphere, joined to a physics of roots and forces.

## Reception

The four roots become, in later popular science, the "four elements," a scheme that lasts through Aristotle, Galen, and medieval medicine. Aristotle himself takes Empedocles seriously as a predecessor in physics and biology and criticizes him on teleology and on the inadequacy of chance mixtures. For this path, Empedocles is a model of how to honor Parmenides' ban on coming-to-be while still explaining the colorful, changing world: keep the ingredients eternal, and let change be arrangement.

## Going further

Empedocles is a poet-philosopher, and the religious and purificatory fragments should not be torn away from the physical ones as if only the four roots "count." Love and Strife, the cycle of the cosmos, and the fate of the daimon-soul are parts of one imagination, even if modern readers would rather have a physics chapter and a religion chapter. The surviving lines are incomplete and often difficult to order. Use a good fragment collection with context, and let the strangeness stand: this is not yet a school treatise, and it is already a serious rival to monism and to later atomism.

## Key ideas

- Four eternal roots; change is mixture and separation, not generation from nothing.
- Love and Strife are the two cosmic forces, in a cycle.
- A doctrine of wandering *daimones* and purification accompanies the physics.
