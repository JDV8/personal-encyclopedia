# The Presocratics

**Prerequisites:** [Foundational concepts](01-foundational-concepts.md)
**Key ideas:** natural principles instead of stopping at myth; Milesian *archai*; Pythagorean number and soul; Heraclitean flux and *logos*; Parmenides' challenge to change; atomism as one reply.

"Presocratic" is a later label. It groups Greek thinkers who were not primarily responding to Socrates, from the sixth century BCE into the fifth. We have them mostly in fragments and in reports by later authors (especially Aristotle), so humility is required. What follows is the standard teaching picture, with that caution attached.

## The Milesians

Thales of Miletus (early sixth century BCE) is traditionally "first." He is credited with predicting an eclipse, with using geometry practically, and with the claim that the *arche* is water. Whether or not the details are right, the pattern is: look for a natural principle that can explain many things (moisture, life, the apparent floating of the earth).

Anaximander, often presented as Thales' successor, pushed further. His *apeiron* — the indefinite or unbounded — is not a familiar element. Things arise from it and return to it according to a kind of cosmic justice. Anaximenes then offered air, with rarefaction and condensation as the mechanism that produces different stuffs. Mechanism matters: it is not enough to name a source, you need a way it can become the many.

## Pythagoras and the Pythagoreans

Pythagoras of Samos (late sixth century) is half philosopher, half founder of a way of life. The early community on Magna Graecia is hard to separate from later legend. Two ideas, at least, had a long afterlife.

First, **number and harmony**. The discovery that musical intervals correspond to simple ratios suggested that the world's order might be mathematical. That intuition runs all the way to Plato's Academy and, much later, to mathematical physics. It is not yet modern science. It is the claim that structure, not just stuff, is fundamental.

Second, **the soul**. Pythagoreans are associated with the transmigration of souls and with practices of purification. The soul becomes something that can be trained, stained, or freed — a thought Socrates and Plato will radicalize.

## Heraclitus

Heraclitus of Ephesus (around 500 BCE) writes in riddles, and later readers made him the philosopher of pure flux. The fragments are more interesting than the slogan. He does say that you cannot step into the same river twice, and that war or strife is a kind of father of things: opposition is not a defect in the cosmos, it is how the cosmos works. He also insists on a common *logos* that most people miss.

So Heraclitus is not simply saying that nothing is stable. He is saying that stability, if it exists, is the stability of a pattern in motion — like a river that remains a river *by* flowing. That is one way to take change seriously without giving up on order.

## Parmenides

Parmenides of Elea (early fifth century) wrote a poem in which a goddess instructs the speaker in the "way of truth" and warns him off the "way of opinion." On the way of truth: what-is is ungenerated, imperishable, whole, unchanging, and complete. You cannot think or speak what-is-not. Coming-to-be would require that something come from what is not, which is disallowed.

This is a shock to the Milesian project. If Parmenides is right, the changing world of the senses cannot be the real object of knowledge. Later Greek philosophy has to answer him, ignore him, or split reality into levels (as Plato does). His student Zeno designed paradoxes of motion and plurality (Achilles and the tortoise among them) to show how quickly ordinary assumptions break.

## The atomists

Leucippus and Democritus offered a daring reply. What-is (atoms) is eternal and unchanging in its basic units; what-is-not (the void) is real enough to allow motion. Macroscopic change is rearrangement, not genuine coming-to-be from nothing. Qualities like color and taste are effects on us; atoms themselves have shape, size, and motion.

This is a metaphysical package with ethical consequences later exploited by Epicurus: if the soul is a configuration of atoms, death is dispersal, not a journey. You do not need to accept that ethics to see the move. Atomism is one of the cleanest early attempts to honor Parmenides' demand for stable being *and* save the appearances of a changing world.

Other fifth-century figures belong in a fuller account — Anaxagoras (mind as orderer), Empedocles (four roots and the forces of love and strife), and the Sophists, who shifted attention to custom, rhetoric, and whether justice is natural or conventional. They set the stage for Socrates.
