# Hellenistic schools: Stoics, Epicureans, Skeptics

**Prerequisites:** [Aristotle](05-aristotle.md)
**Key ideas:** philosophy as a way of life after the classical *polis*; Stoic virtue and living according to nature; Epicurean atomism and freedom from disturbance; Skeptical suspension of judgment.

"Hellenistic" names the period after Alexander the Great's death in 323 BCE, when Greek culture spread across a world of kingdoms and, later, Roman power. The independent *polis* that shaped Plato and Aristotle was no longer the obvious container of a life. The schools that dominate this period treat philosophy less as a system first and more as a **therapy of the soul**: a trained way to live well when fortune is unstable.

Three schools matter most for a first pass. They disagree sharply, but they share a target: a settled condition of mind (*ataraxia*, freedom from disturbance; or, for the Stoics, a related ideal of not being ruled by the passions).

## Stoics

The Stoa begins with Zeno of Citium in Athens around 300 BCE, and is developed by Cleanthes and especially Chrysippus. Later Roman writers — Seneca, Epictetus, Marcus Aurelius — are the ones most people read now, but they stand on a full system: logic, physics, and ethics.

**Physics:** the cosmos is a living, rational whole. A divine *logos* (often identified with a designing fire, or Zeus) pervades nature. Everything that happens is in some sense fated, as part of that order. Human beings have a share of that reason.

**Ethics:** the only genuine good is **virtue** — a well-ordered rational soul. Health, wealth, and even the lives of those we love are "preferred indifferents": they may be reasonably selected, but they are not what makes a life good. Vice is the only genuine evil. The passions, on the Stoic analysis, are faulty judgments (treating an indifferent as if it were truly good or evil). The aim is not numbness, but *apatheia*: not being enslaved by those judgments.

The practical upshot, especially in Epictetus, is a discipline of what is "up to us" (our judgments and choices) versus what is not (externals). Cosmopolitanism follows from the physics: we are citizens of the universe, not only of a city. Stoicism is easy to quote as self-help; it is harder, and more interesting, as a metaphysics of a rational cosmos plus a very strict claim about what is valuable.

## Epicureans

Epicurus (341–270 BCE) founded the Garden in Athens. He inherited [Democritean atomism](02-presocratics.md) and put it to ethical work.

The world is atoms and void. The gods, if they exist, are not providential managers of human life; they are not watching, and they are not to be feared. The soul is atomic and dissolves at death, so death is "nothing to us": there is no subject left to suffer. This is meant as liberation, not gloom.

The good is **pleasure**, but Epicurus does not mean luxury or frenzy. The highest pleasure is the absence of bodily pain and mental disturbance (*ataraxia*). Desire is to be inventoried: natural and necessary desires (food, shelter, friendship) can be satisfied simply; many socially manufactured desires cannot be satisfied at all and should be dropped. Friendship is central. Justice is a useful contract not to harm or be harmed, not a Form in the sky.

Where the Stoic says "virtue is the only good," the Epicurean says "virtue is valuable because it helps produce a tranquil life." The argument is over what is for the sake of what.

## Skeptics

Greek skepticism has two main historical streams. **Pyrrhonism**, associated with Pyrrho of Elis and later written up by Sextus Empiricus, recommends *epoché*: suspension of judgment about non-evident matters. For any claim, a counter-claim can be opposed. The skeptic does not conclude that knowledge is impossible (that would be a dogma). He simply finds himself unable to decide, and *ataraxia* follows as a result, like a shadow.

The **Academic skeptics**, running Plato's Academy in a skeptical phase (Arcesilaus, Carneades), argued especially against Stoic claims to certainty. Some allowed "probable" or convincing impressions as a basis for action without knowledge.

Skepticism is easy to parody as a refusal to believe anything. In its careful form it is a discipline: distinguish what appears from what you are entitled to assert, and notice how quickly dogmatism outruns the evidence. It also forces the other schools to explain how knowledge is possible — the epistemological branch in a sharp, practical key.

These three schools, more than the Presocratics, became the popular philosophies of the Roman world. They are also the Greek side's reminder that metaphysics and logic were never only academic. They were tools for living.
