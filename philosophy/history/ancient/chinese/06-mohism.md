# Mohism

**Prerequisites:** [Foundational concepts](01-foundational-concepts.md) and [Confucius](02-confucius-and-the-analects.md)
**Key ideas:** Mozi as a rival to the Confucians; impartial care; opposition to aggressive war and to lavish ritual; Heaven's will as a standard; later Mohist work on language and inference.

Mozi (Mo Di, fifth to early fourth century BCE) founded the most organized early rival to the Confucian school. Mohists were known as a disciplined community, sometimes involved in organizing the defense of cities under siege. Their core texts, collected in the *Mozi*, argue in a more explicitly reasoned, chapter-by-chapter style than the *Analects*. If Confucius teaches mainly by pointing at an exemplary life, Mozi often teaches by stating a standard and testing policies against it.

## Impartial care

The signature doctrine is *jian ai*, usually translated "impartial care" or "universal love." Mozi diagnoses the world's disorders as coming from partiality: I care for my family, my state, my circle, and I harm others to benefit them. The remedy is to care for others' families and states as one cares for one's own.

This is an ethical first principle with political consequences. It is also a direct challenge to Confucian graded love, which starts with filial piety and extends outward. Mozi thinks that kind of starting point licenses nepotism and war. Confucians (Mencius especially) reply that impartial care is psychologically unrealistic and that it undermines the family feeling on which moral life actually grows. The argument is still live whenever someone asks whether charity begins at home, and how far "home" may be allowed to shrink the moral world.

Mozi's test of a doctrine is often consequential in spirit: does it benefit the world — increasing population, wealth, and order — or does it waste them? He is not a modern utilitarian with a felicific calculus, but he is closer to "judge by outcomes for the people" than Confucius is.

## Against aggression, excess, and fatalism

Several practical campaigns follow. **Against aggressive war:** offensive warfare is condemned as a larger version of the crimes we already know how to name (theft, murder), even when states dignify it. Defensive war is another matter; the Mohist reputation for siege defense belongs here. **Against lavish funerals and music:** Confucian ritual, Mozi argues, buries wealth and time that should serve the living. **Against fatalism:** if people believe that outcomes are already fixed, they will not exert themselves. Heaven and spirits, in the *Mozi*, are not an excuse for passivity. They are enforcers of a moral standard.

## Heaven's will and the problem of standards

How do we know what to take as a model? Mozi appeals to the will of Heaven (*Tian*), to the practices of the sage-kings, and to the evidence of benefit. Heaven itself cares impartially, so impartial care is not a human whim. This gives Mohism a theology that Confucianism often leaves quieter, and that Xunzi will later reject in this interventional form.

The **later Mohists** (chapters sometimes called the "Mohist Canons") developed some of the most technical early Chinese work on language, kinds, similarity and difference, and valid inference. They were trying to make standards of judgment public and precise — an epistemology and a logic, in the sense of the [philosophy overview](../../../README.md). That work is difficult and fragmentary, but it matters for the first-principles story: careful argument about names and reasons is not a Greek exclusive.

Mohism as an organized movement fades after the Qin and early Han, while Confucianism becomes state orthodoxy. The questions did not fade. Every time a tradition argues about impartiality versus loyalty, or about wasteful ritual versus social benefit, it is on Mohist terrain.
