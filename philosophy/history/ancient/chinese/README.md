# Ancient Chinese philosophy

**Prerequisites:** [Philosophy](../../../README.md) and [Ancient philosophy](../README.md)
**Key ideas:** political and ritual crisis as the starting problem; *Tian*, *Dao*, and *De* as shared vocabulary; the Hundred Schools as rival first principles, not one "Eastern" view.

Ancient Chinese philosophy, in this first pass, means the thought of the late Zhou period — especially the Spring and Autumn and Warring States centuries (roughly sixth to third centuries BCE) — and the early imperial synthesis that followed. It is often introduced through famous books (*Analects*, *Daodejing*, *Zhuangzi*, *Han Feizi*). Those books make more sense if you start with the situation and the shared words.

The Zhou political and ritual order was coming apart. States made war, hereditary elites failed, and the old ceremonial forms no longer guaranteed harmony. The leading question is often: **how can order be restored**, in the person, the family, and the state? That is a political question, but it immediately becomes ethical (what is a good person?), metaphysical (what is the Way of heaven and earth?), and epistemological (what standards should we use to judge claims and names?).

"Chinese philosophy" is not one answer. Confucians, Daoists, Mohists, and Legalists disagreed as sharply as Platonists, Epicureans, and Skeptics. The phrase "Hundred Schools" (*baijia*) is a later shorthand for that argument.

A note on names and texts. "Confucius" is a Latinized form of Kongzi; "Mencius" of Mengzi; "Laozi" is both a name and a title ("Old Master"). Many of these books are layered: compiled, edited, and in some cases written down over generations. Traditional attribution and modern textual scholarship often diverge. This path will flag that where it matters, and will still use the received texts as the things later Chinese thought actually read.
