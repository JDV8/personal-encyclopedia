# Shang Yang

**Prerequisites:** the survey [Legalism](../07-legalism.md); helpful: [Han Feizi](han-feizi.md)

## Name and dates

Shang Yang (d. 338 BCE), also known as Gongsun Yang, Lord Shang, was a minister in the western state of Qin whose reforms helped turn Qin into the war-capable machine that would later unify China. He was, in the traditional narrative, a foreign adviser who broke the power of hereditary nobility, standardized measures and laws, tied rank to military and agricultural merit, and was eventually killed when court politics turned — torn apart by chariots, in the grim anecdote, under laws he himself had made strict.

## Sources

The [*Book of Lord Shang*](../texts/book-of-lord-shang.md) (*Shangjunshu*) is a collection of essays in his name, some perhaps close to his milieu, some later. It is one of the most uncompromising political texts of the period: agriculture and war as the two handles of the state, a suspicion of official "talkers," and a theory that law must change with the times rather than defer to ancient kings. Historical material in *Shiji* (Sima Qian) supplies the biography and should be read as a Han narrative about Qin, not as a neutral file.

## Thought

Shang Yang's first-principles bet is that a state surrounded by enemies cannot be run on inherited custom and moral exhortation. **Law must be public, uniform, and heavy enough to be believed.** Rewards for agricultural output and for taking enemy heads re-rank the elite around functions the state needs. The old nobility's privileges are a threat, not a foundation. Culture that does not serve farm and war — including, in this rhetoric, the wandering persuaders of the Hundred Schools — is a drain.

"Changing with the times" is an explicit anti-traditionalism: what the former kings did was for their conditions; repeating them now is a mistake. This is the opposite of Confucius's self-description as a transmitter. It is also, in a harsh key, a theory of institutions as historically contingent, which is more sophisticated than the "Legalists love punishment" slogan.

The moral problem is not hidden in the text. People are to be used. The state's strength is the good. Whether that strength can be a condition for later peace, or whether it is only a recipe for permanent mobilization, is a question the Qin empire's brief life made urgent.

## Reception

[Han Feizi](han-feizi.md) treats Shang Yang as a source for *fa*. Confucian and later memory made him a symbol of cruel efficiency. Modern historians still argue about how much of Qin's rise to credit him with, and how much of the book is his. For this path, he is the practical, Qin-facing face of Legalism, as Han Feizi is the synthesizer and stylist.

## Key ideas

- Public, uniform law and rewards tied to agriculture and war remake the elite.
- Institutions should change with the times, not copy the former kings.
- The *Book of Lord Shang* is a school text as well as a name on a life.
