# Yin-Yang and the Five Phases

**Prerequisites:** [Foundational concepts](01-foundational-concepts.md)
**Key ideas:** correlative cosmology rather than a single founder-school; yin and yang as paired, complementary processes; the Five Phases as a cycle of relations; a framework later applied to bodies, seasons, colors, and states.

Yin-Yang thought and the Five Phases (*wuxing*) are often packaged as "a school" (sometimes associated with Zou Yan in the late Warring States). It is more accurate, for a first pass, to treat them as a **cosmological toolkit** that many traditions used, especially in the Qin and Han, to organize the world into intelligible patterns. The question this toolkit answers is not "what is the one *arche*?" in the Milesian sense. It is "how do processes relate, succeed one another, and resonate across scales?"

## Yin and yang

*Yin* and *yang* begin as ordinary words (shady and sunny sides of a hill) and become names for complementary aspects of process: dark/light, rest/activity, winter/summer, interior/exterior, yielding/firm. Two cautions.

First, they are not a simple morality play of bad and good, even though later popular usage sometimes slides that way. The aim is usually balance and timely interplay, not the extermination of one pole. Second, they are not two separate "stuffs" sitting in a box. They are a way of talking about how things unfold and turn into each other. Midday yang gives way to yin; midwinter yin turns toward yang. The pattern is cyclical and relational.

This is metaphysics as process: reality is grasped through phases and relations more than through static substances. Comparisons to Heraclitean flux, or to Aristotle's potentiality and actuality, can be suggestive if you do not force a translation. The Chinese model is less about saving the logic of "being" and more about mapping correspondences.

## The Five Phases

The Five Phases are Wood, Fire, Earth, Metal, and Water. "Elements" is a misleading translation if it suggests five lumps of matter. *Xing* is about going, conducting, or phases of activity. The interesting content is in the **relations**:

- A generating (or "engendering") cycle: Wood feeds Fire, Fire produces Earth (ash), Earth bears Metal, Metal collects Water (as in dew on metal, in the traditional explanations), Water nourishes Wood.
- An overcoming cycle: Water douses Fire, Fire melts Metal, Metal cuts Wood, Wood breaks Earth, Earth dams Water.

Once you have cycles like these, you can correlate phases with seasons, directions, colors, organs, tastes, and political symbols. Han thought (for example in the *Huainanzi*, and in Dong Zhongshu's Confucian synthesis) uses this apparatus to link Heaven, the body, and the empire. A dynasty may be associated with a phase; ritual color and calendar follow; anomalies in nature can be read as signs that the human order is out of phase with Heaven.

This last step is the politically charged one. Correlative cosmology can be a science of patterns, a medicine (classical Chinese medicine is unintelligible without these relations), or a language of legitimation and omen. All three uses are historical. None should be silently dropped in favor of a purely "mystical" picture, or a purely "proto-scientific" one.

## How to read this alongside the schools

Yin-Yang and *wuxing* do not replace Confucius, Laozi, or Han Feizi. They often absorb them. A Han Confucian can talk about *ren* and also about the resonance between ruler and cosmos. A medical text can talk about *qi* moving in phases without settling the Mencius–Xunzi debate. The toolkit is portable.

For this encyclopedia, the first-principles takeaway is about **what kind of explanation is being offered**. Instead of reducing the many to one material *arche*, this style explains the many by placing them in a web of timed, opposed, and generating relations. Whether that is insight or an over-tidy grid is a question you can keep asking as you meet particular applications. The grid itself is part of the deep grammar of later Chinese thought.
