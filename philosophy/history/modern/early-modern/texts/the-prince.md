## Dating and context

Written in Italian by [Machiavelli](../thinkers/niccolo-machiavelli.md) in 1513, after his dismissal and torture by the restored Medici, and circulated in manuscript; it was printed in 1532, five years after his death. Its Italian title, *De Principatibus* in his letters ("Of Principalities"), became *Il Principe*. It is dedicated to Lorenzo de' Medici, grandson of Lorenzo the Magnificent. In a famous letter of 10 December 1513 to his friend Francesco Vettori, Machiavelli describes his days in exile and his evenings, when he puts on courtly robes and converses with the ancients in his study, and he mentions this little work.

## Outline

The book is short: 26 chapters.

- **Chapters 1–11: kinds of principality.** Hereditary, mixed, new; acquired by one's own arms and *virtù* or by fortune and others' arms; by crime; civil; ecclesiastical. Cesare Borgia (ch. 7) is the model of a new prince who used fortune and others' arms well but fell when fortune turned.
- **Chapters 12–14: arms.** Mercenaries and auxiliaries are useless and dangerous; a prince must have his own army and study war.
- **Chapters 15–23: the prince's conduct.** Ch. 15's "effectual truth"; generosity and parsimony; cruelty and mercy and whether it is better to be loved or feared (ch. 17); how far princes should keep faith, with the image of the fox and the lion (ch. 18); avoiding contempt and hatred; fortresses; counsellors and flatterers.
- **Chapters 24–26: Italy and fortune.** Why Italian princes lost their states; fortune as a river and (in a notorious image) as a woman to be mastered; an "exhortation to seize Italy and free her from the barbarians".

## Key ideas

- The ruler must learn "to be able not to be good" when necessity requires.
- It is safer to be feared than loved, if one cannot be both, but never hated.
- A prince must imitate the fox, to recognize traps, and the lion, to frighten wolves.
- *Virtù* can prepare against fortune, but not eliminate it.

## Interpretation and reception

The book's apparent amoralism shocked readers and earned a place on the Church's Index of forbidden books (1559). Debates continue: was it a sincere manual, a job application to the Medici, a satire exposing tyranny (as Rousseau and Spinoza suggested), or a patriotic call for a saviour of Italy? Read it alongside the *Discourses on Livy* to see the republican side of the author. Recommended translations: Harvey Mansfield (Chicago), Quentin Skinner and Russell Price (Cambridge Texts in the History of Political Thought), or Tim Parks (Penguin), which is lively.
