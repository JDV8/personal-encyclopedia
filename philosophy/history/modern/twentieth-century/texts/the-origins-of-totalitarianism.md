# Arendt, *The Origins of Totalitarianism*

**Prerequisites:** [Hannah Arendt](../thinkers/hannah-arendt.md)

@@IMG@@

## Dating and form

[Arendt](../thinkers/hannah-arendt.md) wrote the book in New York in the late 1940s, and it was published in 1951 (in Britain under the title *The Burden of Our Time*). A second edition (1958) added the chapter "Ideology and Terror" and an epilogue on the Hungarian Revolution; later editions dropped the epilogue and added prefaces to each part. It is long (over 500 pages), dense with historical detail, and in three parts.

## Outline

- **Part One: Antisemitism.** Modern political antisemitism differs from older religious hatred. It grew as Jews, who had served European states as financiers, lost their function while retaining visible wealth; it fed on the decline of the nation-state. The Dreyfus Affair in France is a "dress rehearsal".
- **Part Two: Imperialism.** Late nineteenth-century imperialism made expansion for its own sake the aim of politics, joined "superfluous" capital and "superfluous" men, and produced race-thinking and bureaucratic rule over colonized peoples, practices that later came home to Europe. The collapse of the nation-state after 1918 created masses of stateless people who discovered that the "Rights of Man" were unenforceable without membership in a political community: what they lacked was "the right to have rights".
- **Part Three: Totalitarianism.** Nazi Germany and the Soviet Union under Stalin were a new form of government. They mobilized atomized, lonely "masses" in movements; they used propaganda and a layered "onion" structure of organization; they treated laws of Nature (race) or History (class) as the real law, and terror as its execution. The concentration and extermination camps were the laboratories where the belief that "everything is possible" was tested, making human beings superfluous.
- **"Ideology and Terror."** Ideologies claim to explain everything by the logic of one idea. Terror eliminates human spontaneity and plurality. Totalitarianism is rooted in the modern experience of loneliness. The book ends with hope: every end in history contains a new beginning, because each person born is a beginning.

## Key ideas

- Totalitarianism is a novel form of domination, not merely extreme tyranny.
- It grows from antisemitism, imperialism, the crisis of the nation-state and mass loneliness.
- Its essence is ideology plus terror, aimed at total domination.

## Reception

The book helped define the Cold War concept of totalitarianism, though Arendt's analysis is philosophical rather than a policy doctrine. Historians have criticized her treatment of the Soviet Union (less researched than Nazi Germany), the link she draws between imperialism and Nazism (though postcolonial scholars later revived it), and her account of Jewish history. It remains a classic, frequently cited in discussions of authoritarianism, propaganda and refugees.
