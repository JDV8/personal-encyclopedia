# Girard, *Violence and the Sacred*

**Prerequisites:** [René Girard](../thinkers/rene-girard.md); [Mimetic theory](../09-mimetic-theory.md)

@@IMG@@

## Dating and form

*La Violence et le sacré* was published by Grasset in Paris in 1972; the English translation by Patrick Gregory appeared in 1977 (Johns Hopkins University Press). It extends [Girard's](../thinkers/rene-girard.md) theory of mimetic desire, developed in his earlier literary criticism, into a theory of the origins of religion and culture. It reads ancient Greek tragedy (Sophocles' *Oedipus the King*, Euripides' *Bacchae*), ethnographies of ritual, and earlier theorists of religion (Freud, Lévi-Strauss). Girard deliberately left the Bible aside here, taking it up in later books.

## Main arguments

- **Sacrifice.** Sacrifice diverts violence from members of the community onto a substitute victim who cannot be avenged. Its function is to protect the community from its own violence.
- **Sacrificial crisis.** When the system of differences and rituals breaks down, violence spreads by imitation; rivals become "doubles", indistinguishable in their reciprocal violence, as in tragedy's quarrels between brothers and the plague in Thebes.
- **The surrogate victim.** The crisis is resolved when the violence of all converges on one arbitrarily chosen victim, often marked by a sign of difference (a stranger, a king, someone lame). His killing restores peace, which seems to confirm his guilt and, later, his sacred power. This is the "generative unanimity" behind myth.
- **Myth and ritual.** Myths are the persecutors' accounts of this event; rituals re-enact it in controlled form; prohibitions prevent the conflicts that led to it. Oedipus, accused of parricide and incest and expelled, is a scapegoat whose story is told from the community's point of view.
- **Dionysus and the monstrous double.** In the *Bacchae*, the god's arrival brings frenzied violence; hallucinated "monstrous doubles" express the confusion of the crisis.
- **Freud and Lévi-Strauss.** Girard rejects Freud's Oedipus complex as an account of desire, but praises *Totem and Taboo* for seeing a collective murder at the origin of culture; he criticizes structuralism for ignoring the violence that founds the differences it studies.

## Key ideas

- Religion originates in the management of violence.
- A spontaneous collective murder of a scapegoat founds culture; myths conceal it.
- Ritual sacrifice repeats the founding murder to keep peace.

## Reception

The book made Girard internationally known and shaped work in religious studies, theology, literary theory and political thought. Anthropologists criticized its universal claims and selective use of ethnography; others find its central mechanism untestable. It remains a central text for the Colloquium on Violence and Religion, founded in 1990 to explore mimetic theory.
