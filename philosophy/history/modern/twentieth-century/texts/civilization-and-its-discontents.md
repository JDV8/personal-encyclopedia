# Freud, *Civilization and Its Discontents*

**Prerequisites:** [*The Interpretation of Dreams*](the-interpretation-of-dreams.md)

@@IMG@@

*Freud was a psychologist, not a philosopher; this late essay is his most philosophical work, a reflection on happiness, aggression and society.*

## Dating and form

*Das Unbehagen in der Kultur* ("the unease in culture") was written in 1929 and published in Vienna in 1930. [Freud](../thinkers/sigmund-freud.md) was 73, ill with the jaw cancer that would kill him, and writing as fascism rose. It is a short book in eight chapters. James Strachey's translation (1961) is standard; David McLintock's (Penguin, 2002) is titled *Civilization and Its Discontents* as well.

## Outline

- **Ch. 1: The oceanic feeling.** The French writer Romain Rolland had suggested to Freud that religion's source is an "oceanic" feeling of oneness with the universe. Freud says he cannot find it in himself and traces it to the infant's early state before the ego separates from the world.
- **Ch. 2: The purpose of life.** People seek happiness, but suffering threatens from three directions: our own bodies, the external world, and our relations with other people, the last being the most painful. Methods of coping (intoxication, withdrawal, sublimation into work and art, love, religion) are all partial.
- **Ch. 3: Civilization as cause of suffering.** Civilization protects us from nature and regulates relations, but demands renunciation of instinct; many people are hostile to it.
- **Ch. 4–5: Love and aggression.** Civilization binds people through Eros, but the commandment to love one's neighbour as oneself runs against human nature. Humans have a powerful inclination to aggression; Freud quotes the Latin proverb *homo homini lupus* ("man is a wolf to man"). He doubts communism's claim that abolishing private property would end aggression.
- **Ch. 6: The death drive.** Aggression is the outward expression of a death drive (introduced in *Beyond the Pleasure Principle*, 1920) at war with Eros.
- **Ch. 7–8: Guilt.** Civilization turns aggression inward: the super-ego, internalized authority, punishes the ego through guilt, even for wishes. The sense of guilt is "the most important problem in the development of civilization", and the price of progress is a loss of happiness through heightened guilt.
- **Ending.** Humanity's fate depends on whether Eros can prevail against the drive to aggression and self-destruction. In the 1931 edition Freud added a final sentence asking who can foresee with what success and result.

## Key ideas

- Civilization is built on instinctual renunciation.
- Human aggression is a deep drive; love of neighbour is an ideal at war with nature.
- The super-ego turns aggression inward as guilt; civilization trades happiness for security.

## Reception

The book influenced critical theory (Herbert Marcuse's *Eros and Civilization*, 1955, argued that much repression is "surplus" and historically specific), cultural criticism (Philip Rieff, Norman O. Brown) and debates about human nature and violence. It echoes [Hobbes](../../early-modern/thinkers/thomas-hobbes.md) and anticipates later arguments about aggression; critics reject the death drive as speculative.
