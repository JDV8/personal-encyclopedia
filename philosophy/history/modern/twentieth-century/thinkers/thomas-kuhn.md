## Life and context

Thomas Samuel Kuhn (1922–1996) was born in Cincinnati, Ohio. He studied physics at Harvard, worked on radar countermeasures during the Second World War, and took a PhD in physics in 1949. While a graduate student he was asked by Harvard's president, James Conant, to help teach a course on science for non-scientists using historical case studies. Reading Aristotle's *Physics*, he was struck that Aristotle seemed foolish only when read through Newtonian eyes; read on its own terms, it was coherent. That experience turned him to the history and philosophy of science. He wrote *The Copernican Revolution* (1957), taught at Berkeley, Princeton and MIT, and published [*The Structure of Scientific Revolutions*](../texts/the-structure-of-scientific-revolutions.md) in 1962, as a monograph in the *International Encyclopedia of Unified Science*, a series founded by the logical positivists whose picture of science it helped overturn. He died in Cambridge, Massachusetts, in 1996.

## Core ideas

**Paradigms.** Mature sciences work within paradigms: shared exemplars (model problems and solutions), theories, instruments and values that define what counts as a legitimate problem and solution. Kuhn later distinguished the broad "disciplinary matrix" from "exemplars", partly in response to the criticism (by Margaret Masterman) that he used "paradigm" in more than twenty senses.

**Normal science.** Most science is puzzle-solving within a paradigm, extending and refining it. Failures are usually blamed on the scientist, not the paradigm.

**Anomaly, crisis, revolution.** Persistent anomalies can produce a crisis, during which alternatives emerge. A revolution replaces one paradigm with another that is not simply a cumulative addition.

**Incommensurability.** Rival paradigms may lack a common measure: key terms (like "mass" in Newton and Einstein) change meaning, problems are reprioritized, and standards differ. Scientists on opposite sides "practice their trades in different worlds".

**Theory choice.** There is no algorithm for choosing between paradigms, but shared values such as accuracy, consistency, scope, simplicity and fruitfulness guide choice, weighted differently by different scientists.

**Progress without truth?** Science progresses in problem-solving power, Kuhn argued, but not necessarily toward a fixed truth; he compared it to evolution, which moves from primitive beginnings rather than toward a goal.

## Influence and criticism

*The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* is one of the most cited academic books of the twentieth century, and "paradigm shift" entered everyday language (often loosely). It inspired the sociology of scientific knowledge and science studies. [Popper](karl-popper.md) and his followers attacked it as relativist; Kuhn repeatedly denied that he was a relativist or that science was irrational. Philosophers debated whether incommensurability is coherent (Donald Davidson argued that the idea of a radically different conceptual scheme makes no sense). Historians have found his stages too tidy for many episodes.

## Key ideas

- Science alternates between normal science within paradigms and revolutionary paradigm change.
- Paradigms define legitimate problems and solutions; anomalies can provoke crisis.
- Rival paradigms can be incommensurable; theory choice is guided by shared values, not rules.
- Kuhn reshaped how philosophers and historians understand science.
