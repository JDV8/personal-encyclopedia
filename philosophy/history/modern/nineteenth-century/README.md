# Nineteenth-century philosophy

After Kant, philosophy split into powerful and often hostile currents. The German idealists, culminating in Hegel, made history and spirit the key to reality. Schopenhauer answered that reality is blind will and life mostly suffering. In Britain, Bentham and Mill built a reforming ethics of utility and a defence of liberty; Marx turned Hegel's dialectic into a materialist theory of history and revolution. In America, Emerson and Thoreau preached self-reliance and nature. Kierkegaard, and in their novels Dostoevsky and Tolstoy, turned to the single individual facing faith, despair and death. Nietzsche announced the death of God and asked what values could survive it, reworking Schopenhauer's will to live into a will to power. The twentieth century inherited every one of these questions.

## References & further reading

- Frederick Copleston, *A History of Philosophy*, vols. 7–8 (Intermediate). Fichte to Nietzsche, and British empiricism to Mill.
- Peter Singer, *Hegel: A Very Short Introduction* (Beginner).
- Christopher Janaway, *Schopenhauer: A Very Short Introduction* (Beginner).
- Patrick Gardiner, *Kierkegaard: A Very Short Introduction* (Beginner).
- Michael Tanner, *Nietzsche: A Very Short Introduction* (Beginner).
- Walter Kaufmann, *Nietzsche: Philosopher, Psychologist, Antichrist* (Intermediate). The classic postwar reading of the will to power as a psychology of self-overcoming.
- Gilles Deleuze, *Nietzsche and Philosophy* (Advanced). Will to power as differential force, not a will that wants power as an object.
- Martin Heidegger, *Nietzsche* (Advanced). The major metaphysical reading, based in part on the posthumous compilation.
- Karl Löwith, *From Hegel to Nietzsche* (Advanced). The classic study of how the century broke with Hegel.
