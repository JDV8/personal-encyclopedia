# Welfare economics

Welfare economics asks how to evaluate economic outcomes. This section defines Pareto efficiency, extends the analysis from single markets to general equilibrium, states the two fundamental welfare theorems and their limits, and confronts the tension between efficiency and equity. It then examines whether individual preferences can be combined into social choices (Arrow's theorem) and how cost-benefit analysis is used in practice.

**Prerequisites:** [Consumer theory](../03-consumer-theory/README.md) and [Market failure](../07-market-failure/README.md).

## References & further reading

- Hal R. Varian, *Intermediate Microeconomics: A Modern Approach* (Intermediate). The standard intermediate text, with careful treatment of consumer and producer theory.
- Amartya Sen, *Collective Choice and Social Welfare* (Advanced). Social choice and welfare from a Nobel laureate.
- Kenneth J. Arrow, *Social Choice and Individual Values* (Advanced). The 1951 book containing the impossibility theorem.
- John Rawls, *A Theory of Justice* (Intermediate). The veil of ignorance and the difference principle.
- Arthur M. Okun, *Equality and Efficiency: The Big Tradeoff* (Beginner). The leaky bucket and the efficiency–equity trade-off.
- Anthony E. Boardman, David H. Greenberg, Aidan R. Vining and David L. Weimer, *Cost-Benefit Analysis: Concepts and Practice* (Intermediate). The standard practical text.
- Andreu Mas-Colell, Michael D. Whinston and Jerry R. Green, *Microeconomic Theory* (Advanced). The graduate-level reference for consumer, producer, general equilibrium and information theory.
