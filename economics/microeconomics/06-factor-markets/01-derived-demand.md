So far the course has studied markets for goods and services. But firms also buy: they hire labour, rent or buy capital, and lease land. These are **factor markets**, the markets for the [[scarcity|factors of production]]. Factor markets determine wages, interest and rents, and therefore how national income is divided among workers, savers and landowners. This section applies supply and demand to them.

## Demand for a factor is derived

People want bread for its own sake. Nobody wants bakers for their own sake: a bakery hires bakers because they help produce bread that customers will buy. The demand for a factor of production is a **derived demand**: it is derived from the demand for the goods and services the factor helps produce.

This has an immediate consequence. Anything that changes the demand for a product changes the demand for the factors that make it. When demand for electric vehicles rises, demand for battery engineers, lithium and specialised factories rises too. When demand for coal falls, demand for coal miners falls. Alfred Marshall analysed derived demand in *Principles of Economics* (1890).

## The circular flow

Factor markets and product markets form a loop:

```mermaid
flowchart LR
  H["Households: own labour, capital, land"] -->|"Supply factors"| FM["Factor markets"]
  FM -->|"Wages, interest, rent"| H
  FM -->|"Factors hired"| F["Firms"]
  F -->|"Goods and services"| PM["Product markets"]
  PM -->|"Spending"| F
  H -->|"Buy goods"| PM
```

Households sell factor services to firms and receive income; they spend that income on goods and services produced by firms. Prices in both kinds of market coordinate the flow.

## What determines how much of a factor a firm wants?

A profit-maximising firm hires one more unit of a factor if it adds more to revenue than to cost. The extra revenue from one more unit of a factor is its **marginal revenue product** (MRP):

**MRP = marginal product × marginal revenue**

For a firm selling in a competitive product market, marginal revenue equals price, so MRP is also called the **value of the marginal product** (VMP = MP × P).

The firm hires up to the point where the MRP of the factor equals its price (the wage for labour, the rental rate for capital). This rule is the factor-market version of MR = MC, and the next lesson develops it for labour.

## Why derived demand slopes down

Because of [[diminishing-marginal-returns|diminishing marginal returns]], each additional worker or machine adds less output than the one before, so its MRP falls. A lower factor price makes it worth hiring more, down the falling MRP curve. So factor demand curves slope downward.

## What makes factor demand elastic

Marshall identified conditions under which the demand for a factor is more elastic (more responsive to its price). Later refined by John Hicks, they are known as the **Hicks-Marshall laws of derived demand**. Demand for a factor is more elastic when:

1. **The demand for the final product is more elastic.** If customers drop the product when its price rises, a rise in wages that pushes up the price will cut employment sharply.
2. **Other factors can easily substitute for it.** If machines can replace workers, a wage rise leads firms to automate.
3. **The supply of other factors is elastic**, so substitution does not drive up their prices much.
4. **The factor's cost is a large share of total cost.** A 10 percent rise in the wage of a factor that accounts for 2 percent of cost barely affects the product price; one that accounts for 60 percent affects it a lot. (Hicks noted this rule holds only under a further condition about substitution, but it is a good guide.)

These laws help explain why some groups of workers have more bargaining power than others. A small group of highly specialised workers whose wages are a tiny share of total costs, and for whom there are no substitutes (say, air traffic controllers or certain elevator technicians), can win large wage increases without much loss of employment.

## Factor prices and income distribution

Because factor prices determine incomes, factor markets are where microeconomics meets the question of distribution. Classical economists such as Ricardo saw the division of output among wages, profits and rent as the central problem of economics. The neoclassical answer, developed by John Bates Clark and others around 1900 as **marginal productivity theory**, is that in competitive markets each factor is paid its marginal revenue product. Whether that payment is "fair" is a separate, normative question; Clark thought it was, while critics have challenged both the theory's assumptions and its ethics. This section covers both the theory and the debates, ending with [[income-inequality|income inequality]].

## Key ideas

- Factor markets are markets for labour, capital and land.
- Demand for a factor is derived from demand for the products it helps make.
- Firms hire a factor until its marginal revenue product equals its price.
- Factor demand slopes down because of diminishing marginal returns.
- The Hicks-Marshall laws explain when factor demand is elastic.
