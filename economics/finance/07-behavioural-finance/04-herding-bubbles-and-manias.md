# Herding, bubbles and manias

Every few decades prices rise far above anything that can be justified by the cash flows they represent, then collapse. The tulip mania of the 1630s, the South Sea bubble of 1720, the 1920s boom, the dot-com bubble of the late 1990s and others are the most famous examples. Understanding why they form is the most valuable lesson behavioural finance offers a long-term investor.

## What is a bubble?

A **bubble** is a rapid, sustained rise in an asset's price beyond what its fundamental value can justify, driven by expectations of further price increases rather than by cash flows, and typically ending in a sharp fall. Definitions are contested: a bubble can only be proven after the fact, and economists like Eugene Fama question whether the concept is useful, since if bubbles could be identified in advance one could profit from it. Robert Shiller, in *Irrational Exuberance* (2000), defined a bubble as a situation in which news of price increases spurs investor enthusiasm that spreads by psychological contagion and amplifies stories that justify the increases.

## Herding

**Herding** is behaving like others: buying because everyone else is. It arises for several reasons:

- **Information cascades:** people reasonably infer that others know something and follow. Once enough have acted, later people ignore their own information. Cascades can be fragile and wrong.
- **Social proof and conformity:** Solomon Asch's 1951 experiments showed many people conform to a group's clearly wrong answer.
- **Career and reputation concerns:** as Keynes said, "it is better for reputation to fail conventionally than to succeed unconventionally." Fund managers who lag a booming benchmark risk losing clients, so they join in.
- **Fear of missing out (FOMO)** and envy of neighbours' gains.
- **Positive feedback:** rising prices attract buyers and news coverage, which push prices higher.

## The anatomy of a boom and bust

Economist Charles Kindleberger's *Manias, Panics, and Crashes* drew on Hyman Minsky's model of financial instability to describe stages:

1. **Displacement:** a new event changes expectations: a technology (railways, the internet), a policy, a discovery, or falling interest rates.
2. **Boom:** prices begin to rise; credit expands; early profits attract attention.
3. **Euphoria:** prices rise faster; "this time is different"; newcomers with little experience join; leverage rises. Stories justify valuations.
4. **Profit-taking:** insiders sell; some warning signs appear.
5. **Panic:** a trigger causes a rush to sell; leveraged holders face margin calls and sell, driving prices down further.
6. **Aftermath:** losses, bankruptcies, regulation and often a recession.

**Minsky's hypothesis** is that stability breeds instability: in calm times, borrowers and lenders take more risk, moving from "hedge" finance (income covers debt service) through "speculative" (income covers interest only) to "Ponzi" finance (debt can only be repaid by selling assets or borrowing more at higher prices), until a "Minsky moment" arrives when the process reverses.

## Greater fool theory and rational bubbles

Buyers in a bubble need not be fools: they may know the price is too high but believe they can sell to a **greater fool** later. This is rational for each individual so long as the crowd continues. Economic models of "rational bubbles" show a bubble can persist if each buyer expects the price to continue rising fast enough to compensate. Timing is the problem: an investor who sells too early forgoes gains, and one who sells too late loses.

## A numerical lens

Valuation provides a reality check. Suppose a stock pays no dividend but is expected eventually to earn 5 per share. At a price of 100, the P/E on those eventual earnings is 20. If the price rises to 400 with no change in earnings expectations, the P/E is 80; to justify that price, the company would need to grow earnings fourfold, and then still face normal valuation. See [price-to-earnings ratio](../03-securities/01-stocks/06-the-price-to-earnings-ratio.md) and [DCF valuation](../03-securities/01-stocks/09-discounted-cash-flow-valuation.md). A fall of 75% from 400 back to 100 is required just to return to the original value, which is a typical bubble round trip: after peaking in March 2000, the Nasdaq Composite fell by about 78% by October 2002.

## Is it possible to profit?

Almost no one reliably predicts the top. Isaac Newton, the story goes, invested in the South Sea Company, sold at a profit, then bought back near the top and lost heavily; the details of the story vary by account. The lesson commonly drawn is that even great intelligence does not immunise against herd behaviour.

## Defences

1. **Valuation discipline:** know what you are paying for and what has to go right.
2. **Diversify** and keep the asset allocation within limits; rebalancing mechanically sells assets that boomed.
3. **Beware the "story + FOMO + leverage" combination.**
4. **Avoid borrowing to invest:** leverage turns a drawdown into forced selling.
5. **Remember your "sleep-at-night" level:** use only money you can lose.
6. **Be sceptical of financial advice that cites only recent returns.**
7. **Keep a record of what you believed** in earlier booms.

## Key ideas

- A bubble is a price rise driven by expectations of further rises; herding, social proof, career risk and feedback sustain it.
- Kindleberger and Minsky describe stages from displacement to panic, with rising leverage along the way.
- Individually rational "greater fool" behaviour can sustain a bubble; timing the top is nearly impossible.
- Defences: valuation discipline, diversification, rebalancing, no leverage and humility.
