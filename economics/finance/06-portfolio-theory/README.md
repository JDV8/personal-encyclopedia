# Portfolio theory: risk, return and the market

This section moves from single securities to whole portfolios. It derives portfolio return and risk, the efficient frontier and the idea of a single best risky portfolio, then CAPM and beta, which tell us which risk earns a reward. It continues with the efficient market hypothesis and the evidence on active versus passive investing, practical asset allocation and rebalancing, factor models that extend CAPM, and the tools for measuring performance fairly. The formulas are developed with numeric examples you can reproduce in a spreadsheet.

**Prerequisites:** [Foundations](../01-foundations/README.md), especially variance, correlation and diversification; [Securities](../03-securities/README.md) for the assets.

## Suggested reading order

Read 01 to 03 in order: they form the core theory. Lessons 04 and 05 are about evidence and can be read in either order. Lesson 06 is the most practical, 07 builds on CAPM, and 08 is the toolkit for judging results, including your own.

## References & further reading

- Harry Markowitz, "Portfolio Selection", *Journal of Finance* (1952) (Advanced). The paper that began modern portfolio theory.
- William F. Sharpe, "Capital Asset Prices: A Theory of Market Equilibrium under Conditions of Risk", *Journal of Finance* (1964) (Advanced). The original CAPM paper.
- Eugene F. Fama, "Efficient Capital Markets: A Review of Theory and Empirical Work", *Journal of Finance* (1970) (Advanced). The classic statement of the efficient market hypothesis.
- Eugene F. Fama and Kenneth R. French, "The Cross-Section of Expected Stock Returns", *Journal of Finance* (1992) (Advanced). Size and value versus beta.
- Zvi Bodie, Alex Kane and Alan J. Marcus, *Investments* (Intermediate). The standard textbook for all topics in this section.
- Edwin J. Elton, Martin J. Gruber, Stephen J. Brown and William N. Goetzmann, *Modern Portfolio Theory and Investment Analysis* (Advanced). A rigorous reference on portfolio theory.
- Burton G. Malkiel, *A Random Walk Down Wall Street* (Beginner). A readable introduction to efficient markets, portfolio theory and the case for diversification.
- William J. Bernstein, *The Intelligent Asset Allocator* (Intermediate). Practical guidance on asset allocation with the underlying theory.
- Charles D. Ellis, *Winning the Loser's Game* (Beginner). A short argument for low-cost, disciplined investing.
- Larry E. Swedroe and Andrew L. Berkin, *The Incredible Shrinking Alpha* (Beginner to Intermediate). On the evidence about skill, factors and active management.
- Andrew W. Lo, *Adaptive Markets* (Intermediate). An evolutionary reconciliation of efficient markets and behavioural finance.
