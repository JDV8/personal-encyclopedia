For most households, the largest asset is a home. Real estate is also a major institutional asset class. This lesson looks at property as an investment, how its value is judged, and how investors can own it through the stock market using **real estate investment trusts** (REITs).

## Property as an investment

A property can produce two returns: **income** (rent, less operating costs) and **capital appreciation** (a rising price). Unlike a share, a building is large, indivisible, costly to trade (legal fees, land transfer taxes, commissions), unique, and typically bought with a mortgage. It is **illiquid**: selling can take months. It must be maintained, insured and managed.

**Net operating income (NOI)** is rental income minus operating expenses (property tax, insurance, maintenance, management), before mortgage payments and taxes. The **capitalisation rate** is NOI divided by value, a quick measure of yield; it works like a reverse [perpetuity](../../01-foundations/06-annuities-and-perpetuities.md): value = NOI ÷ cap rate.

Example: a building is bought for 10,000,000 with NOI of 600,000: cap rate 6%. If market cap rates rise to 7% (say because interest rates rose), the same NOI implies a value of 600,000 ÷ 0.07 = 8,571,429, a 14.3% fall. If 5,000,000 of the purchase was financed by debt, the owner's equity falls from 5,000,000 to 3,571,429: a 28.6% loss. This is **leverage** at work, magnifying losses just as it magnifies gains.

Features that distinguish property:

- **Local:** values depend on a city, a neighbourhood, a street.
- **Interest-rate sensitive:** higher rates raise mortgage costs and cap rates.
- **Partial inflation hedge:** rents can rise with prices over time, though not reliably or quickly.
- **Appraisal-based pricing:** values are estimated, so measured volatility of private real estate looks lower than it really is, because prices are "smoothed".

## REITs

A **real estate investment trust** is a company or trust that owns, and usually operates, income-producing real estate, and whose units or shares trade on a stock exchange. REITs let investors hold property with small amounts of money and daily liquidity, diversified over many buildings.

The US created REITs in 1960, with legislation intended to give small investors access to large-scale real estate. Canada's first REIT of this modern kind, RioCan, listed on the Toronto Stock Exchange in 1993, and Canada's REIT sector is among the largest in the world per capita.

Key features:

- **Pass-through taxation:** a REIT generally pays little or no corporate tax if it distributes most of its taxable income to unitholders, who pay tax on the distributions. In the US, qualifying REITs must pay out at least 90% of taxable income as dividends. In Canada, qualifying trusts and the "REIT exception" rules apply. This produces high **distribution yields**, but the distributions can include return of capital and are taxed in complex ways: the tax breakdown matters in non-registered accounts.
- **Types:** equity REITs (own property: apartments, offices, retail, industrial, health care, data centres), mortgage REITs (own mortgages and mortgage securities, more sensitive to rates), and hybrids.
- **Valuation:** analysts use **funds from operations** (FFO) and **adjusted FFO** (AFFO) instead of net income, since accounting depreciation overstates the economic decline of buildings, together with **net asset value** (NAV) per unit. A REIT can trade at a premium or discount to its NAV.

## How REITs behave

Because they trade on exchanges, REITs price daily and react to interest rates and equity market sentiment: during stress, such as 2008 and March 2020, listed REITs fell far more sharply than appraisal-based property values, which update slowly. Over the long term they have behaved partly like stocks (shared market risk) and partly like real estate (rent income, tied to local property markets). Their correlation with a broad equity index is moderate to high; as a diversifier they are imperfect. See [correlation and diversification](../../01-foundations/11-correlation-and-diversification.md).

## Comparing ways to own property

| Route | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|
| Own directly | Control, leverage, possible tax advantages, a use value if you live in it | Concentrated, illiquid, time-consuming, costly to trade |
| REIT or REIT ETF | Liquid, diversified, low minimums | Market-like price swings, tax complexity, fees |
| Private real estate funds | Direct property exposure | Illiquid, high fees, possible redemption limits |
| Mortgage investments | Contracted interest income | Credit risk, illiquidity |

Home ownership is partly consumption and partly investment: you also "earn" the rent you do not pay. Treating a primary residence as a diversified investment is risky if it makes up most of one's wealth; see [asset allocation](../../06-portfolio-theory/06-asset-allocation-and-rebalancing.md).

## Key ideas

- Real estate returns come from income (NOI) and price changes; it is local, illiquid and usually leveraged.
- Cap rate = NOI ÷ value; a rise in cap rates cuts value, and leverage magnifies the equity loss (14.3% on assets became 28.6% on equity in our example).
- REITs offer liquid, diversified exposure with pass-through tax treatment and high distributions.
- Listed REITs behave partly like stocks, so they diversify less than appraisal-based figures suggest.
