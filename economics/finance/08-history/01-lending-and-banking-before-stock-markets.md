# Lending and banking before stock markets

**Prerequisites:** [Money and value](../01-foundations/01-money-and-value.md), [Credit and banking](../01-foundations/02-credit-and-banking.md).

Finance is much older than stock exchanges. Lending, interest, deposit-taking and even government bonds were developed in the ancient and medieval worlds, and the institutions that later appeared, such as stock markets, grew from them.

## Ancient credit

The earliest recorded loans come from **Mesopotamia**, where temples and palaces stored grain and silver and lent them to farmers and merchants, recording the debt on clay tablets. The Code of Hammurabi (about 1750 BCE) regulated loans and set limits on interest, with rates of roughly one-fifth for silver and one-third for grain according to common scholarly readings. Interest on grain loans reflected the natural growth of seed and herds, an early intuition of the [time value of money](../01-foundations/04-time-value-of-money.md).

In ancient Greece, *trapezitai* (table-men) changed money, took deposits and made loans, and **maritime loans** financed voyages: the lender was repaid only if the ship arrived, so the interest rate included an insurance premium. Rome developed contract law, banking, and the **publicani**, private companies that collected taxes and supplied the army, with shares (*partes*) that some historians compare to early corporate ownership, though the comparison is debated. See [what is a company](../02-firms-and-capital/01-what-is-a-company.md).

## The medieval problem of interest

Christian canon law condemned **usury**, usually meaning any interest on a loan, and Islamic and Jewish traditions have their own rules on lending, though with different details. Lenders and borrowers found ways around the ban, ones that treated returns as something other than interest: sharing profits in partnerships (the *commenda*), fees for late repayment, and exchange-rate differences on **bills of exchange**.

Illustrative example (hypothetical numbers): a Florentine merchant needs 100 florins now and will repay in Bruges in three months. A banker gives him 100 florins in Florence and takes a bill requiring 110 florins' worth of Flemish money in Bruges. The 10% is presented as the exchange rate, not interest. The bill also let merchants move value across Europe without carrying coin, a rapid improvement for trade. See [credit and banking](../01-foundations/02-credit-and-banking.md).

## Italian banking and bookkeeping

From the 12th to the 15th centuries, northern Italian cities became financial centres. **Venice** issued forced loans to its citizens in the 1100s, which were transferable and paid interest, among the earliest government bonds; the **Casa di San Giorgio** in Genoa (1407) consolidated the city's debt and managed taxes. Family banks such as the **Medici** of Florence (founded 1397) operated branches across Europe, accepting deposits and trading bills of exchange. Merchants refined **double-entry bookkeeping**, which Luca Pacioli described in his 1494 *Summa de arithmetica*, a method which tracks both assets and the claims on them and which is still the basis of the accounts in [the balance sheet](../02-firms-and-capital/04-the-balance-sheet.md).

## Banking north of the Alps

In the 15th and 16th centuries, the **Fugger** family of Augsburg grew rich on mining and lending to the Habsburgs, and became an emblem of the financial power of banking dynasties. Like many lenders to monarchs, they also faced risk: kings defaulted. The Spanish crown went bankrupt many times in the 16th century, taking its creditors down. The history of **sovereign default** is long. See [government bonds](../03-securities/02-bonds/07-government-bonds.md).

## Public banks and the birth of central banking

- **Bank of Amsterdam (Amsterdamsche Wisselbank, 1609):** accepted deposits of coins of many kinds and created stable bank money for merchants, helping make Amsterdam the centre of European finance.
- **Sveriges Riksbank (1668)** in Sweden, regarded as the world's oldest central bank, and the **Bank of England (1694)**, founded to lend to the government, which became a model for other central banks; see [central banks](../05-regulation/06-central-banks-and-monetary-policy.md).

## Lessons

1. **Credit and trust come first;** exchanges came later, built on lending, contracts and credible records.
2. **Interest is the price of time and risk;** regulations on usury show an enduring tension between lending and fairness; see [simple and compound interest](../01-foundations/03-simple-and-compound-interest.md).
3. **Innovation tends to route around rules,** as the bill of exchange did.
4. **Lending to governments is risky,** and the issues of default and repayment shaped later bond markets.
5. **Institutions matter:** banks, ledgers and courts that enforced contracts allowed trade to expand.

## A note on ethics

Medieval debates about usury were also debates about justice, fairness and the common good. See [philosophy: ethics](../../../philosophy/concepts/ethics/README.md) for the broader traditions; Aristotle's view that money should not breed money influenced scholastic arguments, though economic thought later accepted interest as a payment for the time value of money and risk.

## Key ideas

- Lending, interest and credit instruments predate stock markets by thousands of years.
- Medieval bans on usury drove financial innovation, notably the bill of exchange.
- Italian city-states created government debt, double-entry bookkeeping and international banking.
- Public banks and the first central banks grew from merchants' and governments' need for trustworthy money.
